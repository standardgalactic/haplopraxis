   The hyphen-minus symbol - is the form of [86]hyphen most commonly used
   in digital [87]documents. On most keyboards, it is the only character
   that resembles a [88]minus sign or a [89]dash so it is also used for
   these.^[90][1] The name hyphen-minus derives from the original
   [91]ASCII standard,^[92][2] where it was called hyphen (minus).^[93][3]
   The character is referred to as a hyphen, a minus sign, or a dash
   according to the context where it is being used.

Description

   [[94]edit]
   -+−–
   -+−–
   hyphen-minus, plus, minus, and en-dash characters
   in proportional and monospaced fonts

   In early [95]typewriters and [96]character encodings, a single key/code
   was almost always used for hyphen, minus, various dashes, and
   strikethrough, since they all have a roughly similar appearance. The
   current [97]Unicode Standard specifies distinct characters for several
   different [98]dashes, an unambiguous [99]minus sign (sometimes called
   the Unicode minus) at [100]code point U+2212, an unambiguous
   [101]hyphen (sometimes called the Unicode hyphen) at U+2010, the
   hyphen-minus at U+002D and a [102]variety of other hyphen symbols for
   various uses. When a hyphen is called for, the hyphen-minus is a common
   choice as it is well known, easy to enter on [103]keyboards, and still
   the only form recognized by many data formats and computer languages.
   Though the Unicode Standard states that the U+2010 hyphen is
   "preferred" over the hyphen-minus,^[104][4] the standard itself uses
   the hyphen-minus as its hyphen character.^[105][5]

   In most modern [106]computer fonts, the hyphen-minus is either
   identical or very similar to the Unicode hyphen.^[107][6]^[108][a]

   In mathematical texts that include the plus sign, the Unicode minus is
   preferred to the hyphen-minus, because its metrics match the plus sign
   in level and length.^[109][b]

Uses

   [[110]edit]

Typing

   [[111]edit]

   See also: [112]Two consecutive hyphens and [113]Three consecutive
   hyphens

   This character is typed when a hyphen or a minus sign is wanted. Based
   on old typewriter conventions, it is common to use a pair
   -- to represent an [114]em dash —,^[115][7] and to put spaces around it
    -  to represent a [116]spaced en dash  – ; this practice is deprecated
   in professional [117]typography.^[118][8] Some [119]word processors
   [120]automatically convert these to the correct dash. The character can
   also be typed multiple times to simulate a horizontal line (though in
   most cases, repeated entry of the [121]underscore will produce a solid
   line). Alternating the hyphen-minus with spaces produces a "dashed"
   line, often to indicate where paper is to be cut. On a typewriter,
   over-striking a section of text with this is used for
   [122]strikethrough.

Programming languages

   [[123]edit]

   Some [124]programming languages use the hyphen-minus for denoting
   subtraction and [125]additive inverse, often called negation^[126][9]
   in this context.^[127][10]^[128][11] It is rarely used to indicate a
   range, due to ambiguity with subtraction. Generally, other characters,
   such as the Unicode U+2212
   − [129]MINUS SIGN are not recognized as an
   [130]operator.^[[131]citation needed]

   In some programming languages (for example [132]MySQL) -- (two
   hyphen-minus) mark the beginning of a [133]comment. It can be used to
   start the [134]signature block in [135]Usenet news system. [136]YAML
   uses --- (three hyphen-minuses) to end a section.

Command line

   [[137]edit]

   The hyphen-minus character is often used when specifying
   [138]command-line options, a convention popularized by [139]Unix.
   Examples of the "short" form are -R or -q. A user can specify both by
   using -Rq. Some implementations allow two hyphen-minuses to specify
   "long" option names as --recursive or --quiet. These are easier to
   understand when reading commands (some software does not care about the
   number of hyphen-minuses, and either does not allow combinations of
   single-letter options, or requires the user to rearrange them, so they
   do not match a long option). A double hyphen-minus by itself (followed
   by a space) indicates that there are no more options, which is useful
   when one needs to specify a filename that starts with a hyphen-minus.
   An option of just a hyphen-minus (followed by a space) may be
   recognized [140]in lieu of a filename and indicates that [141]stdin is
   to be read.

diff output

   [[142]edit]

   - is used to denote deleted lines in [143]diff output in the
   [144]context format or the [145]unified format.

Encoding

   [[146]edit]

   The glyph has a [147]code point in [148]Unicode as U+002D -
   HYPHEN-MINUS
   . It is also in [149]ASCII with the same value.

   [[156]edit]

       choices.
