   The plus sign (+) and the minus sign (−) are [102]mathematical symbols
   used to denote [103]positive and [104]negative functions, respectively.
   In addition, + represents the operation of [105]addition, which results
   in a [106]sum, while − represents [107]subtraction, resulting in a
   [108]difference.^[109][1] Their use has been extended to many other
   meanings, more or less analogous. Plus and minus are [110]Latin terms
   meaning "more" and "less", respectively.

   The forms ⟨+⟩ and ⟨−⟩ are used in many countries around the world.
   Other designs include ⟨[111]﬩⟩ for plus and ⟨[112]⁒⟩ for minus.

History

   [[113]edit]

   Though the signs now seem as familiar as the [114]alphabet or the
   [115]Hindu–Arabic numerals, they are not of great antiquity. The
   [116]Egyptian hieroglyphic sign for addition, for example, resembled a
   pair of legs walking in the direction in which the text was written
   ([117]Egyptian could be written either from right to left or left to
   right), with the reverse sign indicating subtraction:^[118][2]

   D54

                                     or

   D55

   [119]Nicole Oresme's [120]manuscripts from the 14th century show what
   may be one of the earliest uses of + as a sign for plus.^[121][3]

   In early 15th century Europe, the letters "P" and "M" were generally
   used.^[122][4]^[123][5] The symbols (P with overline, p̄, for più
   (more), i.e., plus, and M with overline, m̄, for meno (less), i.e.,
   minus) appeared for the first time in [124]Luca Pacioli's mathematics
   [125]compendium, [126]Summa de arithmetica, geometria, proportioni et
   proportionalità, first printed and published in [127]Venice in
   1494.^[128][6]

   The + sign is a simplification of the [129]Latin: et (comparable to the
   evolution of the [130]ampersand &).^[131][7] The − may be derived from
   a [132]macron ◌̄ written over ⟨m⟩ when used to indicate subtraction; or
   it may come from a shorthand version of the letter ⟨m⟩ itself.^[133][8]
   [134]A page from Johannes Widmann's book From [135]Johannes Widmann's
   book on "handy and pretty [136]arithmetic for all
   [137]merchants"^[138][9]^[139][10]

   In his 1489 treatise, [140]Johannes Widmann referred to the symbols −
   and + as minus and mer (Modern German mehr; "more"): "[...] was − ist
   das ist minus [...] und das + das ist mer das zu
   addirst".^[141][9]^[142][10]^[143][11] They were not used for addition
   and subtraction in the treatise, but were used to indicate surplus and
   deficit; usage in the modern sense is attested in a 1518 book by
   [144]Henricus Grammateus.^[145][12]^[146][13]

   [147]Robert Recorde, the designer of the [148]equals sign, introduced
   plus and minus to Britain in 1557 in [149]The Whetstone of
   Witte:^[150][14] "There be other 2 signes in often use of which the
   first is made thus + and betokeneth more: the other is thus made − and
   betokeneth lesse."

Plus sign

   [[151]edit]

   "+" redirects here. For other uses, see [152]+ (disambiguation).

   The plus sign (+) is a [153]binary operator that indicates
   [154]addition, as in 2 + 3 = 5. It can also serve as a [155]unary
   operator that leaves its [156]operand [157]unchanged (+x means the same
   as x). This notation may be used when it is desired to emphasize the
   positiveness of a number, especially in contrast with the [158]negative
   numbers (+5 versus −5).

   The plus sign can also indicate many other operations, depending on the
   mathematical system under consideration. Many [159]algebraic
   structures, such as [160]vector spaces and [161]matrix rings, have some
   operation which is called, or is equivalent to, addition. It is though
   conventional to use the plus sign to only denote [162]commutative
   operations.^[163][15]

   The symbol is also used in [164]chemistry and [165]physics. For more,
   see [166]§ Other uses.

Minus sign

   [[167]edit]

   "Minus" redirects here. For other uses, see [168]Minus
   (disambiguation).

   The minus sign (−) has three main uses in mathematics:^[169][16]
    1. The [170]subtraction operator: a [171]binary operator to indicate
       the operation of subtraction, as in 5 − 3 = 2. Subtraction is the
       inverse of addition.^[172][1]
    2. The [173]function whose [174]value for any [175]real or
       [176]complex [177]argument is the [178]additive inverse of that
       argument. For example, if x = 3, then −x = −3, but if x = −3, then
       −x = +3. Similarly, −(−x) = x.
    3. A [179]prefix of a numeric constant. When it is placed immediately
       before an unsigned number, the combination names a negative number,
       the additive inverse of the positive number that the numeral would
       otherwise name. In this usage, '−5' names a number the same way
       'semicircle' names a geometric figure, with the caveat that 'semi'
       does not have a separate use as a function name.

   In many contexts, it does not matter whether the second or the third of
   these usages is intended: −5 is the same number. When it is important
   to distinguish them, a raised minus sign (¯) is sometimes used for
   negative constants, as in [180]elementary education, the programming
   language [181]APL, and some early graphing calculators.^[182][a]

   All three uses can be referred to as "minus" in everyday speech, though
   the binary operator is sometimes read as "take away".^[183][17] In
   American English nowadays, −5 (for example) is generally referred to as
   "negative five" though speakers born before 1950 often refer to it as
   "minus five". (Temperatures tend to follow the older usage; −5° is
   generally called "minus five degrees".)^[184][18] Further, a few
   textbooks in the United States encourage −x to be read as "the opposite
   of x" or "the additive inverse of x"—to avoid giving the impression
   that −x is necessarily negative (since x itself may already be
   negative).^[185][19]

   In mathematics and most programming languages, the rules for the
   [186]order of operations mean that −5^2 is equal to −25:
   [187]Exponentiation binds more strongly than the unary minus, which
   binds more strongly than multiplication or division. However, in some
   programming languages ([188]Microsoft Excel in particular), unary
   operators bind strongest, so in those cases −5^2 is 25, but 0−5^2 is
   −25.^[189][20]

   Similar to the plus sign, the minus sign is also used in [190]chemistry
   and [191]physics. (For more, see [192]§ Other uses below.)

Use in elementary education

   [[193]edit]

   Some elementary teachers use raised minus signs before numbers to
   disambiguate them from the operation of subtraction.^[194][21] The same
   convention is also used in some computer languages. For example,
   subtracting −5 from 3 might be read as "positive three take away
   negative 5", and be shown as

          3 − ^−5 becomes 3 + 5 = 8,

   which can be read as:

          ^+3 −1(^−5)

   or even as

          ^+3 − ^−5 becomes ^+3 + ^+5 = ^+8.

Use as a qualifier

   [[195]edit]

   When placed after a number, a plus sign can indicate an open range of
   numbers. For example, "18+" is commonly used as shorthand for "ages 18
   and up" although "eighteen plus", for example, is now common usage.

   In US [196]grading systems, the plus sign indicates a grade one level
   higher and the minus sign a grade lower. For example, B− ("B minus") is
   one grade lower than B. In some occasions, this is extended to two plus
   or minus signs (e.g., A++ being two grades higher than
   A).^[[197]citation needed]

   A common trend in branding, particularly with streaming video services,
   has been the use of the plus sign at the end of brand names, e.g.
   [198]Google+, [199]Disney+, [200]Paramount+, and [201]Apple TV+. Since
   the word "plus" can mean an advantage, or an additional amount of
   something, such "+" signs imply that a product offers extra features or
   benefits.

   Positive and negative are sometimes abbreviated as +ve and
   −ve,^[202][22] and on batteries and cell terminals are often marked
   with + and −.

Blood

   [[205]edit]

   [206]Blood types are often qualified with a plus or minus to indicate
   the presence or absence of the [207]Rh factor. For example, A+ means
   [208]type A blood with the Rh factor present, while B− means type B
   blood with the Rh factor absent.

Music

   [[209]edit]

   In music, [210]augmented chords are symbolized with a plus sign,
   although this practice is not universal (as there are other methods for
   spelling those chords). For example, "C+" is read "C augmented chord".
   Sometimes the plus is written as a [211]superscript.

Uses in computing

   [[212]edit]

   As well as the normal mathematical usage, plus and minus signs may be
   used for a number of other purposes in computing.

   Plus and minus signs are often used in [213]tree view on a computer
   screen—to show if a folder is collapsed or not.

   In some programming languages, [214]concatenation of [215]strings is
   written "a" + "b", and results in "ab".

   In most programming languages, subtraction and negation are indicated
   with the ASCII [216]hyphen-minus character, -. In [217]APL a raised
   minus sign (here written using U+00AF
   ¯ MACRON) is used to denote a negative number, as in ¯3. While in
   [218]J a negative number is denoted by an [219]underscore, as in _5.

   In [220]C and some other computer programming languages, two plus signs
   indicate the [221]increment operator and two minus signs a decrement;
   the position of the operator before or after the variable indicates
   whether the new or old value is read from it. For example, if x equals
   6, then y = x++ increments x to 7 but sets y to 6, whereas y = ++x
   would set both x and y to 7. By extension, ++ is sometimes used in
   computing terminology to signify an improvement, as in the name of the
   language [222]C++.

   In [223]regular expressions, + is often used to indicate "1 or more" in
   a pattern to be matched. For example, x+ means "one or more of the
   letter x". This is the [224]Kleene plus notation. Hyphen-minus usually
   indicates a range ([A-Z] - any capital from 'A' to 'Z'), although it
   can stand for itself ([ABCDE-] any capital from 'A' to 'E' or '-').

   There is no concept of negative zero in mathematics, but in computing
   [225]−0 may have a separate representation from zero. In the [226]IEEE
   floating-point standard, 1 / −0 is [227]negative infinity (

   + is also used to denote added lines in [230]diff output in the
   [231]context format or the [232]unified format.

Other uses

   [[233]edit]

   In physics, the use of plus and minus signs for different
   [234]electrical charges was introduced by [235]Georg Christoph
   Lichtenberg.

   In chemistry, superscripted plus and minus signs are used to indicate
   an ion with a positive or negative charge of 1 (e.g., NH+
   4 ). If the charge is greater than 1, a number indicating the charge is
   written before the sign (as in SO2−
   4 ).

   A plus sign prefixed to a telephone number is used to indicate the form
   used for [236]International Direct Dialing.^[237][23] Its precise usage
   varies by technology and national standards. In the [238]International
   Phonetic Alphabet, [239]subscripted plus and minus signs are used as
   diacritics to indicate [240]advanced or retracted articulations of
   speech sounds.

   The minus sign is also used as tone letter in the orthographies of
   [241]Dan, [242]Krumen, [243]Karaboro, [244]Mwan, [245]Wan, [246]Yaouré,
   [247]Wè, [248]Nyabwa, and [249]Godié.^[250][24] The Unicode character
   used for the tone letter (U+02D7
   ˗ MODIFIER LETTER MINUS SIGN) is different from the mathematical minus
   sign.

   The plus sign sometimes represents /[251]ɨ/ in the orthography of
   [252]Huichol.^[253][25]

   In the [254]algebraic notation used to record games of [255]chess, the
   plus sign + is used to denote a move that puts the opponent into
   [256]check, while a double plus ++ is sometimes used to denote
   [257]double check. Combinations of the plus and minus signs are used to
   evaluate a move (+/−, +/=, =/+, −/+).

   In linguistics, a superscript plus ^+ sometimes replaces the
   [258]asterisk, which denotes unattested [259]linguistic reconstruction.

   In [260]botanical names, a plus sign denotes [261]graft-chimaera.

   In Catholicism, the plus sign before a last name denotes a [262]Bishop,
   and a double plus is used to denote an Archbishop.

Alternative minus signs

   [[267]edit]

   [268][220px-Skjermbilete_2012-11-03_kl._02.48.36.png] "÷" being used as
   a minus sign (not as a division sign) in an excerpt from an official
   Norwegian trading statement form called «Næringsoppgave 1» for the
   taxation year 2010

   There is a [269]commercial minus sign, ⁒, which is used in Germany and
   Scandinavia. The symbol ÷ [270]is used to denote subtraction in
   [271]Scandinavia.^[272][26]

   The [273]hyphen-minus symbol (-) is the form of [274]hyphen most
   commonly used in digital [275]documents. On most keyboards, it is the
   only character that resembles a [276]minus sign or a [277]dash so it is
   also used for these.^[278][27] The name hyphen-minus derives from the
   original [279]ASCII standard,^[280][28] where it was called
   hyphen–(minus).^[281][29] The character is referred to as a hyphen, a
   minus sign, or a dash according to the context where it is being used.

Alternative plus sign

   [[282]edit]

   See also: [283]Up tack

   [284][110px-U%2BFB29.svg.png]

   A [285]Jewish tradition that dates from at least the 19th century is to
   write plus using the symbol ﬩, to avoid the writing of a symbol + that
   could look like a [286]Christian cross.^[287][30]^[288][31] This
   practice was adopted into [289]Israeli schools and is still commonplace
   today in [290]elementary schools (including [291]secular schools) but
   in fewer [292]secondary schools.^[293][31] It is also used occasionally
   in books by religious authors, but most books for adults use the
   international symbol +. [294]Unicode has this symbol at position U+FB29
   ﬩ HEBREW LETTER ALTERNATIVE PLUS SIGN.^[295][32]
