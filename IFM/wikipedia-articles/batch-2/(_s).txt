   Brackets

                               ( )        [ ] { } ⟨ ⟩
                        Brackets ([175]BE)
                                     or
                            parentheses ([176]AE)

   or
   round brackets (BE)^[177][1]
   Square brackets (BE)
   or
   brackets (AE)^[178][1]
   Braces (BE&AE)
   or
   curly brackets (BE)^[179][1]
   Angle brackets (BE&AE)^[180][1]
   or
   chevrons

   ^[181][2]

   A bracket is either of two tall fore- or back-facing [182]punctuation
   marks commonly used to isolate a segment of text or data from its
   surroundings.^[183][3] They come in four main pairs of shapes, as given
   in the box to the right, which also gives their names, that vary
   between [184]British and [185]American English.^[186][1] "Brackets",
   without further qualification, are in British English the (...) marks
   and in American English the [...] marks.^[187][1]^[188][3]

   Other minor bracket shapes exist, such as (for example) slash or
   diagonal brackets used by linguists to enclose [189]phonemes.^[190][4]

   Brackets are typically deployed in symmetric pairs, and an individual
   bracket may be identified as a 'left' or 'right' bracket or,
   alternatively, an "opening bracket" or "closing bracket",^[191][5]
   respectively, depending on the [192]directionality of the context.

   In casual writing and in technical fields such as computing or
   linguistic analysis of [193]grammar, brackets [194]nest, with segments
   of bracketed material containing embedded within them other further
   bracketed sub-segments.^[195][3] The number of opening brackets matches
   the number of closing brackets in such cases.^[196][3]

   [197]Various forms of brackets are used in mathematics, with specific
   mathematical meanings, often for denoting specific [198]mathematical
   functions and [199]subformulas.

History

   [[200]edit]

   Angle brackets or [201]chevrons ⟨ ⟩ were the earliest type of bracket
   to appear in [202]written English. [203]Erasmus coined the term lunula
   to refer to the round brackets or [204]parentheses ( ) recalling the
   shape of the [205]crescent moon ([206]Latin: luna).^[207][6]

   Most typewriters only had the left and right parentheses. Square
   brackets appeared with some teleprinters.

   Braces (curly brackets) first became part of a character set with the
   8-bit code of the [208]IBM 7030 Stretch.^[209][7]

   In 1961, [210]ASCII contained parentheses, square, and curly brackets,
   and also less-than and greater-than signs that could be used as angle
   brackets.

Typography

   [[211]edit]

   In English, [212]typographers mostly prefer not to set brackets in
   [213]italics, even when the enclosed text is italic.^[214][8] However,
   in other languages like [215]German, if brackets enclose text in
   italics, they are usually also set in italics.^[216][9]

Uses of ( )

   [[258]edit]

   Parentheses contain [259]adjunctive material that serves to clarify (in
   the manner of a [260]gloss) or is aside from the main point.^[261][21]

   A comma before or after the material can also be used, though if the
   sentence contains commas for other purposes, visual confusion may
   result. A [262]dash before and after the material is also sometimes
   used.

   Parentheses may be used in formal writing to add supplementary
   information, such as "Senator John McCain ([263]R - Arizona) spoke at
   length". They can also indicate shorthand for "[264]either singular or
   plural" for nouns, e.g. "the claim(s)". It can also be used for
   [265]gender-neutral language, especially in languages with
   [266]grammatical gender, e.g. "(s)he agreed with his/her physician"
   (the slash in the second instance, as one alternative is replacing the
   other, not adding to it).

   Parenthetical phrases have been used extensively in informal writing
   and stream of consciousness literature. Examples include the southern
   American author [267]William Faulkner (see [268]Absalom, Absalom! and
   [269]the Quentin section of The Sound and the Fury) as well as poet
   [270]E. E. Cummings.

   Parentheses have historically been used where the [271]em dash is
   currently used in alternatives, such as "parenthesis)(parentheses".
   Examples of this usage can be seen in editions of [272]Fowler's
   Dictionary of Modern English Usage.

   Parentheses may be nested (generally with one set (such as this) inside
   another set). This is not commonly used in formal writing (though
   sometimes other brackets [especially square brackets] will be used for
   one or more inner set of parentheses [in other words, secondary {or
   even tertiary} phrases can be found within the main parenthetical
   sentence]).

Language

   [[273]edit]

   A [274]parenthesis in rhetoric and [275]linguistics refers to the
   entire bracketed text, not just to the enclosing marks used (so all the
   text in this set of round brackets may be described as "a
   parenthesis").^[276][22] Taking as an example the sentence "Mrs.
   Pennyfarthing (What? Yes, that was her name!) was my landlady.", the
   explanatory phrase between the parentheses is itself called a
   parenthesis. Again, the parenthesis implies that the meaning and flow
   of the bracketed phrase is supplemental to the rest of the text and the
   whole would be unchanged were the parenthesized sentences removed. The
   term refers to the syntax rather than the enclosure method: the same
   clause in the form "Mrs. Pennyfarthing – What? Yes, that was her
   name! – was my landlady" is also a parenthesis.^[277][23] (In
   non-specialist usage, the term "parenthetical phrase" is more widely
   understood.^[278][24])

   In [279]phonetics, parentheses are used for indistinguishable^[280][25]
   or unidentified utterances. They are also seen for silent articulation
   (mouthing),^[281][26] where the expected phonetic transcription is
   derived from lip-reading, and with periods to indicate silent pauses,
   for example (...) or (2 sec).

Enumerations

   [[282]edit]

   An unpaired right parenthesis is often used as part of a label in an
   ordered list, such as this one:

   a) [283]educational testing,
   b) technical writing and diagrams,
   c) [284]market research, and
   d) [285]elections.

Accounting

   [[286]edit]

   Traditionally in [287]accounting, contra amounts are placed in
   parentheses. A debit balance account in a series of credit balances
   will have parenthesis and vice versa.

Parentheses in mathematics

   [[288]edit]

   Main articles: [289]Glossary of mathematical symbols § Parentheses, and
   [290]Bracket (mathematics)

   Parentheses are used in [291]mathematical notation to indicate
   grouping, often inducing a different [292]order of operations. For
   example: in the usual order of algebraic operations, 4 × 3 + 2 equals
   14, since the [293]multiplication is done before the [294]addition.
   However, 4 × (3 + 2) equals 20, because the parentheses override normal
   precedence, causing the addition to be done first. Some authors follow
   the convention in mathematical equations that, when parentheses have
   one level of nesting, the inner pair are parentheses and the outer pair
   are square brackets. Example:

Parentheses in programming languages

   [[295]edit]

   Parentheses are included in the syntaxes of many [296]programming
   languages. Typically needed to denote an argument; to tell the compiler
   what data type the Method/Function needs to look for first in order to
   initialise. In some cases, such as in [297]LISP, parentheses are a
   fundamental construct of the language. They are also often used for
   scoping functions and operators and for arrays. In [298]syntax diagrams
   they are used for grouping, such as in [299]extended Backus–Naur form.

   In Mathematica and the Wolfram language, parentheses are used to
   indicate grouping – for example, with pure anonymous functions.

Taxonomy

   [[300]edit]

   If it is desired to include the [301]subgenus when giving the
   [302]scientific name of an animal species or [303]subspecies, the
   subgenus's name is provided in parentheses between the [304]genus name
   and the [305]specific epithet.^[306][27] For instance, Polyphylla
   (Xerasiobia) alba is a way to cite the species Polyphylla alba while
   also mentioning that it is in the subgenus Xerasiobia.^[307][28] There
   is also a convention of citing a subgenus by enclosing it in
   parentheses after its genus, e.g., Polyphylla (Xerasiobia) is a way to
   refer to the subgenus Xerasiobia within the genus Polyphylla.^[308][29]
   Parentheses are similarly used to cite a subgenus with the name of a
   [309]prokaryotic species, although the [310]International Code of
   Nomenclature of Prokaryotes (ICNP) requires the use of the abbreviation
   "subgen". as well, e.g., Acetobacter (subgen. Gluconoacetobacter)
   liquefaciens.^[311][30]

Chemistry

   [[312]edit]

   Parentheses are used in [313]chemistry to denote a repeated
   substructure within a molecule, e.g. HC(CH[3])[3] ([314]isobutane) or,
   similarly, to indicate the stoichiometry of ionic compounds with such
   substructures: e.g. Ca(NO[3])[2] ([315]calcium nitrate).

   This is a notation that was pioneered by [316]Berzelius, who wanted
   chemical formulae to more resemble algebraic notation, with brackets
   enclosing groups that could be multiplied (e.g. in 3(AlO[2] + 2SO[3])
   the 3 multiplies everything within the
   parentheses).^[317][31]^[318][32]

   In [319]chemical nomenclature, parentheses are used to distinguish
   structural features and multipliers for clarity, for example in the
   polymer [320]poly(methyl methacrylate).^[321][33]

Uses of [ ]

   [[342]edit]

   Square brackets are often used to insert explanatory material or to
   mark where a [word or] passage was omitted from an original material by
   someone other than the original author, or to mark modifications in
   quotations.^[343][36] In transcribed interviews, sounds, responses and
   reactions that are not words but that can be described are set off in
   square brackets — "... [laughs] ...".

   When quoted material is in any way altered, the alterations are
   enclosed in square brackets within the quotation to show that the
   quotation is not exactly as given, or to add an
   [344]annotation.^[345][37] For example: The Plaintiff asserted his
   cause is just, stating,

     [m]y causes is [346][sic] just.

   In the original quoted sentence, the word "my" was capitalized: it has
   been modified in the quotation given and the change signalled with
   brackets. Similarly, where the quotation contained a grammatical error
   (is/are), the quoting author signalled that the error was in the
   original with "[sic]" (Latin for 'thus').

   A bracketed [347]ellipsis, [...], is often used to indicate omitted
   material: "I'd like to thank [several unimportant people] for their
   tolerance [...]"^[348][38] Bracketed comments inserted into a quote
   indicate where the original has been modified for clarity: "I
   appreciate it [the honor], but I must refuse", and "the future of
   psionics [see definition] is in doubt". Or one can quote the original
   statement "I hate to do laundry" with a (sometimes grammatical)
   modification inserted: He "hate[s] to do laundry".

   Additionally, a small letter can be replaced by a capital one, when the
   beginning of the original printed text is being quoted in another piece
   of text or when the original text has been omitted for succinctness—
   for example, when referring to a [349]verbose original: "To the extent
   that policymakers and elite opinion in general have made use of
   economic analysis at all, they have, as the saying goes, done so the
   way a drunkard uses a lamppost: for support, not illumination", can be
   quoted succinctly as: "[P]olicymakers [...] have made use of economic
   analysis [...] the way a drunkard uses a lamppost: for support, not
   illumination." When nested parentheses are needed, brackets are
   sometimes used as a substitute for the inner pair of parentheses within
   the outer pair.^[350][39] When deeper levels of nesting are needed,
   convention is to alternate between parentheses and brackets at each
   level.

   Alternatively, empty square brackets can also indicate omitted
   material, usually single letter only. The original, "Reading is also a
   process and it also changes you." can be rewritten in a quote as: It
   has been suggested that reading can "also change[] you".^[351][40]

   In translated works, brackets are used to signify the same word or
   phrase in the original language to avoid ambiguity.^[352][41] For
   example: He is trained in the way of the open hand [karate].

   [353]Style and usage guides originating in the [354]news industry of
   the twentieth century, such as the [355]AP Stylebook, recommend against
   the use of square brackets because "They cannot be transmitted over
   [356]news wires."^[357][42] However, this guidance has little relevance
   outside of the technological constraints of the industry and era.

   In linguistics, [358]phonetic transcriptions are generally enclosed
   within square brackets,^[359][43] whereas [360]phonemic transcriptions
   typically use paired [361]slashes, according to [362]International
   Phonetic Alphabet rules. Pipes (| |) are often used to indicate a
   [363]morphophonemic rather than phonemic representation. Other
   conventions are double slashes (⫽ ⫽), double pipes (‖ ‖) and curly
   brackets ({ }).

   In [364]lexicography, square brackets usually surround the section of a
   dictionary entry which contains the [365]etymology of the word the
   entry defines.

Proofreading

   [[366]edit]

   Brackets (called move-left symbols or move right symbols) are added to
   the sides of text in [367]proofreading to indicate changes in
   indentation:

   Move left [To Fate I sue, of other means bereft, the only refuge for
   the wretched left.
   Center ]Paradise Lost[
   Move up [368][330px-Quote_to_be_Moved_Up.svg.png]

   Square brackets are used to denote parts of the text that need to be
   checked when preparing drafts prior to finalizing a document.

Law

   [[369]edit]

   Square brackets are used in some countries in the citation of [370]law
   reports to identify parallel citations to non-official reporters. For
   example:

     Chronicle Pub. Co. v [371]Superior Court (1998) 54 Cal.2d 548, [7
     Cal.Rptr. 109]

   In some other countries (such as [372]England and Wales), square
   brackets are used to indicate that the year is part of the citation and
   parentheses are used to indicate the year the judgment was given. For
   example:

     National Coal Board v England [1954] AC 403

   This case is in the 1954 volume of the Appeal Cases reports, although
   the decision may have been given in 1953 or earlier. Compare with:

     (1954) 98 Sol Jo 176

   This citation reports a decision from 1954, in volume 98 of the
   [373]Solicitors Journal which may be published in 1955 or later.

   They often denote points that have not yet been agreed to in legal
   drafts and the year in which a report was made for certain [374]case
   law decisions.

Square brackets in mathematics

   [[375]edit]

   Main article: [376]Glossary of mathematical symbols § Square brackets

   Brackets are used in [377]mathematics in a variety of notations,
   including standard notations for [378]commutators, the [379]floor
   function, the [380]Lie bracket, [381]equivalence classes, the
   [382]Iverson bracket, and [383]matrices.

   Square brackets may be used exclusively or in combination with
   parentheses to represent [384]intervals as interval notation.^[385][44]
   For example, [0,5] represents the set of real numbers from 0 to 5
   inclusive. Both parentheses and brackets are used to denote a half-open
   interval; [5, 12) would be the set of all real numbers between 5 and
   12, including 5 but not 12. The numbers may come as close as they like
   to 12, including 11.999 and so forth, but 12.0 is not included. In some
   European countries, the notation [5, 12[ is also
   used.^[386][45]^[387][46] The endpoint adjoining the square bracket is
   known as closed, whereas the endpoint adjoining the parenthesis is
   known as open.^[388][44]

   In [389]group theory and [390]ring theory, brackets denote the
   [391]commutator. In group theory, the commutator [g, h] is commonly
   defined as g^ −1 h^ −1 g h . In ring theory, the commutator [a, b] is
   defined as a b − b a .

Chemistry

   [[392]edit]

   Square brackets can also be used in [393]chemistry to represent the
   [394]concentration of a [395]chemical substance in solution and to
   denote charge a Lewis structure of an ion (particularly distributed
   charge in a [396]complex ion), repeating chemical units (particularly
   in polymers) and transition state structures, among other uses.

Square brackets in programming languages

   [[397]edit]

   Brackets are used in many computer [398]programming languages,
   primarily for [399]array indexing. But they are also used to denote
   general tuples, sets and other structures, just as in mathematics.
   There may be several other uses as well, depending on the language at
   hand. In [400]syntax diagrams they are used for optional portions, such
   as in [401]extended Backus–Naur form.

Double brackets ⟦ ⟧

   [[402]edit]

   Double brackets (or white square brackets or [403]Scott brackets), ⟦ ⟧,
   are used to indicate the semantic evaluation function in [404]formal
   semantics for natural language and [405]denotational semantics for
   programming languages.^[406][47]^[407][48] In the [408]Wolfram
   Language, double brackets, either as iterated single brackets ([[) or
   ligatures (〚) are used for [409]list indexing.^[410][49]

   The brackets stand for a function that maps a linguistic expression to
   its "denotation" or semantic value. In mathematics, double brackets may
   also be used to denote [411]intervals of integers or, less often, the
   [412]floor function. In papyrology, following the [413]Leiden
   Conventions, they are used to enclose text that has been deleted in
   antiquity.^[414][50]

Lenticular brackets【】

   [[415]edit]

   Look up [416]【 】 in Wiktionary, the free dictionary.

   Some [417]East Asian languages use lenticular brackets 【 】, a
   combination of square brackets and round brackets called [418]方頭括號
   (fāngtóu kuòhào) in [419]Chinese and 隅付き括弧 (sumitsuki kakko) in
   [420]Japanese. They are used in titles and headings in both
   Chinese^[421][51] and Japanese. On the Internet, they are used to
   emphasize a text. In Japanese, they are most frequently seen in
   dictionaries for quoting Chinese characters and Sino-Japanese
   loanwords.

Quine corners ⌜⌝ and half brackets ⸤ ⸥ or ⸢ ⸣

   [[428]edit]

   The Quine corners ⌜ and ⌝ have at least two uses in [429]mathematical
   logic: either as [430]quasi-quotation, a generalization of quotation
   marks, or to denote the [431]Gödel number of the enclosed
   [432]expression.

   Half brackets are used in English to mark added text, such as in
   translations: "Bill saw ⸤her⸥".

   In editions of [433]papyrological texts, half brackets, ⸤ and ⸥ or ⸢
   and ⸣, enclose text which is lacking in the papyrus due to damage, but
   can be restored by virtue of another source, such as an ancient
   quotation of the text transmitted by the papyrus.^[434][52] For
   example, [435]Callimachus Iambus 1.2 reads: ἐκ τῶν ὅκου βοῦν κολλύ⸤βου
   π⸥ιπρήσκουσιν. A hole in the papyrus has obliterated βου π, but these
   letters are supplied by an ancient commentary on the poem. Second
   intermittent sources can be between ⸢ and ⸣. Quine corners are
   sometimes used instead of half brackets.^[436][15]

   constructions.^[440][53]

Curly brackets in programming languages

   [[473]edit]

   See also: [474]Bracing style

   In many programming languages, curly brackets enclose groups of
   [475]statements and create a local [476]scope. Such languages ([477]C,
   C#, C++ and many others) are therefore called [478]curly bracket
   languages.^[479][62] They are also used to define structures and
   [480]enumerated type in these languages.

   In various [481]Unix shells, they enclose a group of strings that are
   used in a process known as brace expansion, where each successive
   string in the group is interpolated at that point in the command line
   to generate the command-line's final form.^[482][63] The mechanism
   originated in the [483]C shell and the string generation mechanism is a
   simple interpolation that can occur anywhere in a command line and
   takes no account of existing filenames.^[484][64]

   In [485]syntax diagrams they are used for repetition, such as in
   [486]extended Backus–Naur form.

   In the [487]Z [488]formal specification language, braces define a set.

Curly brackets in mathematics

   [[489]edit]

   Main article: [490]Glossary of mathematical symbols § Braces

   In [491]mathematics they delimit [492]sets, in what is called set
   notation.^[493][65] Braces enclose either a literal list of set
   elements, or a rule that defines the set elements.^[494][65] For
   example:
     * S = {a, b} defines a set S containing a and b.^[495][65]
     * S = {x | x > 0} defines a set S containing elements (implied to be
       numbers) x[0], x[1], and so on where every x[n] satisfies the rule
       that it is greater than zero.^[496][65]

   They are often also used to denote the [497]Poisson bracket between two
   quantities.

   In [498]ring theory, braces denote the [499]anticommutator where {a, b}
   is defined as a b + b a .

   Look up [500]curly bracket, [501]brace, [502]{ }, [503]{, [504]},
   [505]squiggly, or [506]accolade in Wiktionary, the free dictionary.

   ⟨ and ⟩ are angle brackets in both American and British
   English.^[528][1]^[529][3] In computer slang, they are known as
   "brokets".^[530][66]

   Strictly speaking they are distinct from V-shaped chevrons, as they
   have (where the typography permits it) a broader span than
   chevrons,^[531][67] although when printed often no visual distinction
   is made.^[532][4]

   The ASCII less-than and greater-than characters
   <> are often used for angle brackets. In most cases only those
   characters are accepted by computer programs, and the Unicode angle
   brackets are not recognized (for instance, in [533]HTML tags). The
   characters for "single" [534]guillemets ‹› are also often used, and
   sometimes normal guillemets «» when nested angle brackets are needed.

   The angle brackets or chevrons at U+27E8 and U+27E9 are for
   mathematical use and Western languages, whereas U+3008 and U+3009 are
   for East Asian languages. The chevrons at U+2329 and U+232A are
   deprecated in favour of the U+3008 and U+3009 East Asian angle
   brackets. Unicode discourages their use for mathematics and in Western
   texts,^[535][15] because they are canonically equivalent to the CJK
   code points U+300x and thus likely to render as double-width symbols.
   The less-than and greater-than symbols are often used as replacements
   for chevrons.

Uses of ⟨ ⟩

   [[554]edit]

   Angle brackets are infrequently used to denote [555]words that are
   thought instead of spoken, such as:

          ⟨What an unusual flower!⟩

   In [556]textual criticism, and hence in many editions of pre-modern
   works, chevrons denote sections of the text which are illegible or
   otherwise lost; the editor will often insert their own reconstruction
   where possible within them.^[557][68]

   In [558]comic books, chevrons are often used to mark dialogue that has
   been translated notionally from another language; in other words, if a
   character is speaking another language, instead of writing in the other
   language and providing a translation, one writes the translated text
   within chevrons. Since no foreign language is actually written, this is
   only notionally translated.^[[559]citation needed]

   In [560]linguistics, angle brackets identify [561]graphemes (e.g.,
   letters of an alphabet) or [562]orthography, as in "The English word
   /kæt/ is spelled ⟨cat⟩."^[563][69]^[564][70]^[565][68]
   (See [566]IPA Brackets and transcription delimiters.)

   In [567]epigraphy, they may be used for mechanical transliterations of
   a text into the Latin script.^[568][70]

   In [569]East Asian punctuation, angle brackets are used as
   [570]quotation marks. Chevron-like symbols are part of standard
   [571]Chinese, [572]Japanese and – less frequently – [573]Korean
   punctuation, where they generally enclose the titles of books, as: 〈
   ... 〉 or 《 ... 》 for traditional [574]vertical printing — written in
   vertical lines — and as 〈 ... 〉 or 《 ... 》 for [575]horizontal printing
   — in horizontal.

Angle brackets in mathematics

   [[576]edit]

   Main article: [577]Glossary of mathematical symbols § ⟨⟩

   Angle brackets (or 'chevrons') are used in [578]group theory to write
   [579]group presentations, and to denote the [580]subgroup generated by
   a collection of elements. In [581]set theory, chevrons or parentheses
   are used to denote [582]ordered pairs^[583][71] and other [584]tuples,
   whereas curly brackets are used for unordered sets.

   [[595]edit]

   In [596]C++ chevrons (actually less-than and greater-than) are used to
   surround arguments to [597]templates. They are also used to surround
   the names of [598]header files; this usage was inherited from and is
   also found in [599]C.

   In the [600]Z [601]formal specification language, chevrons define a
   sequence.

   In [602]HTML, chevrons (actually 'greater than' and 'less than'
   symbols) are used to bracket meta text. For example <b> denotes that
   the following text should be displayed as bold. Pairs of meta text tags
   are required – much as brackets themselves are usually in pairs. The
   end of the bold text segment would be indicated by </b>. This use is
   sometimes extended as an informal mechanism for communicating mood or
   tone in digital formats such as messaging, for example adding "<sighs>"
   at the end of a sentence.

   Look up [603]angle bracket, [604]⟨ ⟩, [605]< >, [606]broket,
   [607]pointy bracket, or [608]diamond bracket in Wiktionary, the free
   dictionary.
