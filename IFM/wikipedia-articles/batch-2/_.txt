   The asterisk ([153]/ˈæstərɪsk/ *), from [154]Late Latin asteriscus,
   from [155]Ancient Greek ἀστερίσκος, asteriskos, "little
   star",^[156][1]^[157][2] is a [158]typographical symbol. It is so
   called because it resembles a conventional image of a [159]heraldic
   star.

   [160]Computer scientists and [161]mathematicians often vocalize it as
   star (as, for example, in the [162]A* search algorithm or
   [163]C*-algebra). An asterisk is usually five- or six-pointed in
   [164]print and six- or eight-pointed when handwritten, though more
   complex forms exist. Its most common use is to call out a
   [165]footnote. It is also often used to censor offensive words.

   In [166]computer science, the asterisk is commonly used as a
   [167]wildcard character, or to denote [168]pointers, repetition, or
   [169]multiplication.

History

   [[170]edit]

   [171][220px-Asteriskos.jpg] The asteriskos used in an early Greek
   papyrus.[172][220px-Asteriskos.gif] Early asterisks seen in the margin
   of Greek papyrus.[173][220px-Midrashasteriscus.jpg] ^[174][3]

   The asterisk was already in use as a symbol in [175]ice age [176]cave
   paintings.^[177][4] There is also a two-thousand-year-old character
   used by [178]Aristarchus of Samothrace called the asteriskos, [179]※,
   which he used when proofreading Homeric poetry to mark lines that were
   duplicated.^[180][5] [181]Origen is known to have also used the
   asteriskos to mark missing [182]Hebrew lines from his
   [183]Hexapla.^[184][6] The asterisk evolved in shape over time, but its
   meaning as a symbol used to correct defects remained.

   In the Middle Ages, the asterisk was used to emphasize a particular
   part of text, often linking those parts of the text to a marginal
   comment.^[185][7] However, an asterisk was not always used.

   One hypothesis to the origin of the asterisk is that it stems from the
   5000-year-old [186]Sumerian character [187]dingir, 𒀭,^[188][8] though
   this hypothesis seems to only be based on visual appearance.^[189][9]

Usage

   [[190]edit]

Censorship

   [[191]edit]

   Main article: [192]Wordfilter

   When toning down [193]expletives, asterisks are often used to replace
   letters. For example, the word "badword" might become "ba***rd",
   "b*****d", "b******" or even "*******".^[194][10] Vowels tend to be
   censored with an asterisk more than consonants, but the intelligibility
   of censored profanities with multiple syllables such as "b*dw*rd" and
   "b*****d" or "ba****d", or uncommon ones is higher if put in context
   with surrounding text.^[195][11]

   When a document containing [196]classified information is published,
   the document may be [197]"sanitized" ([198]redacted) by replacing the
   classified information with asterisks. For example, the
   [199]Intelligence and Security Committee Russia report.

Competitive sports and games

   [[200]edit]

   In colloquial usage, an asterisk attached to a sporting [201]record
   indicates that it is somehow tainted. This is because results that have
   been considered dubious or set aside are recorded in the record books
   with an asterisk rendering to a footnote explaining the reason or
   reasons for concern.^[202][12]

Baseball

   [[203]edit]

   The usage of the term in sports arose during the 1961 baseball season
   in which [204]Roger Maris of the [205]New York Yankees was threatening
   to break [206]Babe Ruth's 34-year-old single-season [207]home run
   record. Ruth had amassed 60 home runs in a season with only 154 games,
   but Maris was playing the first season in the American League's newly
   expanded 162-game season. Baseball Commissioner [208]Ford C. Frick, a
   friend of Ruth's during the legendary slugger's lifetime, held a press
   conference to announce his "ruling" that should Maris take longer than
   154 games both records would be acknowledged by Major League Baseball,
   but that some "distinctive mark" [his term]^[209][13] be placed next to
   Maris', which should be listed alongside Ruth's achievement in the
   "record books". The asterisk as such a mark was suggested at that time
   by [210]New York Daily News sportswriter [211]Dick Young, not
   Frick.^[212][13] The reality, however, was that MLB actually had no
   direct control over any record books until many years later, and it all
   was merely a suggestion on Frick's part. Within a few years the
   controversy died down and all prominent baseball record keepers listed
   Maris as the single-season record holder for as long as he held the
   record.^[213][13]

   Nevertheless, the stigma of holding a tainted record remained with
   Maris for many years, and the concept of a real or figurative asterisk
   denoting less-than-accepted "official" records has become widely used
   in sports and other competitive endeavors. A 2001 [214]TV movie about
   Maris's record-breaking season was called [215]61* (pronounced
   sixty-one asterisk) in reference to the controversy.

   Uproar over the integrity of baseball records and whether or not
   qualifications should be added to them arose again in the late 1990s,
   when a [216]steroid-fueled power explosion led to the shattering of
   Maris' record. Even though it was obvious - and later
   admitted^[217][14] - by [218]Mark McGwire that he was heavily on
   steroids when he hit 70 home runs in 1998, ruling authorities did
   nothing - to the annoyance of many fans and sportswriters. Three years
   later self-confessed steroid-user [219]Barry Bonds pushed that record
   out to 73, and fans once again began to call for an asterisk in the
   sport's record books.

   Fans were especially critical and clamored louder for baseball to act
   during the 2007 season, as Bonds approached and later broke [220]Hank
   Aaron's career home run record of 755.^[221][15]

   The [222]Houston Astros' 2017 World Series win was marred after an
   investigation by MLB revealed the team's involvement in a
   [223]sign-stealing scheme during that season. Fans, appalled by what
   they perceived to be overly lenient discipline against the Astros
   players, nicknamed the team the "Houston Asterisks".^[224][16]

   In recent years, the asterisk has come into use on baseball scorecards
   to denote a "great defensive play."^[225][17]

Usage in anti-doping campaigns

   [[226]edit]

     * By the end of the first decade of the 21st century, the association
       of baseball and its records with doping had become so notorious
       that the term "asterisk" had become firmly associated with
       [227]doping in sport. In February 2011 the [228]United States
       Olympic Committee and the [229]Ad Council launched an
       [230]anti-steroid campaign called "Play Asterisk Free"^[231][18]
       aimed at teens. The campaign, whose logo uses a [232]heavy
       asterisk(✱), first launched in 2008 under the name Don't Be An
       Asterisk.^[233][19]

Cricket

   [[234]edit]

     * In [235]cricket, it signifies a total number of runs scored by a
       batsman without losing his wicket; e.g. "107*" means "107 [236]not
       out".
     * Where only the scores of the two batsmen that are currently in are
       being shown, an asterisk following a batsman's score indicates that
       he is due to face the next ball to be delivered.
     * When written before a player's name on a scorecard, it indicates
       the [237]captain of the team.
     * It is also used on television when giving a career statistic during
       a match. For example, "47*" in a number of matches column means
       that the current game is the player's 47th.

Other sports

   [[238]edit]

   During the first decades of the 21st century, the term asterisk to
   denote a tainted accomplishment^[[239]citation needed] caught on in
   other sports first in North America and then, due in part to North
   American sports' widespread media exposure, around the world.

Computing

   [[240]edit]

Computer science

   [[241]edit]

     * In [242]computer science, the asterisk is used in [243]regular
       expressions to denote zero or more repetitions of a pattern; this
       use is also known as the [244]Kleene star or Kleene closure after
       [245]Stephen Kleene. In the [246]Unified Modeling Language, the
       asterisk is used to denote zero to many classes.

Computer interfaces

   [[247]edit]

     * In some [248]command line interfaces, such as the [249]Unix shell
       and [250]Microsoft's [251]CMD, the asterisk is the [252]wildcard
       character and stands for any [253]string of [254]characters. This
       is also known as a wildcard symbol. A common use of the wildcard is
       in searching for files on a computer. For instance, if a user
       wished to find a document called Document 1, search terms such as
       Doc* and D*ment* would return this file. Due to being a wildcard,
       they could also return files like Document 2 and (only the latter)
       Dark Knight Monument.jpg. Document* would in fact return any file
       that begins with Document, and D*ment* any file that began with D
       and had 'ment' somewhere in its name.
     * In some [255]graphical user interfaces an asterisk is pre- or
       appended to the current working document name shown in a window's
       title bar to indicate that unsaved changes exist.
     * In many computing and [256]Internet applications an asterisk, or
       another character, is displayed to indicate that a character of a
       password or other confidential information has been entered,
       without the risk of displaying the actual character.
     * In [257]Commodore (and related) [258]files systems, an asterisk
       appearing next to a filename in a directory listing denotes an
       improperly closed file, commonly called a "splat file".
     * In travel industry [259]Global Distribution Systems, the asterisk
       is the display command to retrieve all or part of a [260]Passenger
       Name Record.
     * In [261]HTML [262]web forms, a (usually red) asterisk can be used
       to denote required fields.
     * In [263]Markdown, surrounding a set of characters or words in one
       asterisk italicizes, two asterisks bolds, and three asterisks both
       italicizes and bolds.^[264][20] See the table below for examples of
       all three uses of the asterisk in Markdown, including how it
       translates to HTML and how it renders.

   Markdown Italicized text is the *cat's meow*. I just love **bold
   text**. This text is ***really important***.
   HTML Italicized text is the <em>cat's meow</em>. I just love
   <strong>bold text</strong>. This text is <em><strong>really
   important</strong></em>.
   Rendered Output Italicized text is the cat's meow. I just love bold
   text. This text is really important.

     * [265]Chat room etiquette calls on one asterisk to correct a
       misspelled word or typo that has already been submitted. For
       example, one could post lck, then follow it with *luck or luck*
       (the placement of the * on the left or right is a matter of
       personal style) to correct the word's spelling, or if it's someone
       else that notices the mistake, they might also use *luck or
       luck*.^[266][21] This also applies to typos that result in a
       different word from the intended one but are correctly spelled.
          + Enclosing a phrase between two asterisks is used to denote an
            action the user is "performing", e.g. *pulls out a paper*,
            although this usage is also common on forums, and less so on
            most chat rooms due to /me or similar commands. [267]Hyphens
            (-action-) and double [268]colons (::action::) as well as the
            operator /me are also used for similar purposes.

Adding machines and printing calculators

   [[269]edit]

     * Some models of [270]adding machines and [271]printing calculators
       use the asterisk to denote the total, or the terminal sum or
       difference of an addition or subtraction sequence, respectively.
       The symbol is sometimes given on the printout to indicate this
       total.^[[272]citation needed]

Programming languages

   [[273]edit]

   Many [274]programming languages and [275]calculators use the asterisk
   as a symbol for [276]multiplication. It also has a number of special
   meanings in specific languages, for instance:
     * In some programming languages such as the [277]C, [278]C++, and
       [279]Go programming languages, the asterisk is used to
       [280]dereference or declare a [281]pointer variable.
     * In the [282]Common Lisp programming language, the names of
       [283]global variables are conventionally set off with asterisks,
       *LIKE-THIS*.
     * In the [284]Ada, [285]Fortran, [286]Perl, [287]Python, [288]Ruby
       programming languages, in some dialects of the [289]Pascal
       programming language, and many others, a double asterisk is used to
       signify [290]exponentiation: 5**3 is 5^3 = 125.
     * In the [291]Perl programming language, the asterisk is used to
       refer to the [292]typeglob of all variables with a given name.
     * In the programming languages [293]Ruby and [294]Python, * has two
       specific uses. First, the unary * operator applied to a list object
       inside a function call will expand that list into the arguments of
       the function call. Second, a parameter preceded by * in the
       parameter list for a function will result in any extra positional
       parameters being aggregated into a [295]tuple (Python) or
       [296]array (Ruby), and likewise a parameter preceded by ** will
       result in any extra [297]keyword parameters being aggregated into a
       [298]dictionary (Python) or [299]hash (Ruby).
     * In the [300]APL language, the asterisk represents the
       [301]exponential and [302]exponentiation functions, with *X
       representing e^X, and Y*X representing Y^X.
     * In IBM [303]Job Control Language, the asterisk has various
       functions, including in-stream data in the DD statement, the
       default print stream as SYSOUT=*, and as a self-reference in place
       of a procedure step name to refer to the same procedure step where
       it appears.
     * In [304]Haskell, the asterisk represents the set of well-formed,
       fully applied types; that is, a 0-ary kind of types.
     * In many markup languages, putting text between two asterisks makes
       the text bold or italic. For example, *Hello world!* will often
       turn into "Hello world!" or "Hello world!".

Comments in programming languages

   [[305]edit]

   Main article: [306]block comments

   In the [307]B programming language and languages that borrow syntax
   from it, such as [308]C, [309]PHP, [310]Java, or [311]C#, comments in
   the [312]source code (for information to people, ignored by the
   [313]compiler) are marked by an asterisk combined with the slash:

 /* This section displays message if user input was not valid
    (comment ignored by compiler) */

   Some [314]Pascal-like programming languages, for example, [315]Object
   Pascal, [316]Modula-2, [317]Modula-3, and [318]Oberon, as well as
   several other languages including [319]ML, [320]Wolfram Language
   ([321]Mathematica), [322]AppleScript, [323]OCaml, [324]Standard ML, and
   [325]Maple, use an asterisk combined with a parenthesis:

 (* Do not change this variable - it is used later
    (comment ignored by compiler) *)

   [326]CSS also uses the slash-star comment format.

body {
  /* This ought to make the text more readable for far-sighted people */
  font-size: 24pt;
}

   Each computing language has its own way of handling comments; /* ... */
   and similar notations are not universal.

History of information technology

   [[327]edit]

   The asterisk was a supported symbol on the IBM 026 [328]Keypunch
   (introduced in 1949 and used to create [329]punch cards with data for
   early computer systems).^[330][22] It was also included in the
   [331]FIELDATA character encoding^[332][23] and the [333]ASCII
   standard.^[334][24]^[335][25]^[336][26]

Education

   [[343]edit]

     * In the [344]A-Level examinations in the [345]United Kingdom and the
       [346]PSLE in [347]Singapore, A* ("A-star") is a special top grade
       that is distinguished from grade A.
     * In the [348]Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE)
       examination in [349]Hong Kong, 5** (5-star-star) and 5* (5-star)
       are two special top grades that are distinguished from Level 5.
       Level 5** is the highest level a candidate can attain in HKDSE.

Fluid mechanics

   [[350]edit]

   In [351]fluid mechanics an asterisk in superscript is sometimes used to
   mean a property at sonic speed.^[352][27]

Games

   [[353]edit]

     * Certain categories of character types in [354]role-playing games
       are called splats, and the game supplements describing them are
       called [355]splatbooks. This usage originated with the shorthand
       "*book" for this type of supplement to various [356]World of
       Darkness games, such as Clanbook: Ventrue (for [357]Vampire: The
       Masquerade) or Tribebook: Black Furies (for [358]Werewolf: The
       Apocalypse), and this usage has spread to other games with similar
       character-type supplements. For example, [359]Dungeons & Dragons
       Third Edition has had several lines of splatbooks: the "X & Y"
       series including Sword & Fist and Tome & Blood prior to the "3.5"
       revision, the "Complete X" series including Complete Warrior and
       Complete Divine, and the "Races of X" series including Races of
       Stone and Races of the Wild.
     * In many [360]MUDs and [361]MOOs, as well as "male", "female", and
       other more esoteric genders, there is a gender called "splat",
       which uses an asterisk to replace the letters that differ in
       standard English gender pronouns. For example, h* is used rather
       than him or her. Also, asterisks are used to signify doing an
       action, for example, "*action*".
     * [362]Game show producer [363]Mark Goodson used a six-pointed
       asterisk as his trademark. It is featured prominently on many set
       pieces from [364]The Price Is Right.
     * [365]Scrabble players put an asterisk after a word to indicate that
       an illegal play was made.^[366][28]

Human genetics

   [[367]edit]

     * In human genetics, [368]* is used to denote that someone is a
       member of a [369]haplogroup and not any of its subclades (see
       [370]* (haplogroup)).

Linguistics

   [[371]edit]

   In [372]linguistics, an asterisk may be used for a range of purposes
   depending on what is being discussed. The symbol is used to indicate
   reconstructed words of [373]proto-languages (for which there are no
   records). For modern languages, it may be placed before posited
   problematic word forms, phrases or sentences to flag that they are
   hypothetical, ungrammatical, unpronounceable, etc.

   Historical linguist [374]August Schleicher is cited as first using the
   asterisk for linguistic purposes, specifically for [375]unattested
   forms that are [376]linguistic reconstructions.^[377][29]^: 208 

   Using the asterisk for descriptive and not just historical purposes
   arose in the 20th century.^[378][30]^: 334  By analogy with its use in
   historical linguistics, the asterisk was variously prepended to
   "hypothetical" or "unattested" elements in modern
   language.^[379][30]^: 332  Its usage also expanded to include
   "non-existent" or "impossible" forms. [380]Leonard Bloomfield (1933)
   uses the asterisk with forms such as *cran, impossible to occur in
   isolation: cran- only occurs within the compound
   cranberry.^[381][31]^: 160 ^[382][30]^: 331  Such usage for a
   "non-existent form" was also found in French, German and Italian works
   in the middle of the 20th century.^[383][30]^: 332–34 

   Asterisk usage in linguistics later came to include not just impossible
   forms, but "[384]ungrammatical sentences", those that are "ill formed
   for the native speaker".^[385][30]^: 332  The expansion of asterisk
   usage to entire sentences is often credited to [386]Noam Chomsky, but
   Chomsky in 1968 already describes this usage as
   "conventional".^[387][30]^: 330  Linguist Fred Householder claims some
   credit,^[388][32]^: 365 ^[389][30]^: 331  but Giorgio Graffi argues
   that using an asterisk for this purpose predates his
   works.^[390][30]^: 336 ^[391][a]

   The meaning of the asterisk usage in specific linguistic works may go
   unelucidated so can be unclear.^[392][32]^: 369 ^[393][b] Linguistics
   sometimes uses double asterisks (**), another symbol such as the
   [394]question mark, or both symbols (e.g. ?*) to indicate degrees of
   unacceptability.^[395][32]^: 369 

Historical linguistics

   [[396]edit]

   In [397]historical linguistics, the asterisk marks words or phrases
   that are not directly recorded in texts or other media, and that are
   therefore [398]reconstructed on the basis of other linguistic material
   by the [399]comparative method.^[400][33]

   In the following example, the [401]Proto-Germanic word *ainlif is a
   reconstructed form.
     * *ainlif → [402]endleofan → eleven

   A double asterisk (**) sometimes indicates an intermediary or proximate
   reconstructed form (e.g. a single asterisk for reconstructed
   [403]thirteenth century Chinese and a double asterisk for
   reconstructions of older [404]Ancient Chinese^[405][34]^: 5  or a
   double asterisk for proto-[406]Popolocan and a single asterisk for
   intermediary forms^[407][35]^: 322 ).

   In other cases, the double asterisk denotes a form that would be
   expected according to a rule, but is not actually found. That is, it
   indicates a reconstructed form that is not found or used, and in place
   of which another form is found in actual usage:
     * For the plural, **kubar would be expected, but separate masculine
       plural akābir ‏[408]أكابر‎ and feminine plural kubrayāt
       ‏[409]كبريات‎ are found as irregular forms.

Ungrammaticality

   [[410]edit]

   In most areas of linguistics, but especially in [411]syntax, an
   asterisk in front of a word or phrase indicates that the word or phrase
   is not used because it is [412]ungrammatical.^[413][30]^: 332 
     * wake her up / *wake up her

   An asterisk before a parenthesis indicates that the lack of the word or
   phrase inside is ungrammatical, while an asterisk after the opening
   bracket of the parenthesis indicates that the existence of the word or
   phrase inside is ungrammatical—e.g., the following indicates "go the
   station" would be ungrammatical:
     * go *(to) the station

   Use of an asterisk to denote forms or sentences that are ungrammatical
   is often complemented by the use of the [414]question mark (?) to
   indicate a word, phrase or sentence that is avoided, questionable or
   strange, but not necessarily outright ungrammatical.^[415][c]

   Other sources go further and use several symbols (e.g. the asterisk,
   question mark, and [416]degree symbol °) to indicate gradations or a
   continuum of acceptability.^[417][d]

Ambiguity

   [[418]edit]

   Since a word marked with an asterisk could mean either "unattested" or
   "impossible", it is important in some contexts to distinguish these
   meanings. In general, authors retain asterisks for "unattested", and
   prefix ^x, **, †, or ? for the latter meaning.^[419][e] An alternative
   is to append the asterisk (or another symbol, possibly to differentiate
   between even more cases) at the end.^[[420]citation needed]

Optimality theory

   [[421]edit]

   In [422]optimality theory, asterisks are used as "violation marks" in
   tableau cells to denote a violation of a constraint by an output
   form.^[423][41]

Phonetic transcription

   [[424]edit]

   In phonetic transcription using the [425]International Phonetic
   Alphabet and similar systems, an asterisk was historically used to
   denote that the word it preceded was a proper noun.^[426][42]^[427][43]
   See this example from W. Perrett's 1921 transcription of Gottfried
   Keller's Das Fähnlein der sieben Aufrechten:^[428][44]

          ˈkɑinə ˈreːdə, virt ˈniçts daˈraˑus! zɑːktə *ˈheːdigər ˈkurts.
          (»Keine Rede, wird nichts daraus!« sagte Hediger kurz.)

   This convention is no longer usual.^[429][45]

Mathematical typography

   [[466]edit]

   In fine mathematical typography, the [467]Unicode character U+2217
   ∗ ASTERISK OPERATOR (in HTML, &lowast;; not to be confused with U+204E
   ⁎ LOW ASTERISK) is available. This character also appeared in the
   position of the regular asterisk in the [468]PostScript symbol
   character set in the Symbol font included with Windows and Macintosh
   operating systems and with many printers.^[[469]citation needed] It
   should be used for a large asterisk that lines up with the other
   mathematical operators, sitting on the math centerline rather than on
   the text baseline.^[470][47]

Music

   [[471]edit]

     * In [472]musical notation the sign [473][20px-Music-pedalup.svg.png]
       (U+1D1AF 𝆯 MUSICAL SYMBOL PEDAL UP MARK) indicates when the
       [474]sustain pedal of the piano should be lifted.
     * In [475]liturgical music, an asterisk is often used to denote a
       deliberate pause.

Religious texts

   [[476]edit]

     * In the Geneva Bible and the King James Bible, an asterisk is used
       to indicate a marginal comment or scripture reference.
     * In the [477]Leeser Bible, an asterisk is used to mark off the seven
       subdivisions of the weekly Torah portion. It is also used to mark
       the few verses to be repeated by the reader of the Haftara.
     * In American printings of the [478]Book of Common Prayer, an
       asterisk is used to divide a verse of a Psalm in two portions for
       responsive reading. British printings use a spaced colon (" : ")
       for the same purpose.^[479][48]
     * In [480]pointed psalms, an asterisk is used to denote a break or
       breath.

Star of Life

   [[481]edit]

   [482][220px-Star_of_life2.svg.png] The Star of Life may represent
   [483]emergency medical services

   A [484]Star of Life, a six-bar asterisk overlaid with the [485]Rod of
   Asclepius (the symbol of health), may be used as an alternative to
   cross or crescent symbols on ambulances.

Statistical results

   [[486]edit]

   In many scientific publications, the asterisk is employed as a
   shorthand to denote the [487]statistical significance of results when
   testing [488]hypotheses. When the likelihood that a result occurred by
   chance alone is below a certain level, one or more asterisks are
   displayed. Popular significance levels are <0.05 (*), <0.01 (**), and
   <0.001 (***).

Telephony

   [[489]edit]

   Further information: [490]E.161

   On a [491]tone dialling telephone keypad, the asterisk (called star) is
   one of the two special keys (the other is the [492]'square key – almost
   invariably replaced by the [493]number sign # (called 'pound sign'
   (US), 'hash' (other countries), or 'hex'), and is found to the left of
   the zero^[494][49]). They are used to navigate menus in systems such as
   [495]voice mail, or in [496]vertical service codes.

Typography

   [[497]edit]

   removed.

   [504]books · [505]scholar · [506]JSTOR (April 2020) ([507]Learn how and
   when to remove this message)

     * The asterisk is used to call out a [508]footnote, especially when
       there is only one on the page. Less commonly, multiple asterisks
       are used to denote different footnotes on a page (i.e., *, **,
       ***).^[509][50]^[510][51] Typically, an asterisk is positioned
       after a word or phrase and preceding its accompanying footnote.
       Other characters are also used for this purpose, such as
       [511]dagger (†, ‡) or superscript letters and numbers (as in
       Wikipedia). In marketing and advertising, asterisks or other
       symbols are used to refer readers discreetly to terms or conditions
       for a certain statement, the "[512]small print".
     * In English-language typography the asterisk is placed after all
       other punctuation marks (for example, commas, colons, or periods)
       except for the dash.^[513][52]^[514][53]

   [515][220px-Section_break_02_by_Pengo.jpg] Asterisks used to illustrate
   a [516]section break in [517]Alice's Adventures in Wonderland
     * Three spaced asterisks centered on a page is called a [518]dinkus
       and may represent a jump to a different scene, thought, or
       [519]section.
     * A group of three asterisks arranged in a triangular formation is
       called an [520]asterism. It may be used instead of a name on a
       title page.^[521][51]
     * One or more asterisks may be used as censorship over all or part of
       a word.
     * Asterisks are sometimes used as an alternative to
       [522]typographical bullets to indicate items of a list.
     * Asterisks can be used in textual media to represent *[523]emphasis*
       when [524]bold or [525]italic text is not available (e.g.,
       [526]Twitter, [527]text messaging).
     * Asterisks may denote conversational [528]repair, or corrections to
       misspelling or misstatements in previous electronic messages,
       particularly when replacement or retraction of a previous writing
       is not possible, such as with "immediate delivery" messages or
       "instant messages" that can not be edited. Usually this takes the
       form of a message consisting solely of the corrected text, with an
       asterisk placed before (or after) the correction. For example, one
       might send a message reading "*morning" or "morning*" to correct
       the misspelling in the message "I had a good
       mroning".^[529][21]^[[530]citation needed]
     * Bounding asterisks as "a kind of self-describing stage direction",
       as linguist [531]Ben Zimmer has put it. For example, in "Another
       gas station robbery *sigh*", the writer uses *sigh* to express
       disappointment (but does not necessarily literally sigh).^[532][54]

Notes

   [[558]edit]

    1. [559]^ "...Chomsky adopted, with some delay, a convention which had
       been (possibly) circulated among generative grammarians by
       Householder. However, Householder (who was not a generative
       grammarian) was simply following a practice which had already been
       introduced by others, and which was so automatic as to be adopted
       almost unconsciously."^[560][30]^: 336 
    2. [561]^ The numerous and confusing uses are detailed in Householder
       (1973).^[562][32]
    3. [563]^ One article notes succinctly that "...common practice in
       linguistics [is that] an asterisk preceding a word, a clause or a
       sentence is used to indicate ungrammaticality or unacceptability,
       while a question mark is used to indicate questionable
       usage",^[564][36]^: 15  another that, "A question mark indicates
       that the example is marginal; an asterisk indicates
       unacceptability"^[565][37]^: 409  and another that "examples
       preceded by an asterisk are ungrammatical, and those preceded by a
       question mark would be considered strange".^[566][38]^: 623 
    4. [567]^ One example is "rough approximations of acceptability are
       given in four gradations and indicated as follows: normal and
       preferred, no mark; acceptable but not preferred, degree sign °;
       marginally acceptable, question mark (?); unacceptable, asterisk
       (*)."^[568][39]^: 123–24 
    5. [569]^ For example, one linguistic article states that, "A question
       mark (?) denotes uncertainty; an asterisk (*) indicates a
       classificatory base not encountered in my own
       data."^[570][40]^: 119
