   Alternative decimal expansion of 1

   [139][300px-999_Perspective.svg.png] Stylistic impression of the number
   0.9999..., representing the digit 9 repeating infinitely

   In [140]mathematics, 0.999... (also written as 0.9, 0..9, or 0.(9))
   denotes the smallest number greater than or equal to every [141]number
   in the sequence (0.9, 0.99, 0.999, ...). It can be proved that this
   number is [142]1; that is,

          [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
          displaystyle="true" scriptlevel="0"> <mn>0.999...</mn>
          <mo>=</mo> <mn>1.</mn> </mstyle> </mrow> <annotation
          encoding="application/x-tex">{\displaystyle
          0.999...=1.}</annotation> </semantics> :MATH]
          {\displaystyle 0.999...=1.}

   Despite common misconceptions, 0.999... is not "almost exactly 1" or
   "very, very nearly but not quite 1"; rather, "0.999..." and "1"
   represent exactly the same number.

   An elementary proof is given below that involves only [143]elementary
   arithmetic and the fact that there is no [144]positive real number less
   than all
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
   displaystyle="true" scriptlevel="0"> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD">
   <mstyle displaystyle="false" scriptlevel="0"> <mfrac> <mn>1</mn> <msup>
   <mn>10</mn> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mi>n</mi> </mrow> </msup>
   </mfrac> </mstyle> </mrow> </mstyle> </mrow> <annotation
   encoding="application/x-tex">{\displaystyle {\tfrac
   {1}{10^{n}}}}</annotation> </semantics> :MATH]
   {\displaystyle {\tfrac {1}{10^{n}}}} where n is a natural number, a
   property that results immediately from the [145]Archimedean property of
   the [146]real numbers.

   There are many other ways of showing this equality, from [147]intuitive
   arguments to [148]mathematically rigorous [149]proofs. The intuitive
   arguments are generally based on properties of [150]finite decimals
   that are extended without proof to infinite decimals. The proofs are
   generally based on basic properties of real numbers and methods of
   [151]calculus, such as [152]series and [153]limits. A question studied
   in [154]mathematics education is why some people reject this equality.

   In [155]other number systems, 0.999... can have the same meaning, a
   different definition, or be undefined. Every nonzero [156]terminating
   decimal has two equal representations (for example, 8.32000... and
   8.31999...). Having values with multiple representations is a feature
   of all [157]positional numeral systems that represent the real numbers.

Elementary proof

   [[158]edit]
   [159][220px-Archimedean_property_for_Achilles_and_Tortoise_example.svg.
   png] The [160]Archimedean property: any point x before the finish line
   lies between two of the points P[n] (inclusive).

   It is possible to prove the equation 0.999... = 1 using just the
   mathematical tools of comparison and addition of (finite) [161]decimal
   numbers, without any reference to more advanced topics such as
   [162]series and [163]limits. The proof given [164]below is a direct
   formalization of the intuitive fact that, if one draws 0.9, 0.99,
   0.999, etc. on the [165]number line, there is no room left for placing
   a number between them and 1. The meaning of the notation 0.999... is
   the least point on the number line lying to the right of all of the
   numbers 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, etc. Because there is ultimately no room
   between 1 and these numbers, the point 1 must be this least point, and
   so 0.999... = 1.

Intuitive explanation

   [[166]edit]

   If one places 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, etc. on the [167]number line, one sees
   immediately that all these points are to the left of 1, and that they
   get closer and closer to 1. For any number
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
   displaystyle="true" scriptlevel="0"> <mi>x</mi> </mstyle> </mrow>
   <annotation encoding="application/x-tex">{\displaystyle x}</annotation>
   </semantics> :MATH]
   {\displaystyle x} that is less than 1, the sequence 0.9, 0.99, 0.999,
   and so on will eventually reach a number larger than ⁠
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
   displaystyle="true" scriptlevel="0"> <mi>x</mi> </mstyle> </mrow>
   <annotation encoding="application/x-tex">{\displaystyle x}</annotation>
   </semantics> :MATH]
   {\displaystyle x} ⁠. So, it does not make sense to identify 0.999...
   with any number smaller than 1. Meanwhile, every number larger than 1
   will be larger than any decimal of the form 0.999...9 for any finite
   number of nines. Therefore, 0.999... cannot be identified with any
   number larger than 1, either. Because 0.999... cannot be bigger than 1
   or smaller than 1, it must equal 1 if it is to be any real number at
   all.^[168][1]^[169][2]

   numbers. Real numbers may be enlarged into [175]number systems, such as
   [176]hyperreal numbers, with infinitely small numbers
   ([177]infinitesimals) and infinitely large numbers ([178]infinite

   0.999... is generally not used, as there is no smallest number among
   the numbers larger than all 0.(9)[n].^[181][a]

Least upper bounds and completeness

   [[182]edit]

   Part of what this argument shows is that there is a [183]least upper
   bound of the sequence 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, etc.: the smallest number that
   is greater than all of the terms of the sequence. One of the
   [184]axioms of the [185]real number system is the [186]completeness
   axiom, which states that every bounded sequence has a least upper
   bound.^[187][7]^[188][8] This least upper bound is one way to define
   infinite decimal expansions: the real number represented by an infinite
   decimal is the least upper bound of its finite truncations.^[189][9]
   The argument here does not need to assume completeness to be valid,
   because it shows that this particular sequence of rational numbers has
   a least upper bound and that this least upper bound is equal to
   one.^[190][10]

Algebraic arguments

   [[191]edit]

   Simple algebraic illustrations of equality are a subject of pedagogical
   discussion and critique. [192]Byers (2007) discusses the argument that,
   in elementary school, one is taught that
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
   displaystyle="false" scriptlevel="0"> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD">
   <mfrac> <mn>1</mn> <mn>3</mn> </mfrac> </mrow> </mstyle> </mrow>
   <annotation encoding="application/x-tex">{\textstyle {\frac
   {1}{3}}}</annotation> </semantics> :MATH]
   {\textstyle {\frac {1}{3}}} = 0.333..., so, ignoring all essential
   subtleties, "multiplying" this identity by 3 gives 1 = 0.999.... He
   further says that this argument is unconvincing, because of an
   unresolved ambiguity over the meaning of the [193]equals sign; a
   student might think, "It surely does not mean that the number 1 is
   identical to that which is meant by the notation 0.999...‍." Most
   undergraduate mathematics majors encountered by Byers feel that while
   0.999... is "very close" to 1 on the strength of this argument, with
   some even saying that it is "infinitely close", they are not ready to
   say that it is equal to 1.^[194][11] [195]Richman (1999) discusses how
   "this argument gets its force from the fact that most people have been
   indoctrinated to accept the first equation without thinking", but also
   suggests that the argument may lead skeptics to question this
   assumption.^[196][12]

   Students who did not accept the first argument sometimes accept the
   second argument, but, in Byers's opinion, still have not resolved the
   ambiguity, and therefore do not understand the representation of
   infinite decimals. [197]Peressini & Peressini (2007), presenting the
   same argument, also state that it does not explain the equality,
   indicating that such an explanation would likely involve concepts of
   infinity and [198]completeness.^[199][13] [200]Baldwin & Norton (2012),
   citing [201]Katz & Katz (2010a), also conclude that the treatment of
   the identity based on such arguments as these, without the formal
   concept of a limit, is premature.^[202][14] [203]Cheng (2023) concurs,
   arguing that knowing one can multiply 0.999... by 10 by shifting the
   decimal point presumes an answer to the deeper question of how one
   gives a meaning to the expression 0.999... at all.^[204][15] The same
   argument is also given by [205]Richman (1999), who notes that skeptics
   may question whether

   {\displaystyle x} from both sides.^[207][12] [208]Eisenmann (2008)
   similarly argues that both the multiplication and subtraction which
   removes the infinite decimal require further justification.^[209][16]

Analytic proofs

   [[210]edit]

   [211]Real analysis is the study of the logical underpinnings of
   [212]calculus, including the behavior of sequences and series of real
   numbers.^[213][17] The proofs in this section establish 0.999... = 1
   using techniques familiar from real analysis.

   theme.^[240][24]

Proofs from the construction of the real numbers

   [[249]edit]
   Further information: [250]Construction of the real numbers

   Some approaches explicitly define real numbers to be certain
   [251]structures built upon the rational numbers, using [252]axiomatic
   set theory. The [253]natural numbers {0, 1, 2, 3, ...} begin with 0 and
   continue upwards so that every number has a successor. One can extend
   the natural numbers with their negatives to give all the [254]integers,
   and to further extend to ratios, giving the [255]rational numbers.
   These number systems are accompanied by the arithmetic of addition,
   subtraction, multiplication, and division.^[256][28]^[257][29] More
   subtly, they include [258]ordering, so that one number can be compared
   to another and found to be less than, greater than, or equal to another
   number.^[259][30]

   The step from rationals to reals is a major extension. There are at
   least two popular ways to achieve this step, both published in 1872:
   [260]Dedekind cuts and [261]Cauchy sequences. Proofs that 0.999... = 1
   that directly uses these constructions are not found in textbooks on
   real analysis, where the modern trend for the last few decades has been
   to use an axiomatic analysis. Even when a construction is offered, it
   is usually applied toward proving the axioms of the real numbers, which
   then support the above proofs. However, several authors express the
   idea that starting with a construction is more logically appropriate,
   and the resulting proofs are more self-contained.^[262][c]

Infinite decimal representation

   [[290]edit]

   Commonly in [291]secondary schools' mathematics education, the real
   numbers are constructed by defining a number using an integer followed
   by a [292]radix point and an infinite sequence written out as a string
   to represent the [293]fractional part of any given real number. In this
   construction, the set of any combination of an integer and digits after
   the decimal point (or radix point in non-base 10 systems) is the set of
   real numbers. This construction can be rigorously shown to satisfy all
   of the [294]real axioms after defining an [295]equivalence relation
   over the set that defines 1 =[eq] 0.999... as well as for any other
   nonzero decimals with only finitely many nonzero terms in the decimal
   string with its trailing 9s version. In other words, the equality
   0.999... = 1 holding true is a necessary condition for strings of
   digits to behave as real numbers should.^[296][38]^[297][39]

Dense order

   [[298]edit]
   Further information: [299]Dense order

   One of the notions that can resolve the issue is the requirement that
   real numbers be densely ordered. Dense ordering implies that if there
   is no new element strictly between two elements of the set, the two
   elements must be considered equal. Therefore, if 0.99999... were to be
   different from 1, there would have to be another real number in between
   them but there is none: a single digit cannot be changed in either of
   the two to obtain such a number.^[300][40]

Generalizations

   [[301]edit]

   The result that 0.999... = 1 generalizes readily in two ways. First,
   every nonzero number with a finite decimal notation (equivalently,
   endless trailing 0s) has a counterpart with trailing 9s. For example,
   0.24999... equals 0.25, exactly as in the special case considered.
   These numbers are exactly the decimal fractions, and they are
   [302]dense.^[303][41]^[304][9]

   Second, a comparable theorem applies in each radix or [305]base. For
   example, in base 2 (the [306]binary numeral system) 0.111... equals 1,
   and in base 3 (the [307]ternary numeral system) 0.222... equals 1. In
   general, any terminating base
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
   displaystyle="true" scriptlevel="0"> <mi>b</mi> </mstyle> </mrow>
   <annotation encoding="application/x-tex">{\displaystyle b}</annotation>
   </semantics> :MATH]
   {\displaystyle b} expression has a counterpart with repeated trailing
   digits equal to
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
   displaystyle="true" scriptlevel="0"> <mi>b</mi> </mstyle> </mrow>
   <annotation encoding="application/x-tex">{\displaystyle b}</annotation>
   </semantics> :MATH]
   {\displaystyle b} − 1. Textbooks of real analysis are likely to skip
   the example of 0.999... and present one or both of these
   generalizations from the start.^[308][42]

   Alternative representations of 1 also occur in non-integer bases. For
   example, in the [309]golden ratio base, the two standard
   representations are 1.000... and 0.101010..., and there are infinitely
   many more representations that include adjacent 1s. Generally, for
   [310]almost all
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
   displaystyle="true" scriptlevel="0"> <mi>q</mi> </mstyle> </mrow>
   <annotation encoding="application/x-tex">{\displaystyle q}</annotation>
   </semantics> :MATH]
   {\displaystyle q} between 1 and 2, there are uncountably many base-
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
   displaystyle="true" scriptlevel="0"> <mi>q</mi> </mstyle> </mrow>
   <annotation encoding="application/x-tex">{\displaystyle q}</annotation>
   </semantics> :MATH]
   {\displaystyle q} expansions of 1. In contrast, there are still
   uncountably many ⁠
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
   displaystyle="true" scriptlevel="0"> <mi>q</mi> </mstyle> </mrow>
   <annotation encoding="application/x-tex">{\displaystyle q}</annotation>
   </semantics> :MATH]
   {\displaystyle q} ⁠, including all natural numbers greater than 1, for
   which there is only one base-
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
   displaystyle="true" scriptlevel="0"> <mi>q</mi> </mstyle> </mrow>
   <annotation encoding="application/x-tex">{\displaystyle q}</annotation>
   </semantics> :MATH]
   {\displaystyle q} expansion of 1, other than the trivial 1.000...‍.
   This result was first obtained by [311]Paul Erdős, Miklos Horváth, and
   István Joó around 1990. In 1998 Vilmos Komornik and [312]Paola Loreti
   determined the smallest such base, the [313]Komornik–Loreti constant
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
   displaystyle="true" scriptlevel="0"> <mi>q</mi> </mstyle> </mrow>
   <annotation encoding="application/x-tex">{\displaystyle q}</annotation>
   </semantics> :MATH]
   {\displaystyle q} = 1.787231650...‍. In this base, 1 =
   0.11010011001011010010110011010011...; the digits are given by the
   [314]Thue–Morse sequence, which does not repeat.^[315][43]

   A more far-reaching generalization addresses [316]the most general
   positional numeral systems. They too have multiple representations, and
   in some sense, the difficulties are even worse. For example:^[317][44]
     * In the [318]balanced ternary system,
       [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
       displaystyle="false" scriptlevel="0"> <mrow

       </mrow> </mstyle> </mrow> <annotation
       encoding="application/x-tex">{\textstyle {\frac
       {1}{2}}}</annotation> </semantics> :MATH]
       {\textstyle {\frac {1}{2}}} = 0.111... = 1.111...‍.
     * In the reverse [319]factorial number system (using bases 2!, 3!,
       4!, ... for positions after the decimal point), 1 = 1.000... =
       0.1234...‍.

   [320]Petkovšek (1990) has proven that for any positional system that
   names all the real numbers, the set of reals with multiple
   representations is always dense. He calls the proof "an instructive
   exercise in elementary [321]point-set topology"; it involves viewing
   sets of positional values as [322]Stone spaces and noticing that their
   real representations are given by [323]continuous functions.^[324][45]

   may be closer to Cantor's original argument uses base 2, and by turning
   base-3 expansions into base-2 expansions, one can prove the
   uncountability of the Cantor set as well.^[347][50]

Skepticism in education

   [[348]edit]

   Students of mathematics often reject the equality of 0.999... and 1,
   for reasons ranging from their disparate appearance to deep misgivings
   over the [349]limit concept and disagreements over the nature of
   [350]infinitesimals. There are many common contributing factors to the
   confusion:
     * Students are often "mentally committed to the notion that a number
       can be represented in one and only one way by a decimal". Seeing
       two manifestly different decimals representing the same number
       appears to be a [351]paradox, which is amplified by the appearance
       of the seemingly well-understood number 1.^[352][g]
     * Some students interpret "0.999..." (or similar notation) as a large
       but finite string of 9s, possibly with a variable, unspecified
       length. If they accept an infinite string of nines, they may still
       expect a last 9 "at infinity".^[353][51]
     * Intuition and ambiguous teaching lead students to think of the
       limit of a sequence as a kind of infinite process rather than a
       fixed value since a sequence need not reach its limit. Where
       students accept the difference between a sequence of numbers and
       its limit, they might read "0.999..." as meaning the sequence
       rather than its limit.^[354][52]

   These ideas are mistaken in the context of the standard real numbers,
   although some may be valid in other number systems, either invented for
   their general mathematical utility or as instructive
   [355]counterexamples to better understand 0.999...; see [356]§ In
   alternative number systems below.

   Many of these explanations were found by [357]David Tall, who has
   studied characteristics of teaching and cognition that lead to some of
   the misunderstandings he has encountered with his college students.
   Interviewing his students to determine why the vast majority initially
   rejected the equality, he found that "students continued to conceive of
   0.999... as a sequence of numbers getting closer and closer to 1 and
   not a fixed value, because 'you haven't specified how many places there
   are' or 'it is the nearest possible decimal below 1'".^[358][23]

   The elementary argument of multiplying 0.333... =
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
   displaystyle="false" scriptlevel="0"> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD">

   {1}{3}}}</annotation> </semantics> :MATH]
   {\textstyle {\frac {1}{3}}} by 3 can convince reluctant students that
   0.999... = 1. Still, when confronted with the conflict between their
   belief in the first equation and their disbelief in the second, some
   students either begin to disbelieve the first equation or simply become
   frustrated.^[359][53] Nor are more sophisticated methods foolproof:
   students who are fully capable of applying rigorous definitions may
   still fall back on intuitive images when they are surprised by a result
   in advanced mathematics, including 0.999...‍. For example, one real
   analysis student was able to prove that 0.333... =
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle

   <annotation encoding="application/x-tex">{\textstyle {\frac
   {1}{3}}}</annotation> </semantics> :MATH]
   {\textstyle {\frac {1}{3}}} using a [360]supremum definition but then
   insisted that 0.999... < 1 based on her earlier understanding of
   [361]long division.^[362][54] Others still can prove that
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
   displaystyle="false" scriptlevel="0"> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD">

   <annotation encoding="application/x-tex">{\textstyle {\frac
   {1}{3}}}</annotation> </semantics> :MATH]
   {\textstyle {\frac {1}{3}}} = 0.333..., but, upon being confronted by
   the [363]fractional proof, insist that "logic" supersedes the
   mathematical calculations.

   [364]Mazur (2005) tells the tale of an otherwise brilliant calculus
   student of his who "challenged almost everything I said in class but
   never questioned his calculator", and who had come to believe that nine
   digits are all one needs to do mathematics, including calculating the
   square root of 23. The student remained uncomfortable with a limiting
   argument that 9.99... = 10, calling it a "wildly imagined infinite
   growing process".^[365][55]

   As part of the [366]APOS Theory of mathematical learning, [367]Dubinsky
   et al. (2005) propose that students who conceive of 0.999... as a
   finite, indeterminate string with an infinitely small distance from 1
   have "not yet constructed a complete process conception of the infinite
   decimal". Other students who have a complete process conception of
   0.999... may not yet be able to "encapsulate" that process into an
   "object conception", like the object conception they have of 1, and so
   they view the process 0.999... and the object 1 as incompatible. They
   also link this mental ability of encapsulation to viewing
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
   displaystyle="false" scriptlevel="0"> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD">

   <annotation encoding="application/x-tex">{\textstyle {\frac
   {1}{3}}}</annotation> </semantics> :MATH]
   {\textstyle {\frac {1}{3}}} as a number in its own right and to dealing
   with the set of natural numbers as a whole.^[368][56]

   The fact that 0.999... is equal to 1 has been compared to [385]Zeno's
   paradox of the runner.^[386][62] The runner paradox can be
   mathematically modeled and then, like 0.999..., resolved using a
   geometric series. However, it is not clear whether this mathematical
   treatment addresses the underlying metaphysical issues Zeno was
   exploring.^[387][63]

In alternative number systems

   [[388]edit]

   Although the real numbers form an extremely useful [389]number system,
   the decision to interpret the notation "0.999..." as naming a real
   number is ultimately a convention, and [390]Timothy Gowers argues in
   Mathematics: A Very Short Introduction that the resulting identity
   0.999... = 1 is a convention as well:

     However, it is by no means an arbitrary convention, because not
     adopting it forces one either to invent strange new objects or to
     abandon some of the familiar rules of arithmetic.^[391][64]

Infinitesimals

   [[392]edit]

   Main article: [393]Infinitesimal

   Some proofs that 0.999... = 1 rely on the [394]Archimedean property of
   the real numbers: that there are no nonzero [395]infinitesimals.
   Specifically, the difference 1 − 0.999... must be smaller than any
   positive rational number, so it must be an infinitesimal; but since the
   reals do not contain nonzero infinitesimals, the difference is zero,
   and therefore the two values are the same.

   However, there are mathematically coherent ordered [396]algebraic
   structures, including various alternatives to the real numbers, which
   are non-Archimedean. [397]Non-standard analysis provides a number
   system with a full array of infinitesimals (and their

p-adic numbers

   [[435]edit]

   Main article: [436]p-adic number

   When asked about 0.999..., novices often believe there should be a
   "final 9", believing 1 − 0.999... to be a positive number which they
   write as "0.000...1". Whether or not that makes sense, the intuitive
   goal is clear: adding a 1 to the final 9 in 0.999... would carry all
   the 9s into 0s and leave a 1 in the ones place. Among other reasons,
   this idea fails because there is no "final 9" in 0.999...‍.^[437][71]
   However, there is a system that contains an infinite string of 9s
   including a last 9.

   including the sequence (3, 33, 333, ...) converging to −1. The 10-adic
   analogue is ...999 = −1.

   ends to represent the real numbers and eventually repeating right ends

          of real numbers in any base". (pp. 503–507)

     [939]Pugh, Charles Chapman (2002). [940]Real Mathematical Analysis.
   Springer-Verlag. [941]ISBN [942]978-0-387-95297-0.

          While assuming familiarity with the rational numbers, Pugh
          introduces [943]Dedekind cuts as soon as possible, saying of the
          axiomatic treatment, "This is something of a fraud, considering
          that the entire structure of analysis is built on the real
          number system." (p. 10) After proving the least upper bound
          property and some allied facts, cuts are not used in the rest of
          the book.

     [965]Rudin, Walter (1976) [1953]. [966]Principles of Mathematical
   Analysis (3e ed.). McGraw-Hill. [967]ISBN [968]978-0-07-054235-8.

          A textbook for an advanced undergraduate course. "Experience has
          convinced me that it is pedagogically unsound (though logically
          correct) to start off with the construction of the real numbers
          from the rational ones. At the beginning, most students simply
          fail to appreciate the need for doing this. Accordingly, the
          real number system is introduced as an ordered field with the
          least-upper-bound property, and a few interesting applications
          of this property are quickly made. However, Dedekind's
          construction is not omitted. It is now in an Appendix to Chapter
          1, where it may be studied and enjoyed whenever the time is
          ripe." (p. ix)
