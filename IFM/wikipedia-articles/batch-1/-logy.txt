   -logy is a [86]suffix in the English language, used with words
   originally adapted from [87]Ancient Greek ending in [88]-λογία
   (-logía).^[89][1] The earliest English examples were anglicizations of
   the French [90]-logie, which was in turn inherited from the [91]Latin
   [92]-logia.^[93][2] The suffix became productive in English from the
   18th century, allowing the formation of new terms with no Latin or
   Greek precedent.

   The English suffix has two separate main senses, reflecting two sources
   of the -λογία suffix in Greek:^[94][3]
     * a combining form used in the names of school or bodies of
       knowledge, e.g., [95]theology (loaned from Latin in the [96]14th
       century) or [97]sociology. In words of the type [98]theology, the
       suffix is derived originally from -λογ- (-log-) (a variant of
       -λεγ-, -leg-), from the [99]Greek verb λέγειν (legein, 'to
       speak').^[100][4] The suffix has the sense of "the character or
       deportment of one who speaks or treats of [a certain subject]", or
       more succinctly, "the study of [a certain subject]".^[101][5] (The
       Ancient Greek noun λόγος lógos mentioned below can also be
       translated, among other things, as "subject matter".^[102][6])
     * the root word nouns that refer to kinds of speech, writing or
       collections of writing, e.g., [103]eulogy or [104]trilogy. In words
       of this type, the "-logy" element is derived from the Greek noun
       λόγος ([105]logos, 'speech', 'account', 'story').^[106][4] The
       suffix has the sense of "[a certain kind of] speaking or
       writing".^[107][7]

   [108]Philology is an exception: while its meaning is closer to the
   first sense, the etymology of the word is similar to the second
   sense.^[109][8]

-logy versus -ology

   [[110]edit]

   In English names for fields of study, the suffix -logy is most
   frequently found preceded by the euphonic connective vowel o so that
   the word ends in -ology.^[111][9] In these Greek words, the [112]root
   is always a noun and -o- is the [113]combining vowel for all
   declensions of Greek nouns. However, when new names for fields of study
   are coined in modern English, the formations ending in -logy almost
   always add an -o-, except when the root word ends in an "l" or a vowel,
   as in these exceptions:^[114][10] [115]analogy, dekalogy, disanalogy,
   [116]genealogy, [117]genethlialogy, [118]hexalogy; herbalogy (a variant
   of [119]herbology), [120]mammalogy, [121]mineralogy, [122]paralogy,
   petralogy (a variant of [123]petrology); [124]elogy; [125]heptalogy;
   [126]antilogy, [127]festilogy; [128]trilogy, [129]tetralogy,
   [130]pentalogy; [131]palillogy, [132]pyroballogy; [133]dyslogy;
   [134]eulogy; and [135]brachylogy.^[136][7] Linguists sometimes jokingly
   refer to [137]haplology as haplogy (subjecting the word haplology to
   the process of haplology itself).

Additional usage as a suffix

   [[138]edit]

   Per [139]metonymy, words ending in -logy are sometimes used to describe
   a subject rather than the study of it (e.g., [140]technology). This
   usage is particularly widespread in medicine; for example,
   [141]pathology is often used simply to refer to "the disease" itself
   (e.g., "We haven't found the pathology yet") rather than "the study of
   a disease".

   Books, journals, and treatises about a subject also often bear the name
   of this subject (e.g., the scientific journal [142]Ecology).

   When appended to other English words, the suffix can also be used
   humorously to create [143]nonce words (e.g., [144]beerology as "the
   study of beer"). As with other [145]classical compounds, adding the
   suffix to an initial word-stem derived from Greek or [146]Latin may be
   used to lend grandeur or the impression of scientific rigor to humble
   pursuits, as in cosmetology ("the study of beauty treatment") or
   cynology ("the study of dog training").

Compound series of works of art

   [[147]edit]

   The -logy or -ology suffix is commonly used to indicate finite series
   of art works like books or movies. For paintings, the "tych" suffix is
   more common (e.g. [148]diptych, [149]triptych). Examples include:
     * [150]Trilogy for three works
     * [151]Tetralogy for four works
     * [152]Pentalogy for five works
     * [153]Hexalogy for six works
     * [154]Heptalogy for seven works

   Further terms like duology (two, mostly in [155]genre fiction)
   quadrilogy (four) and octalogy (eight) have been coined but are rarely
   used: for a series of 10, sometimes "decalog" is used (e.g. in the
   [156]Virgin Decalog) instead of "decalogy".
