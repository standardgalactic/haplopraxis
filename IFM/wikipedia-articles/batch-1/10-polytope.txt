   In ten-dimensional [101]geometry, a 10-polytope is a 10-dimensional
   [102]polytope whose boundary consists of [103]9-polytope [104]facets,
   exactly two such facets meeting at each [105]8-polytope [106]ridge.

   A uniform 10-polytope is one which is [107]vertex-transitive, and
   constructed from [108]uniform [109]facets.

Regular 10-polytopes

   [[110]edit]

   Regular 10-polytopes can be represented by the [111]Schläfli symbol
   {p,q,r,s,t,u,v,w,x}, with x {p,q,r,s,t,u,v,w} 9-polytope [112]facets
   around each [113]peak.

   There are exactly three such [114]convex regular 10-polytopes:
    1. {3,3,3,3,3,3,3,3,3} - [115]10-simplex
    2. {4,3,3,3,3,3,3,3,3} - [116]10-cube
    3. {3,3,3,3,3,3,3,3,4} - [117]10-orthoplex

   There are no nonconvex regular 10-polytopes.

Euler characteristic

   [[118]edit]

   The topology of any given 10-polytope is defined by its [119]Betti
   numbers and [120]torsion coefficients.^[121][1]

   The value of the [122]Euler characteristic used to characterise
   polyhedra does not generalize usefully to higher dimensions, and is
   zero for all 10-polytopes, whatever their underlying topology. This
   inadequacy of the Euler characteristic to reliably distinguish between
   different topologies in higher dimensions led to the discovery of the
   more sophisticated Betti numbers.^[123][1]

   Similarly, the notion of orientability of a polyhedron is insufficient
   to characterise the surface twistings of toroidal polytopes, and this
   led to the use of torsion coefficients.^[124][1]

Regular and uniform hyperbolic honeycombs

   [[240]edit]

   There are no compact hyperbolic Coxeter groups of rank 10, groups that
   can generate honeycombs with all finite facets, and a finite
   [241]vertex figure. However, there are [242]3 paracompact hyperbolic
   Coxeter groups of rank 9, each generating uniform honeycombs in 9-space
