   [120]Category

   The dollar sign, also known as the peso sign, is a [121]currency symbol
   consisting of a [122]capital ⟨[123]S⟩ crossed with one or two vertical
   strokes ($ or [124]Dollar sign with two vertical lines
   depending on [125]typeface), used to indicate the unit of various
   [126]currencies around the world, including most currencies denominated
   "[127]dollar" or "[128]peso". The explicitly double-barred [129]Dollar
   sign with two vertical lines sign is called [130]cifrão in the
   [131]Portuguese language.

   The sign is also used in several compound currency symbols, such as the
   [132]Brazilian real (R$), the [133]Nicaraguan córdoba (C$) and the
   [134]United States dollar (US$): in local use, the nationality prefix
   is usually omitted. In countries that have other [135]currency symbols,
   the US dollar is often assumed and the "US" prefix omitted.

   The one- and two-stroke versions are often considered mere stylistic
   ([136]typeface) variants, although in some places and epochs one of
   them may have been specifically assigned, by law or custom, to a
   specific currency. The [137]Unicode computer encoding standard defines
   a single code for both.

   In most [138]English-speaking countries that use that symbol, it is
   placed to the left of the amount specified, e.g. "$1", read as "one
   dollar".

History

   [[139]edit]

   The symbol appears in business correspondence in the 1770s from
   [140]Spanish America, the early independent U.S., British America and
   Britain, referring to the Spanish American peso,^[141][1]^[142][2] also
   known as "[143]Spanish dollar" or "piece of eight" in British America.
   Those coins provided the model for the currency that the United States
   adopted in 1792, and for the larger coins of the new Spanish American
   republics, such as the [144]Mexican peso, [145]Argentine peso,
   [146]Peruvian real, and [147]Bolivian sol coins.

   With the [148]Coinage Act of 1792, the United States Congress created
   the U.S. dollar, defining it to have "the value of a Spanish milled
   dollar as the same is now current"^[149][3]^[150][4] but a variety of
   foreign coins were deemed to be [151]legal tender until the
   [152]Coinage Act of 1857 ended this status.^[153][5]

   The earliest U.S. dollar coins did not have any dollar symbol. The
   first occurrence in print is claimed to be from 1790s, by a
   Philadelphia printer [154]Archibald Binny, creator of the
   [155]Monticello typeface.^[156][6] The $1 [157]United States Note
   issued by the United States in 1869 included a large symbol consisting
   of a "U" with the right bar overlapping an "S" like a single-bar dollar
   sign, as well as a very small double-stroke dollar sign in the legal
   warning against forgery.^[158][7]

Earlier history of the symbol

   [[159]edit]

   [160][220px-Potosi_Real.jpg] A piece of eight from the [161]Potosí
   mint, showing the Pillars of Hercules with "S" ribbons, and two "PTSI"
   monograms at about 4 and 8 o'clock around the edge

   It is still uncertain, however, how the dollar sign came to represent
   the Spanish American peso. There are currently several competing
   hypotheses:
   [162][220px-Columnas_Plus_Ultra.png] Iconic representation of the
   Pillars of Hercules with ribbon Plus Ultra ("further beyond"), in
   [163]Seville (16th century).
     * The most widely accepted theory holds that the sign grew out of the
       Spanish and Spanish American [164]scribal abbreviation "p^s" for
       pesos. A study of late 18th- and early 19th-century
       [165]manuscripts shows that the s gradually came to be written over
       the p, developing into a close equivalent to the "$"
       mark.^[166][8]^[167][9]^[168][10]^[169][11]^[170][12] [171]Oliver
       Pollock, a wealthy [172]Irish trader and early supporter of the
       American Revolution, used the abbreviation "ps", sometimes run
       together in a way that almost exactly resembled the dollar sign, in
       a letter dated 1778.^[173][6]^[174][13] There are documents showing
       the common use of the two-stroke version in Portugal already by
       1775.^[175][14]
     * Another hypothesis derives the sign from a depiction of the
       [176]Pillars of Hercules, a classical symbol for two sides of the
       [177]Strait of Gibraltar, with a [178]ribbon wrapped around each
       pillar (or both pillars) in the form of an "S". This device is a
       support element of the [179]Spanish coat of arms, and appeared on
       the most common real de ocho coins circulating at the time in the
       Americas and Europe; namely, those minted at the [180]Potosí mint
       in [181]Bolivia, which operated from 1573 to
       1825.^[182][15]^[183][10] Indeed, one of the names used for Spanish
       dollars in [184]Qing Dynasty China was 雙柱; Shuāngzhù;
       'double-pillar'.^[185][16]

   [186][220px-Philip_V_Coin_silver%2C_8_Reales_Mexico.jpg] The Pillars of
   Hercules wrapped by a cloth band, on an 18th century Spanish
   coin.[187]Dollar symbol evolution Development of the dollar sign,
   according to two hypotheses.
     * A variant of the above theory claims that the sign comes from the
       [188]mark of the mint at [189]Potosí, where a large portion of the
       Spanish Empire's silver was mined. A feature on these coins were
       the letters "P T S I" superimposed. The core of this [190]monogram
       is a (single-stroked) "$" sign.^[191][17]

   [192][220px-Sample_ledger_with_dollar_sign_in_John_Collins%2C_An_Introd
   uction_to_Merchants-Accompts%2C_1686.png] Sample ledger with a sign for
   dollar from John Collins 1686
     * Yet another hypothesis notes that the English word "dollar" for the
       Spanish piece of eight originally came (through [193]Dutch daalder)
       from [194]Joachimsthaler or [195]thaler, a similar large German
       silver coin that was widely used in Europe. It is therefore
       conjectured that the dollar sign derived from a symbol consisting
       of a superimposing S and I or J that was used to denote the German
       silver coin. Such symbol appears in the 1686 edition of An
       Introduction to Merchants' Accounts by [196]John Collins.^[197][18]
       Alternatively, the symbol could have come from a snake and cross
       emblem on the thaler coins.^[198][6]

Less likely theories

   [[199]edit]

   The following theories seem to have been discredited or contradicted by
   documentary evidence:
     * In 1937, historian [200]James Alton James claimed that the symbol
       with two strokes was an adapted design of patriot [201]Robert
       Morris in 1778, in letters written to
       Pollock.^[202][19]^[203][20]^[204][13]
     * In 1939, H. M. Larson suggested that the sign could derive from a
       combination of the Greek character [205]"psi" (ψ) and
       "S".^[206][21]
     * A theory claims that the sign started off as a monogram of "US",
       with a narrow "U" superimposed on the "S"; the bottom part of the
       "U" would have been lost, producing the dollar sign with two
       vertical lines. This theory was mentioned in letters to [207]Notes
       and Queries in 1876.^[208][8] [209]Henry Towne begins his classic
       1886 essay on management with this theory.^[210][22] This was also
       claimed by [211]Ayn Rand in her 1957 novel [212]Atlas Shrugged; in
       her version of the theory the monogram "US" would have been used on
       money bags issued by the [213]United States
       Mint.^[214][23]^[215][6]
     * Authors T. Seijas and J. Frederick noted that the captors of slaves
       in Spanish territories sometimes branded enslaved people with a
       symbol very similar to a one-barred dollar sign. Esclavo is Spanish
       for "slave," and clavo means nail. A dollar sign would then be S +
       clavo.^[216][15]
     * A theory often cited in [217]Portuguese speaking countries is that
       the "S" part of the doubly-stroked sign is a schematic
       representation of the path followed by the [218]Umayyad Caliphate
       general [219]Tariq Ibn Ziyad in his conquest of the [220]Visigoth
       kingdom in 711 [221]CE, and the two strokes represent the Pillars
       of Hercules that he would have crossed along that path. That symbol
       would have been engraved in coins commemorating his victory, and
       then became symbolic of currency in general.^[222][24]

Currencies that use the dollar sign

   [[223]edit]

As symbol of the currency

   [[224]edit]

   The numerous currencies called "[225]dollar" use the dollar sign to
   express money amounts. The sign is also generally used for the many
   currencies called "[226]peso" (except the [227]Philippine peso, which
   uses the symbol "[228]₱"). Within a country the dollar/peso sign may be
   used alone. In other cases, and to avoid ambiguity in international
   usage, it is usually combined with other glyphs, e.g. CA$ or Can$ for
   [229]Canadian dollar. Particularly in professional contexts, the
   unambiguous [230]ISO 4217 three letter code (AUD, MXN, USD, etc.) is
   preferred.

   The dollar sign, alone or in combination with other glyphs, is or was
   used to denote several currencies with other names, including:
     * [231]Brazilian cruzeiro (various currencies, all defunct): ₢$,
       CR[232] Dollar sign with two vertical lines , Cr[233] Dollar sign
       with two vertical lines , NCr[234] Dollar sign with two vertical
       lines , etc.
     * [235]Brazilian real: R$
     * [236]Ethiopian birr (until 1976): E$
     * [237]Macanese pataca: MOP$
     * [238]Nicaraguan córdoba: C$
     * [239]Samoan tālā (a transliteration of the word dollar): $
     * [240]Tongan paʻanga: T$
     * [241]Malaya and British Borneo dollar (1957–1967): $
     * [242]Malaysian ringgit (1967–1997): $, M$, M[243] Dollar sign with
       two vertical lines
     * [244]South Vietnamese đồng (1953–1975): $, [245]Dollar sign with
       two vertical lines

Prefix or suffix

   [[246]edit]

   In the United States, Mexico, Australia, Argentina, Chile, Colombia,
   New Zealand, Hong Kong, Pacific Island nations, and English-speaking
   Canada, the sign is written before the number ("$5"), even though the
   word is written or spoken after it ("five dollars", "cinco pesos"). In
   French-speaking Canada, exceptionally, the dollar symbol usually
   appears after the number,^[247][25] e.g., "5$". (The [248]cent symbol
   is written after the number in most countries that use it, e.g.,
   "5[249]¢".)

Use in the Portuguese Empire

   [[250]edit]

   [251][220px-Car_with_price.jpg] Car for sale in Cape Verde, showing use
   of the cifrão as decimals separator

   In Portugal, Brazil, and other parts of the [252]Portuguese Empire, the
   two-stroke variant of the sign (with the name [253]cifrão (
   Portuguese pronunciation: [254][siˈfɾɐ̃w] ^[255]ⓘ) was used as the
   thousands separator of amounts in the national currency, the [256]real
   (plural "réis", abbreviated "Rs."): 123[257] Dollar sign with two
   vertical lines 500 meant "123500 réis". This usage is attested in 1775,
   but may be older by a century or more.^[258][14] It is always written
   with two vertical lines: [259][15px-Cifr%C3%A3o_symbol.svg.png] . It is
   the official sign of the [260]Cape Verdean escudo ([261]ISO 4217: CVE).

   In 1911, Portugal redefined the national currency as the [262]escudo,
   worth 1000 réis, and divided into 100 centavos; but the cifrão
   continued to be used as the [263]decimal separator,^[264][26] so that
   123[265] Dollar sign with two vertical lines 50 meant 123.50 escudos or
   123 escudos and 50 centavos. This usage ended in 2002 when the country
   switched to the [266]euro. (A similar scheme to use a letter symbol
   instead of a decimal point is used by the [267]RKM code in electrical
   engineering since 1952.)

   [268]Cape Verde, a republic and former Portuguese colony, similarly
   switched from the real to their local [269]escudo and centavos in 1914,
   and retains the cifrão usage as decimals separator as of 2021. Local
   versions of the Portuguese escudo were for a time created also for
   other overseas colonies, including [270]East Timor (1958–1975),
   [271]Portuguese India (1958–1961), [272]Angola (1914–1928 and
   1958–1977), [273]Mozambique (1914–1980), [274]Portuguese Guinea
   (1914–1975), and [275]São Tomé and Príncipe (1914–1977); all using the
   cifrão as decimals separator.^[[276]citation needed]

   [277]Brazil retained the real and the cifrão as thousands separator
   until 1942, when it switched to the [278]Brazilian cruzeiro, with comma
   as the decimals separator. The dollar sign, officially with one stroke
   but often rendered with two, was retained as part of the currency
   symbol "Cr$", so one would write Cr$13,50 for 13 cruzeiros and 50
   centavos.^[279][27]

   The cifrão was formerly used by the [280]Portuguese escudo (ISO: PTE)
   before its replacement by the [281]euro and by the [282]Portuguese
   Timor escudo (ISO: TPE) before its replacement by the [283]Indonesian
   rupiah and the [284]US dollar.^[285][28] In Portuguese and Cape Verdean
   usage, the cifrão is placed as a decimal point between the escudo and
   [286]centavo values.^[287][29] The name originates in the [288]Arabic
   ṣifr (‏[289]صِفْر‎), meaning 'zero'.^[290][30]

   Outside the Portuguese cultural sphere, the [291]South Vietnamese đồng
   before 1975 used a method similar to the cifrão to separate values of
   đồng from its decimal subunit xu. For example,
   7[292] Dollar sign with two vertical lines 50 meant 7 đồng and 50 xu.

One stroke vs. two strokes

   [[293]edit]

   [294][170px-Cifr%C3%A3o_symbol.svg.png] Double-barred dollar or Cifrão
   sign

   See also: [295]Pound sign § Double bar style

   In some places and at some times, the one- and two-stroke variants have
   been used in the same contexts to distinguish between the U.S. dollar
   and other local currency, such as the former [296]Portuguese
   escudo.^[297][26]

   However, such usage is not standardized, and the [298]Unicode
   specification considers the two versions as [299]graphic variants of
   the same symbol—a [300]typeface design choice.^[301][31] Computer and
   typewriter keyboards usually have a single key for that sign, and many
   character encodings (including [302]ASCII and [303]Unicode) reserve a
   single numeric code for it. Indeed, dollar signs in the same digital
   document may be rendered with one or two strokes, if different
   [304]computer fonts are used, but the underlying [305]codepoint U+0024
   (ASCII 36[10]) remains unchanged.

   When a specific variant is not mandated by law or custom, the choice is
   usually a matter of expediency or aesthetic preference. Both versions
   were used in the US in the 18th century. (An 1861 [306]Civil War-era
   advertisement depicts the two-stroked symbol as a snake.^[307][13]) The
   two-stroke version seems to be generally less popular today, though
   used in some "old-style" fonts like [308]Baskerville.

Use in computer software

   [[309]edit]

   Because of its use in early American computer applications such as
   business accounting, the dollar sign is almost universally present in
   computer [310]character sets, and thus has been appropriated for many
   purposes unrelated to money in [311]programming languages and
   [312]command languages.

Encoding

   [[313]edit]

   The dollar sign "$" has Unicode code point U+0024 (inherited from
   [314]ASCII via [315]Latin-1).^[316][31]
     * U+0024 $ DOLLAR SIGN (

   &dollar;)^[317][a]

   There are no separate encodings for one- and two-line variants. The
   choice is typeface-dependent, they are [318]allographs. However, there
   are three other code points that originate from other East Asian
   standards: the Taiwanese [319]small form variant, the CJK
   [320]fullwidth form, and the Japanese [321]emoji. The glyphs for these
   code points are typically larger or smaller than the primary code
   point, but the difference is mostly aesthetic or typographic, and the
   meanings of the symbols are the same.
     * U+FE69 ﹩ SMALL DOLLAR SIGN
     * U+FF04 ＄ FULLWIDTH DOLLAR SIGN
     * U+1F4B2 💲 HEAVY DOLLAR SIGN

   However, for usage as the special character in various computing
   applications (see following sections), U+0024 is typically the only
   code that is recognized.

   Support for the two-line variant varies. As of 2019,^[322][update] the
   [323]Unicode standard considers the distinction between one- and
   two-bar dollar signs a [324]stylistic distinction between fonts, and
   has no separate [325]code point for the cifrão. The symbol is not in
   the October 2019 "pipeline",^[326][34] though it has been requested
   formally.^[327][26]

   Among others, the following fonts display a double-bar dollar sign for
   code point 0024:^[[328]citation needed] regular-weight
   [329]Baskerville, [330]Big Caslon, [331]Bodoni MT, [332]Garamond: ($)

   In [333]LaTeX, with the textcomp package installed, the cifrão ([334]
   Dollar sign with two vertical lines
   ) can be input using the command \textdollaroldstyle. However, because
   of [335]font substitution and the lack of a dedicated code point, the
   author of an electronic document who uses one of these fonts intending
   to represent a cifrão cannot be sure that every reader will see a
   double-bar glyph rather than the single barred version. Because of the
   continued lack of support in Unicode, a single bar dollar sign is
   frequently employed in its place even for official
   purposes.^[336][29]^[337][35] Where there is any risk of
   misunderstanding, the [338]ISO 4217 three-letter acronym is used.

Programming languages

   [[339]edit]

     * In [340]BASIC, $ is appended to a [341]variable name to define that
       variable’s [342]data type as a [343]character string, for example,
       H$="Hello, world!".  In discussion, the variable H$ would be
       referred to as “H string.”
     * $ is prefixed to names to define [344]variables in the [345]PHP
       language and the [346]AutoIt automation script language,
       [347]scalar variables in the [348]Perl language (see [349]sigil
       (computer programming)), and global variables in the [350]Ruby
       language. In Perl programming this includes scalar elements of
       [351]arrays $array[7] and [352]hashes $hash{foo}.
     * In [353]Unix shells, and later in other programming languages, $
       introduces an expression that should be evaluated to yield text.
       Languages that have adopted this convention include [354]Perl,
       [355]JavaScript, [356]C#, [357]Scala, [358]Groovy and [359]Kotlin.
       Other languages, including [360]Java and [361]Python, use it to
       mark the place where the result of an expression elsewhere should
       be inserted into text.
     * $ is used for defining hexadecimal constants in [362]Pascal-like
       languages such as [363]Delphi, and in some variants of
       [364]assembly language, most notably, in the Motorola 6800 and
       68000, and MOS Technology 6502 assembly languages.
     * $ is used in the [365]ALGOL 68 language to delimit [366]transput
       format regions.
     * $ is used in the [367]TeX [368]typesetting language to delimit
       mathematical regions.
     * In many versions of [369]FORTRAN 66, $ could be used as an
       alternative to a quotation mark for delimiting strings.
     * In [370]PL/M, $ can be used to put a visible separation between
       words in compound identifiers. For example, Some$Name refers to the
       same thing as 'SomeName'.
     * In [371]Haskell, $ is used as a function application operator.
     * In an [372]AutoHotkey script, a hotkey declared with $ is not
       triggered by a 'Send' command elsewhere in the script.
     * The [373]jQuery library defines $ as its main symbol, primarily as
       a function that queries a web page for one or more [374]HTML
       elements, but also with other utilities attached to it as
       properties like $.ajax. The [375]Prototype.js library defines it
       similarly for querying.
     * In [376]ASP.NET, the dollar sign used in a tag in the web page
       indicates an expression will follow it. The expression that follows
       is .NET [377]language-agnostic, as it will work with C#,
       [378]VB.NET, or any CLR supported language.
     * In [379]Erlang, the dollar sign precedes character literals. The
       dollar sign as a character can be written $$.
     * In [380]COBOL the $ sign is used in the [381]Picture clause to
       depict a floating currency symbol as the left most character. The
       default symbol is $; however, if the CURRENCY= or CURRENCY SIGN
       clause is specified, many other symbols can be used.
     * In some [382]assembly languages, such as [383]MIPS, the $ sign is
       used to represent registers.
     * In [384]Honeywell 6000 series assembler, the $ sign, when used as
       an address, meant the address of the instruction in which it
       appeared.
     * In [385]CMS-2, the $ sign is used as a statement terminator.
     * In [386]R, the $ sign is used as a subsetting operator.
     * In [387]Q (programming language from Kx Systems), the $ sign is
       used as a casting/padding/enumeration/conditional operator.
     * In [388]Sass, the $ sign is prefixed to define a variable.
     * In [389]Svelte, the $ sign can be used to mark [390]reactive
       statements.

Operating systems

   [[391]edit]

     * In [392]CP/M and subsequently in all versions of [393]DOS
       ([394]86-DOS, [395]MS-DOS, [396]PC DOS, more) and derivatives, $
       marks the end of text displayed with system function 9. CP/M
       developer [397]Gary Kildall never explained the choice and once
       pointedly remarked that he knew the reason while [398]Bill Gates
       did not.^[399][36] Prior uses of $ for the "end of line" or "end of
       text" include [400]JOVIAL, [401]CMS-2, the [402]QED editor and
       [403]DECsystem-10 (a known influence on CP/M), which displayed the
       character to confirm that the user pressed the [404]escape key to
       complete a line of input.
     * In [405]Microsoft Windows, $ is appended to the share name to hide
       a shared folder or resource. For example, \\server\share will be
       visible to other computers on a network, while \\server\share$ will
       be accessible only by explicit reference. Hiding a shared folder or
       resource will not alter its access permissions but may render it
       inaccessible to programs or other functions which rely on its
       visibility. Most [406]administrative shares are hidden in this way.
     * In the [407]LDAP directory access protocol, $ is used as a line
       separator in various standard entry attributes such as
       postalAddress.
     * In the [408]UNIVAC EXEC 8 operating system, $ means "system". It is
       appended to entities such as the names of system files, the
       "sender" name in messages sent by the operator, and the default
       names of system-created files (like compiler output) when no
       specific name is specified (e.g., TPF$, NAME$, etc.)
     * In [409]RISC OS, $ is used in system variables to separate the
       application name from the variables specific to that application.
       For example Draw$Dir specifies the directory where the !Draw
       application is located. It is also used to refer to the [410]root
       directory of a [411]file system.

Applications

   [[412]edit]

     * [413]Microsoft Excel^[414][37] and other spreadsheet software use
       the dollar sign ($) to denote a fixed row, fixed column reference,
       or an absolute cell reference.
     * The dollar sign introduces a subfield delimiter in [415]computer
       coding of library catalog records.
     * $ matches the end of a line or string in [416]sed, [417]grep, and
       [418]POSIX and [419]Perl [420]regular expressions, as well as the
       end of a line or the file in text editors [421]ed, [422]ex,
       [423]vi, [424]pico, and derivatives.

Other uses

   [[425]edit]

   The symbol is sometimes used derisively, in place of the letter S, to
   indicate greed or excess money such as in "[426]Micro$oft",
   "[427]Di$ney", "[428]Chel$ea" and "[429]GW$"; or supposed overt
   Americanisation as in "[430]$ky". The dollar sign is also used
   intentionally to stylize names such as [431]A$AP Rocky, [432]Ke$ha, and
   [433]Ty Dolla $ign or words such as ¥€$. In 1872, [434]Ambrose Bierce
   referred to California governor [435]Leland Stanford as $tealand
   Landford.^[436][38]

   In [437]Scrabble notation, a dollar sign is placed after a word to
   indicate that it is valid according to the [438]North American word
   lists, but not according to the British word lists.^[439][39]

   A dollar symbol is used as [440]unit of reactivity for a nuclear
   reactor, 0 [441]$ being the threshold of slow criticality, meaning a
   steady reaction rate, while 1 $ is the threshold of [442]prompt
   criticality, which means a nuclear excursion or
   explosion.^[443][40]^[444][41]

   In the 1993 version of the [445]Turkmen Latin alphabet $ was used as a
   transliteration of the Cyrillic letter Ш, in 1999 was replaced by the
   letter Ş.
