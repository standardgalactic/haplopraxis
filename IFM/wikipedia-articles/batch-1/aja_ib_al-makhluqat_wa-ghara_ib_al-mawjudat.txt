   Seminal work on cosmography

   CAPTION: The Wonders of Creatures

   [73][220px-Illustration_of_the_moon_in_Al-qazwini%27s_%22The_Wonders_of
   _Creation%22.jpg]
   An illustration depicting the moon in al-Qazwini's work, The Wonders of
   Creation. Copy was made in 1537/944, probably in western India.^[74][1]
   Author [75]Zakariya al-Qazwini
   Original title عجائب المخلوقات وغرائب الموجودات
   Language [76]Arabic
   Genre [77]Cosmography
   Published 1203
   Publication place Persia

   Aja'ib al-Makhluqat wa Ghara'ib al-Mawjudat ([78]Arabic: عجائب
   المخلوقات وغرائب الموجودات) or The Wonders of Creatures and the Marvels
   of Creation is an important work of [79]paradoxography and
   [80]cosmography by [81]Zakariya al-Qazwini, who was born in [82]Qazwin
   in 1203 shortly before the [83]Mongol invasion of the Khwarazmian
   Empire.

Background to the work

   [[84]edit]

   Qazwini's Aja'ib al-Makhluqat was criticized for being less than
   original. Substantial parts of his work are derivative of [85]Yaqut
   al-Hamawi's [86]Mu'jam al-Buldan.^[87][2]

   Qazwini mentions fifty names as his sources, the most important of whom
   are old geographers and historians such as [88]Istakhri, [89]Ahmad ibn
   Fadlan, [90]al-Masudi, [91]Ibn Hawqal, [92]al-Biruni, [93]Ibn al-Athir,
   [94]al-Maqdisi, and al-Razi. Even though Qazwini's work is a
   compilation of known and unknown sources, it influenced later works of
   [95]Islamic cosmology and [96]Islamic geography through its style and
   language. Qazwini's cosmography is not pure science but was intended to
   entertain its readers by enriching scientific explanations with stories
   and poetry.

Framework

   [[97]edit]
   [98][220px-Manuscript_qazwin.jpg] A manuscript of the treatise copied
   in the 14th century

   Qazwini's cosmography consists of two parts, the first part is
   celestial, dealing with the [99]spheres of the [100]heaven with its
   inhabitants, the [101]angels, and chronology. [102]Astronomical
   knowledge of that time is compiled together with [103]astrological
   ideas.^[104][2]

   The second part discusses the terrestrial: the [105]four elements, the
   seven climes, seas and rivers, a sort of [106]bestiary on the animal
   kingdom (including mankind and the jinns), the plants, and
   minerals.^[107][2] He discusses here humanity and the faculties of his
   [108]soul, his character, weaknesses and illnesses.

   The cosmography of Ahmad al-Tusi (Aḥmad al-Ṭūsī^[109][a]) is very
   similar and bears the same title;^[110][4] though the latter
   characterized by the concept of the [111]tawhid (unity of God) and the
   unity of creation.^[[112]citation needed].

Celestial cosmography

   [[113]edit]
   [114][280px-Al_Qazwini_Cosmology.jpg] The fish (Bahamut) carries on its
   back the giant bull (Kuyuta), and on the green hyacinth slab stands an
   earth-bearing angel.^[115][5]
   —Surüri's Turkish translation of al-Qazwini. Topkapi Palace Museum,
   Istanbul, MSSA A 3632, folio 131a^[116][7]

   Qazwini says that the earth was swinging in all directions until God
   created an angel to bear it on his shoulders and steady it with his
   hands. A green [117]jacinth slab was placed underneath the angel, the
   slab borne by a gigantic bull [118]Kujata,^[119][b] which in turn
   rested on the giant fish
   [120]Bahamut.^[121][8]^[122][9]^[123][10]^[124][c]

   Qazwini's cosmography above has been compared to a similar entry in
   [125]Yaqut al-Hamawi's [126]Mu'jam al-Buldan^[127][14] and [128]ibn
   al-Wardi's Kharīdat al-'Ajā'ib, with minor differences noted.^[129][8]

Time

   [[130]edit]

   When discussing time, Qazwini makes the parallel comparison of the
   [131]Islamic, [132]Roman and [133]Iranian calendars. Thus he links the
   days of the week to the sacred history of [134]Judaism,
   [135]Christianity, and [136]Islam, e.g. the holy days Friday (the day
   of [137]congregational prayer in Islam), Saturday (the [138]Shabbat in
   [139]Judaism) and Sunday (the day of rest in [140]Christianity) and how
   they came to be regarded as [141]holy. The days are also linked to
   lawful and unlawful things and acts.^[[142]citation needed]

Angelology

   [[143]edit]
   [144][220px-Arabic-manuscript.jpg] An illustration from the manuscript
   depicting archangel [145]Gabriel. Egypt/Syria c. 1375–1425 CE

   Qazwini shows that God created many things that are unknown to the
   people (Quran 16:8), and a fundamental part of this, with central
   importance, is God's [146]Throne, [147]His footstool are the angels and
   the [148]jinn. For some [149]Muslims, the footstool is the eighth and
   the Throne the ninth sphere. Furthermore, the [150]Throne of God is the
   [151]qibla for inhabitants of the [152]celestial spheres like the
   [153]Kaaba is for people on earth.

   The angels inhabit the celestial spheres. The angels are good, perfect
   beings without negative feelings or passion; they are obedient, and
   most importantly, they keep the order of the creation and govern
   everything on earth; the jinn and devils are flawed and imperfect
   creatures who possess passion and wrath and are disobedient. Qazwini's
   work contains moreover [154]angelology that has roots partly in the
   [155]Quran and [156]hadith.

   There are two types of angels in the Quran, the one being the guards of
   [157]hell (96:18) and angels that are nearest to God (4:170, 83:21).
   Qazwini also mentions the angels who carry the Throne of God (the idea
   goes back to the [158]Jahiliyya): they are four in number in the form
   of a man, bull, eagle and lion. On the [159]Day of Resurrection, the
   Throne will be carried by "eight" ([160]Quran, [161]Ḥāqqa 69:17), and
   this traditionally refers to eight angels. Next to these is the angel
   ar-Ruh or the Spirit, who is first in order and the greatest. His
   breath quickens the creatures, and he knows the order of the spheres,
   planets, elements, [162]minerals, etc. He is the one who decides the
   movement and stillness of things by the will of God. This angel is
   followed by [163]Israfil; he transmits the orders of God and blows the
   horn. He is not mentioned in the Quran but in hadith and linked to the
   Day of Resurrection. Israfil carries [164]the tablet (lawh) and the pen
   (qalam). Whether the abovementioned angels or Gabriel, [165]Michael or
   others, all of them have a role in keeping the order of the creation.
   It is also believed that angels have about seventy wings each.

   God then sent angels to inhabit the earth. One sent in exile was young
   [166]Azazil, educated by the angels. He acquired their knowledge and
   became like them and even their leader. He fell into disgrace because
   he disobeyed God; he would not prostrate himself before Adam as the
   vicegerent of God on earth. The idea of Azazil comes from Judaism and
   is mentioned in the Quran ([167]Baqara 2:32, etc.) as [168]Iblis. In
   [169]folk Islam, Iblis is believed to be present in baths, bazaars,
   crossroads, and intoxicating drinks and is associated with flutes,
   poetry, tattoos, lies and illnesses.

   God also persecuted and imprisoned many of the jinn and exiled them.
   Jinn and [170]ghouls are then considered terrestrial beings, occupying
   a place between animals and humanity, and discussed in the second part
   of Al-qazwini's work.^[171][2]

Terrestrial cosmography

   [[172]edit]

   The earth, being part of the lower spheres, brings forth minerals,
   plants, and living creatures such as animals and humans. In Qazwini's
   classification, there are seven types of living creatures: man, jinn,
   animals used for riding, animals that graze, beasts, birds, and
   insects—and creatures that look strange or are [173]hybrids.

Man

   [[174]edit]
   [175][220px-Marvels_of_creatures_and_Strange_things_existing_-_Al-qazwi
   ni_-_Rhinoceros%2C_bull_and_tree_creatures.png] Page featuring humans
   and other creatures, painted in [176]Shiraz around 1545

   Humanity has the highest rank in the order of God's creation
   ([177]macrocosm): he is its quintessence ([178]microcosm) and can be
   both the embodiment of the angels and [179]Satan. Humanity has a
   [180]rational soul and can think, talk, and choose to ascend to the
   highest or lowest stations in life. Man's soul is immortal, and he is
   created for immortality; he changes his place of living from the womb
   to the earth and from there to [181]paradise or [182]hellfire.

   Next to man are the [183]jinn, created from smokeless fire and can be
   in different forms. It is also believed that the jinn represent the
   rebellious among men or that angels were created from the light of the
   fire and the devils or jinn from its smoke. According to a legend, the
   jinn were created before [184]Adam and lived on the land, sea, plains
   and mountains and God's mercy for them was boundless. They had a
   government, prophets, religion and laws, but they became disobedient
   and stubborn and broke the rules of the prophets, which culminated in
   chaos on earth. [185]Solomon became their lord whom they obeyed. Ya’juj
   and Ma’juj ([186]Gog and Magog) dwell in the seventh clime, according
   to Qazwini in another work (Ātar al-balad). Traditionally Islam assigns
   their homeland between the fifth and seventh climes.^[187][15]

Bestiary

   [[188]edit]
   [189][220px-Muhammad_ibn_Muhammad_Shakir_Ruzmah-%27i_Nathani_-_A_Fabulo
   us_%28Legendary%29_Bird_Such_as_a_Griffon_or_Simurgh_-_Walters_W659133B
   _-_Full_Page.jpg] [190]Anqa or [191]Simurgh ([192]Phoenix). 1717 CE,
   Ottoman Empire

   God created the birds because He knew that many people would deny the
   existence of flying creatures, especially the angels. Furthermore,
   Qazwini adds as proof that God created birds with three wings, as He
   did the unicorn, the Indian ass with a horn or the bat without wings;
   why not angels? Among the birds, Qazwini classifies the [193]Anqa or
   [194]Simurgh ([195]Phoenix) as the most known bird and the kin of birds
   that lived alone on [196]Mount Qaf. This idea goes as back as to the
   time of [197]Zoroaster. In more recent traditions, the Anqa is a wise
   bird with experience gained throughout many ages and gives warnings and
   moral advice.

   This bird lived without procreation long before Adam was created; he
   was single and the first and most powerful bird. The "[198]golden age"
   of the Simurgh was the time of Solomon in which not only ministers were
   near his throne but also animals and birds with whom Solomon could
   speak; the Anqa also talked to him and was the most respected. The
   second bird that is also recurring in classical [199]Persian literature
   and mentioned by Qazwini is the [200]Homa ([201]paradise bird). When it
   lands on someone's head, that person becomes the king of his land. A
   bird used in Iranian mystical symbolism is the salamander or
   "firebird", which was not seen since the time of Muhammad. Qazwini
   talks about the [202]hoopoe, which has a central role in [203]Iranian
   mysticism, only in passing; here, it is described as being able to see
   water from afar but not the mesh that is in front of its eyes.

   So the hoopoe symbolizes fate: when it comes, human eyes are blinded,
   i.e. a human cannot predict his fate. Another exceptional bird in
   Qazwini's list is the [204]eagle because [205]lions feared it, and from
   his wings, fire appears. Birds that were conceived as strange hybrids
   by Qazwini are the [206]vulture, having the claws of the [207]rooster,
   or the [208]ostrich with the feet of a camel and the body of a bird;
   this bird eats stones and flames and can live in fire for ten years. He
   can also digest the legs of a horse and birds but not date pits. The
   ostrich fears his own shadow and always walks against the sun. There
   are also other rare and strange birds, for example, a giant bird in
   [209]Khuzistan that attacks [210]camels and [211]elephants and has eggs
   similar to [212]crystal; the "purple bird," a white bird that sits on a
   rock in the [213]Chinese Sea and the person that looks at that rock
   must laugh to death, except that this bird lands on the rock; or a bird
   in Tabaristan which is seen in spring and carries one hundred sparrows
   on its tail and eats one each day.

Lapidary

   [[214]edit]

   Some [215]stones are associated to jinn or are a remedy against
   ailments: the [216]emerald cures illnesses and repels devils; a stone
   called the "talk" is used for [217]amulets and [218]magic drinks;
   [219]amber was first discovered by Iblis; [220]Alexander the Great used
   the faylaq stone to protect his men from devils, according to
   [221]Aristotle, from whose [222]lapidary Qazwini often quotes,
   nullifies the influence of magicians and devils and protects from jinn.
   One stone, the bahta, is described as being found at the edge of the
   utmost darkness where the sun has no effect, near the [223]cosmic
   ocean.

Analysis

   [[224]edit]
   This section needs additional citations for [225]verification. Please
   help [226]improve this article by [227]adding citations to reliable
   sources in this section. Unsourced material may be challenged and
   removed. (October 2017) ([228]Learn how and when to remove this
   message)

   Called the "most precious cosmography of the Islamic culture" by
   [229]Carl Brockelmann, Qazwini's cosmography was one of the most read
   works in the [230]Islamic world since numerous manuscripts and
   translations from [231]Arabic into Islamic languages have survived.
   Scholars presented excerpts of it to Western readers.

   In Qazwini's conception, the [232]Universe is the manifestation of the
   absolute Truth or [233]God. God's command, "Be!" caused all things in
   the universe to have a place and a reciprocal relationship between
   themselves. In Islamic tradition, humanity has the task of
   understanding the wisdom of God's creation as much as possible. God is
   the ultimate goal of that cosmic structure.

   Traditional Islamic sciences are connected with cosmology that has an
   essential role within the [234]metaphysical system. Whereas cosmology
   deals with the spiritual side of the universe, cosmography concerns
   itself with the physical aspect and its processes. Qazwini states that
   it is essential that humanity exerts itself to investigate the wondrous
   and wisely conceived creation of God, reflect on it in astonishment,
   and understand it as much as possible. In this way, humanity will gain
   delight in this world and the hereafter. Next to this, Qazwini explains
   important terminology in his book: 1) marvels are a phenomenon that
   confuses man because he is not able to grasp its cause and effects; 2)
   creation is everything except God; it is either essential (body,
   spiritual substance) or accidental (other); 3) the strange is something
   which is rare and differs from the known and familiar things and causes
   astonishment; 4) Creation is divided into several things: it has an
   unknown cause, man cannot grasp it and it is known in its entirety but
   not in its details (e.g. the [235]celestial spheres).

   Moreover, Qazwini informs us in the introduction of his book that he
   left his home and family to study books because he believed that a
   man's best companion on earth are books. He marvelled at the wondrous
   and strange things in God's creation. How perfect a creation it is, as
   stated in the Quran (50:6). In his explanation of created things in the
   powerful and vast universe (51:47), he describes the orbit of the sun
   based on statements of scientists but also quotes a tradition in which
   the angel [236]Gabriel tells [237]Muhammad that the sun moves forward
   500 years or [238]farsakhs (a farsakh is c. 6 km) from the time
   Muhammad says "No" until the time he says "Yes" one after another.

   In Qazwini's view, wondrous things are in the heavens and the earth, as
   the Quran informs (10:101), and in the seas and at their shores since
   it was their beginning and end were not clarified; it was part of the
   unknown world, inhabited with wondrous and strange creatures. Following
   the [239]Judeo-Islamic tradition, Qazwini confirms that in the
   beginning, God created one substance, then He melted it and from the
   smoke became the heaven and the sediments were formed to earth; heaven
   and earth were first together, and God divided them (Quran 21:31) and
   He completed his creation in six days. Altogether God made [240]seven
   heavens and [241]seven earths (Quran 65:12).

   Whether known or unknown, every created thing has a sign of divine
   wisdom within itself and represents the unity of God. Based on
   Ptolemy's design of the universe, Qazwini talks about [242]9 spheres in
   the heaven: the [243]earth, the [244]Moon, [245]Mercury, [246]Venus,
   the [247]Sun, [248]Mars, [249]Jupiter, [250]Saturn and the [251]Sphere
   of spheres, which embraces all other spheres and causes day and night;
   they all have their own [252]orbit. Whereas on the one hand to these
   and other [253]stars, Qazwini refers to the spheres or [254]plants in
   [255]scientific terms, on the other hand he supports the effects of the
   Moon, the [256]North Pole and [257]South Pole on [258]man and
   [259]animal, such as having the power to cure illnesses, with sayings
   among people.

   Humanity's purpose is to achieve perfection and eschew bad habits and
   acts. The good character outweighs in this life, and the next; bad
   character is a sin that can not be forgiven, and through it, hunanity
   descends to the lowest of the low in hell. A human with a good
   character is thus angel-like, and a bad character is the feature of the
   despised Satan—Qazwini's concern here, so to speak, [260]anthropology.

Later influence

   [[261]edit]

   [262]Ahmed Bican Yazıcıoğlu reworked Qazwini's cosmology in the
   [263]Dürr-i Meknûn in the year 1453, providing his Turkish readership
   with a much [264]abridged version (reduced to c. one-fifth of the
   original) in plain Turkish prose, with some new materials
   added.^[265][16] Bican's rendering was later included by [266]Giovanni
   Battista Donado in his Della Letteratura de Turchi, Venice (1688), in a
   shortlist of Turkish works he felt merited translation into
   Italian.^[267][17]
