   Community-based top-level domain name

   CAPTION: .music

  [63][220px-.MUSIC_Logo.png]
    [64]TLD type   Community
       Status      ICANN Registry Agreement for .music was signed on May 4, 2021
    Intended use   [65]music
  Registry website [66]music.us

   .music is a community-based [67]top-level domain name (TLD) operated
   for the benefit of the global music community. It was one of the most
   highly contested [68]new gTLDs, with eight applicants in contention.

   On April 17, 2019, the .MUSIC registry, the [69]International
   Federation of the Phonographic Industry, [70]Confédération
   Internationale des Sociétés d'Auteurs et Compositeurs, the
   [71]International Federation of Musicians, the International Federation
   of Arts Councils and Culture Agencies, the [72]Recording Industry
   Association of America, the [73]National Music Publishers' Association,
   [74]The Recording Academy, the [75]Independent Music Companies
   Association, the Worldwide Independent Network, the [76]American
   Association of Independent Music, the NSAI, the [77]National
   Association of Music Merchants (NAMM), and the Independent Music
   Publishers International Forum (IMPF) announced that the global music
   community based application, that was supported by a global music
   coalition with members representing over 95% of global music consumed,
   was approved by the [78]Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and
   Numbers (ICANN).^[79][1] The .MUSIC registry agreement was signed on
   May 4, 2021.^[80][2]

Applicants

   [[81]edit]
    1. .MUSIC ^[82][3]
    2. [83]Google (Charleston Road Registry Inc.)
    3. [84]Amazon
    4. Donuts (Victor Cross)
    5. Far Further
    6. Top Level Domain Holdings Ltd. (TLDH) and LHL TLD Investment
       Partners
    7. Radix
    8. Famous Four Media[85]GRS Domains

.MUSIC

   [[86]edit]

   A community priority application was applied for by .MUSIC under the
   legal name of DotMusic.^[87][4] MUSIC was headed by [88]Constantine
   Roussos, who launched the .MUSIC Initiative in 2005 to gather support
   from the global music community for a petition to ICANN for the rights
   of .MUSIC. Associated organizations included the International
   Federation of Arts Councils and Culture Agencies (IFACCA), the
   [89]American Association of Independent Music (A2IM), and the National
   Association of Music Manufacturers (NAMM).^[90][5] The initiative's
   objectives included:^[91][6]^[92][7]
     * Music education
     * Fighting [93]piracy
     * Promoting the arts and global music
     * Innovation in music and the internet space
     * Promoting competition

   The team planned to operate the .music TLD using a multi-stakeholder
   approach. A portion of the revenue generated from registrations was
   marked for donation to selected non-profit music organizations. These
   registrations would be restricted to .MUSIC-accredited Community Member
   Organizations (MCMOs).^[94][8] [95]CentralNic was selected as the
   back-end registry services provider.^[96][9]

Far Further

   [[97]edit]

   Far Further was founded by music professionals^[98][10] with the
   objective of uniting the global [99]music industry by providing it with
   a secure domain space^[100][11] to promote music, protect intellectual
   property rights and help advance music education. They submitted a
   community priority application using .music LLC, a subsidiary of Far
   Further LLC.

   The company chose [101]Neustar to provide back-end registry services.
   The domain was to be restricted to registrants qualified via
   association with a number of supporting
   organizations.^[102][12]^[103][13]

TLDH and LHL

   [[104]edit]

   On March 23, 2012, Top Level Domain Holdings Ltd. and LHL TLD
   Investment Partners signed a partnership agreement to apply for the
   .music TLD. Minds + Machines was to provide the back-end registry
   services.^[105][14]

Amazon

   [[106]edit]

   [107]Amazon's application was issued a [108]GAC Early Warning^[109][15]
   from representative of Australia and GAC Chair Heather Dryden. It
   stated that the applicant was "seeking exclusive access to a common
   generic string [..] that relates to a broad market sector," which
   Dryden noted could have unintended consequences and a negative impact
   on competition.^[110][16]

Radix

   [[111]edit]

   Radix received a GAC Early Warning from the U.S. Government. The issue
   did not relate to their technical capabilities or the content of their
   applications, but rather the inclusion of an [112]email address
   associated with the U.S. [113]Federal Bureau of Investigation in a
   recommendation filed with Radix's application.^[114][17]

Objections

   [[115]edit]

   A Legal Rights Objection, as defined by the ICANN approved mediator
   [116]WIPO, is a third party "formal objection to an application on
   several grounds [..] When such an objection is filed, an independent
   panel (comprised of one or three experts) will determine whether the
   applicant’s potential use of the applied-for gTLD would be likely to
   infringe [..] the objector’s existing trademark, or IGO name or
   acronym."^[117][18]

Official objection

   [[118]edit]

   An official objection was filed by DotMusic Limited against .music
   applicants since DotMusic Limited had registered trademarks for
   ".music" and "dotmusic" in nearly thirty countries.^[119][19]

Community objections

   [[120]edit]

   The American Association of Independent Music (AAIM) and DotMusic filed
   community objections against Google, dot Music Limited, Dotmusic Inc.,
   Top Level Domain Holdings Ltd., and Donuts on the basis that they
   applied as open registries without enhanced safeguards or sufficient
   eligibility restrictions to protect music-related intellectual property
   and prevent music piracy. The AAIM also filed a community objection
   against Amazon because of its application's exclusive-access registry
   policies and discriminatory registration eligibility criteria that
   restricted registrations solely to Amazon and its affiliates. For the
   same reason, the International Federation of Arts Councils and Culture
   Agencies and DotMusic filed a community objection against Far
   Further.^[121][20]

   The objections against Amazon and Far Further were based on the ICANN
   Registry Agreement which required that new gTLD registries be subject
   to the requirements of Specification 11, which mandates that a TLD
   Registry must provide non-discriminatory access to registry services to
   all ICANN accredited registrars that enter into and are in compliance
   with the registry-registrar agreement for the TLD.^[122][21] In
   February 2014 ICANN passed resolutions for the new gTLD program which
   mandated specific enhanced safeguards^[123][22] and prohibited
   applicants from operating a new gTLD as an exclusive-access registry
   for a TLD based on a generic term.^[124][23]
