   Chemical compound

   1-Chloro-1,1-difluoroethane (HCFC-142b) is a [107]haloalkane with the
   chemical formula [108]C[109]H[3][110]C[111]Cl[112]F[2]. It belongs to
   the [113]hydrochlorofluorocarbon (HCFC) family of man-made compounds
   that contribute significantly to both [114]ozone depletion and
   [115]global warming when released into the environment. It is primarily
   used as a [116]refrigerant where it is also known as R-142b and by
   trade names including [117]Freon-142b.^[118][2]

Physiochemical properties

   [[119]edit]

   1-Chloro-1,1-difluoroethane is a highly flammable, colorless gas under
   most atmospheric conditions. It has a boiling point of
   -10 °C.^[120][1]^[121][3] Its critical temperature is near
   137 °C.^[122][4]

Applications

   [[123]edit]

   HCFC-142b is used as a refrigerant, as a [124]blowing agent for foam
   plastics production, and as feedstock to make [125]polyvinylidene
   fluoride (PVDF).^[126][5] It was introduced to replace the
   [127]chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) that were initially undergoing a
   phase-out per the Montreal Protocol, but HCFCs still have a significant
   ozone-depletion ability. As of year 2020, HCFC's are replaced by non
   ozone depleting [128]HFCs within many applications.^[129][6]

   In the United States, the [130]EPA stated that HCFCs could be used in
   "processes that result in the transformation or destruction of the
   HCFCs", such as using HCFC-142b as a feedstock to make PVDF. HCFCs
   could also be used in equipment that was manufactured before January 1,
   2010.^[131][7] The point of these new regulations was to phase-out
   HCFCs in much the same way that CFCs were phased out. HCFC-142b
   production in non article 5 countries like the United States was banned
   on January 1, 2020, under the Montreal Protocol.^[132][6]

Production history

   [[133]edit]

   According to the Alternative Fluorocarbons Environmental Acceptability
   Study (AFEAS), in 2006 global production (excluding India and China who
   did not report production data) of HCFC-142b was 33,779 metric tons and
   an increase in production from 2006 to 2007 of 34%.^[134][8]

   For the most part, concentrations of HCFCs in the atmosphere match the
   emission rates that were reported by industries. The exception to this
   is HCFC-142b which had a higher concentration than the emission rates
   suggest it should.^[135][9]

Environmental effects

   [[136]edit]

   [137][310px-HCFC142b_concentration.jpg] Growth of HCFC-142b in Earth's
   atmosphere since year 1992.^[138][10][139][220px-HCFC-142b_mm.png]
   HCFC-142b measured by the Advanced Global Atmospheric Gases Experiment
   ([140]AGAGE) in the lower atmosphere ([141]troposphere) at stations
   around the world. Abundances are given as pollution free monthly mean
   mole fractions in [142]parts-per-trillion.

   The concentration of HCFC-142b in the atmosphere grew to over 20 parts
   per trillion by year 2010.^[143][10] It has an [144]ozone depletion
   potential (ODP) of 0.07.^[145][11] This is low compared to the ODP=1 of
   [146]trichlorofluoromethane (CFC-11, R-11), which also grew about ten
   times more abundant in the atmosphere by year 1985 (prior to
   introduction of HCFC-142b and the Montreal Protocol).

   HCFC-142b is also a minor but potent [147]greenhouse gas. It has an
   estimated [148]lifetime of about 17 years and a 100-year [149]global
   warming potential ranging 2300 to 5000.^[150][12]^[151][13] This
   compares to the GWP=1 of [152]carbon dioxide, which had a much greater
   atmospheric concentration near 400 parts per million in year 2020.
