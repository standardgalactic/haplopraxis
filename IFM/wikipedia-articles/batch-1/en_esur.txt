   'En Esur, also En Esur ([111]Hebrew: עין אֵסוּר;
   [112][[113]ʕ[114]e[115]n [116]ʔ[117]e[118]s[119]u[120]ʁ[121]] [122]eh-N
   eh-s-oor) or Ein Asawir ([123]Arabic: عين الأساور, [124]lit. 'Spring of
   the Bracelets'), is an ancient site located on the northern [125]Sharon
   Plain, at the entrance of the [126]Wadi Ara pass leading from the
   [127]Coastal Plain further inland. The site includes an archaeological
   mound ([128]tell), called Tel Esur or Tell el-Asawir, another unnamed
   mound, and two springs, one of which gives the site its name.

   A 7,000-year-old Early [129]Chalcolithic large village already showing
   signs of incipient urbanisation and with an open space used for cultic
   activities was discovered at the site below later, Bronze Age
   remains.^[130][4]

   During the [131]Early Bronze Age, around 3000 BCE, a massive fortified
   [132]proto-city with an estimated population of 5,000 to 6,000
   inhabitants existed there. It was the largest city in the region,
   larger than other significant sites such as [133]Megiddo and
   [134]Jericho, but smaller than more distant ones in [135]Egypt and
   [136]Mesopotamia. The city was discovered in 1977, but its massive
   extent was realized only in 1993. A major excavation between 2017 and
   2019 ahead of the construction of a highway interchange exposed the
   city's houses, streets and public structures, as well as countless
   artifacts including pottery, figurines and tools. Archaeologists
   announced its discovery in 2019, calling it the "[137]New York of the
   Early Bronze Age".^[138][5]

History

   [[139]edit]

   The site is known in Arabic as Tell el-Asawir. The mound covers an area
   of around 5.5 acres with a maximum height of 11 meters above the plain.
   It appears in the 1799 map drawn by French geographer [140]Pierre
   Jacotin.^[141][6] American archaeologist and biblical scholar
   [142]William F. Albright visited the site during his 1923 trip to
   [143]Mandatory Palestine. He recalled the opinion of German scholar
   [144]Albrecht Alt that Tel Esur is the site of an ancient city called
   "Yaham", located just north of the [145]Menashe Heights and mentioned
   in the sources of the 15th-century BCE Egyptian pharaoh [146]Thutmose
   III, who campaigned against a coalition of [147]Canaanite city-states
   led by the king of [148]Kadesh and gave battle at Megiddo.^[149][7]
   According to the Egyptian account, Thutmose III camped in Yaham before
   he marched on Megiddo and [150]captured the city. Albright stated that
   the location of the site corresponds with the geographic descriptions
   of the Egyptian sources, and his discovery of Bronze Age pottery while
   surveying the mound further confirmed this identification in his
   opinion.^[151][8] Today however, Yaham is identified with a site
   located at the Arab village of Kafr Yama, since 1988 part of
   [152]Zemer, some 10 kilometers south of Tel Esur.^[153][9]

   The discovery of the larger site around Tel Esur and its springs
   occurred in 1977, during the digging of a water reservoir south of the
   mound. A salvage excavation was conducted by archaeologists Azriel
   Zigelman and Ram Gofna of the [154]Tel Aviv University. They discovered
   two settlement layers, one from the [155]Chalcolithic period (the last
   period of the [156]Stone Age) and the Early [157]Bronze Age. The former
   included the foundations of structures made of rough stones and some
   installations. These are dated to the Early Chalcolithic (c. 6000 years
   ago). The latter included the foundations of massive structures made of
   large stones. The widest wall measured 1.7 meters in width. The pottery
   there is dated to the Early Bronze Age I period (3300–3000
   BCE).^[158][10]

   The site was surveyed by Yehuda Neʾeman and by the [159]Manasseh Hill
   Country Survey.^[160][11]^[161][12] A survey and an excavation was
   conducted in 1993 by Eli Yannai of the [162]Israel Antiquities
   Authority (IAA). It revealed the massive extent of the site during the
   Early Bronze Age, as well as settlement remains from the Neolithic and
   Chalcolithic periods, and sherds from the [163]Byzantine and
   [164]Ottoman periods.^[165][13]

   The site was excavated between 2000 and 2002 by a team led by A.
   Zertal.^[166][14] En Esur was excavated by professional and volunteer
   archaeologists between January 2017 and 2019, with the research
   overseen by archaeologists Itai Elad and Yitzhak
   Paz.^[167][15]^[168][1]^[169][16] The work was organized in part by the
   [170]Israel Antiquities Authority and financed by [171]Netivei Israel,
   Israel's national transportation infrastructure
   company.^[172][1]^[173][16] During the process of excavation,
   archaeologists found a temple within the city that was built
   approximately 2,000 years before the rest of the site.^[174][16]

   In an announcement of their discovery, researchers called En Esur
   "cosmopolitan" and the "[175]New York of the Early Bronze
   Age".^[176][5]

Location

   [[177]edit]

   'En Esur stands in the northern Sharon plain, c. 1 km east of
   [178]Moshav [179]Ein Iron, at the outlet of [180]Wadi Ara,^[181][1] a
   valley which allowed the [182]ancient international coastal highway to
   bypass the difficult section squeezed between the [183]sea and western
   [184]Mount Carmel by passing through the mountain. Today, the important
   [185]Highway 65 follows the same route and cuts through the
   archaeological site of En Sur.

Site

   [[186]edit]
   [187][330px-Plan_of_En_Esur.svg.png] Plan of En Esur showing its

   the site has been disturbed by modern developments including several
   buildings and a reservoir.^[189][17]^[190][18]

   The site of En Esur consists of three elements: Tel Esur, which is the
   main tell (a mound of accumulated human settlement layers) covering c.
   28 [191]dunams, a smaller mound southeast of it, and an open field that
   surrounds the mounds, which was occupied by a massive, densely built
   city during the Early Bronze Age.^[192][1] The site is supported by two
   abundant water springs: 'En Esur or 'En Arubot, east of the tell, which
   gives the site its name; and a second, unnamed one southwest of the
   tell.^[193][1]

Neolithic

   [[194]edit]

   Potsherds and stone tools found in the lowest levels excavated in the
   area south of Tel Esur (Area A) show that the site was occupied during
   the [195]Pottery Neolithic period.^[196][1]^[197][19] Little is known
   about this phase; no traces of structures were found, and only a few
   artefacts.^[198][19] Both the pottery and the stone tools resemble
   those of the [199]Jericho IX culture.^[200][20]^[201][21]

Chalcolithic

   [[202]edit]

   The site was occupied throughout the Early [203]Chalcolithic period,
   some 7,000 years ago. There were only scattered finds from Early
   Chalcolithic I and a small occupation in Early Chalcolithic III. It was
   during the Early Chalcolithic II (EC II) period that the site became a
   significant place, reaching a size of 50 hectares.^[204][22]^[205][23]

   The archaeologists uncovered a c. 60 m^2 large area, free of dwellings,
   used for cultic activities.^[206][4] It was found to contain numerous
   articulated sheep, cattle and pig bones, showing that animal parts had
   been buried in this open area during ritual ceremonies. Some 40 metres
   south of there, a shallow pit containing animal bones as well as the
   head of a anthropomorphic clay figurine was discovered, which may also
   be indicative of some cultic activity.^[207][4] The entire space
   between the two findings, set at the margin of the settlement, was
   likely kept open for cultic activity and other functions, although it
   cannot be ruled out that some mud-brick buildings had stood there
   without leaving any discernible remains.^[208][4] Intramural burials of
   adults and infants were also found as well as 237 biconvex
   [209]slingstones and zoomorphic figurines, all in the EC II
   level.^[210][22]

Early Bronze Age

   [[211]edit]

City

   [[212]edit]
   [213][220px-Tel_Esur_%286%29.JPG] Sloping wall of a tower, part of
   Middle Bronze IIA city wall

   Above the Early Chalcolithic settlement, a large walled [214]Early
   Bronze Age city was discovered.^[215][23] It occupied a space of around
   0.65 square kilometers (160 acres) and may have had 5,000 to 6,000
   inhabitants.^[216][24] This would have made the settlement much larger
   than [217]Tel Megiddo in Israel and [218]Jericho in the [219]West Bank,
   and therefore the largest settlement in the [220]Southern Levant during
   this period, but smaller than more distant cities in Egypt and
   Mesopotamia.^[221][16]^[222][23]^[223][25] Archaeologist Itai Elad
   stated that En Esur is double the size of other large settlements known
   in the area.^[224][3] Researchers excavating the site have said that it
   demonstrates early processes of urbanization within Canaanite
   civilization,^[225][3] and that the city would have probably possessed
   a substantial "administrative mechanism."^[226][5] [227]Haaretz
   described the site as "vastly bigger than anything thought possible in
   the Southern Levant 5,000 years ago."^[228][23] Its discoverers have
   called the city a "[229]megalopolis".^[230][26]

   The settlement was abandoned some time towards the end of Early Bronze
   Age IB, much like other nearby sites at this time with the end of the
   [231]Uruk Period, such as Tel Bet She'an and [232]Tel Megiddo, between
   3000 and 2800 BCE.^[233][3]^[234][27]^[235][28] However, unlike Tel Bet
   She'an, Tel Megiddo and other sites in the Levant, there is no evidence
   of a return to urbanization in the following [236]Middle Bronze
   Age.^[237][28]

Structure and character

   [[238]edit]

   The settlement is believed to have existed at the crossroads of two
   important trading routes.^[239][27] Archaeologists excavating the site
   believe that the city was planned, and included not only streets,
   alleys and squares, but also facilities for storage and drainage, and a
   cemetery.^[240][29]^[241][3]^[242][30]^[243][31] En Esur was surrounded
   by fortified walls that were 2 metres (6.6 feet)
   high.^[244][26]^[245][3]

   The site includes about four million [246]artefacts overall, with
   millions of [247]potsherds and [248]flint tools, and some [249]basalt
   stone vessels.^[250][16]^[251][26]^[252][25] These included knives
   related to [253]Caananite blades.^[254][32] The inhabitants of En Esur
   are thought to have been an agricultural people. They would have traded
   with other regions and kingdoms.^[255][29] Archaeologists found pottery
   originating in the Jordan Valley,^[256][23] and sealed
   imprints^[[257]clarification needed] on tools demonstrate that these
   were brought from Egypt.^[258][26]

Temple

   [[259]edit]

   A temple was found within the city,^[260][3] which was located in a
   public area and includes a courtyard with a huge stone basin for
   rituals.^[261][26] Burnt animal bones were found inside the temple,
   providing evidence of possible [262]ritual sacrifices.^[263][26]
   Several figurines were also unearthed inside the temple, including a
   human head and a seal impression showing a cultic scene with person in
   a supplicant position and raising his hands with a horned animal next
   to him.^[264][4]^[265][26]

Necropolis

   [[266]edit]

   Burial caves dating from the fourth to the second millennium BCE were
   found at Tell el-Asawir during 1953 excavations.^[267][33] The Tell
   el-Asawir [268]necropolis, located near a stone quarry, underwent a
   salvage excavation in 2003, resulting in a find presented to the press
   as "the largest Bronze Age necropolis in the world".^[269][34] The
   [270]Ministry of Religious Affairs intervened and the thousands of
   human skeletons excavated from burial chambers measuring up to 100 m^2
   had to be reburied before they could be scientifically studied,
   although their age excluded the possibility of them being the remains
   of Jews, which are not to be disturbed under [271]Jewish religious
   laws.^[272][34]

Late Bronze Age

   [[273]edit]

   During the LBA the mound was the site of a small rural settlement, of
   an agricultural nature, which was covered by a [274]destruction layer.
   It was located in the northern 2.5 acres of the mound. Finds included
   large jars, [275]pithoi, and grinding stones as well as a number of
   flint sickle blades. The pottery assemblage was dated to LB IB/IIA or
   between 1400 and 1375 BCE. The finds included a royal scarab of
   [276]Amenhotep III. The regnal dates of this Phaoroh are not known with
   certainty. Estimates range from 1408–1386 BCE to 1390–1352
   BCE.^[277][35]

Iron Age administrative centre

   [[278]edit]

   In excavations led by S. Bar in 2010–2014 on the western slopes of the
   small mound (Area D), a large public structure from the early 8th
   century BCE (Iron Age IIB) came to light.^[279][2] The fortified tower
   abutted by a storehouse was interpreted as being part of a regional
   administrative centre, due to similarities with other contemporary
   public complexes.^[280][2] However, Tel 'Esur is unique in being the
   only small, countryside settlement, rather than large royal Iron Age
   estate or city, such as [281]Hazor or Megiddo, where the state or king
   built an administrative centre.^[282][2] The architectural complex
   shows enough sophistication to vouch for it being a royal or state-run
   project, in spite of its size of less than 0.5 ha, which would
   otherwise be characteristic of a hamlet or farmstead.^[283][2] It comes
   in the context of both [284]Israel and Judah having many, primarily
   rural settlements established in the 9th–8th centuries BCE.^[285][2]
   The excavation director speculates that the Tel 'Esur compound was
   established during the reign of the [286]Omride king [287]Jeroboam II
   (786–746 BCE), as part of the measures taken by the kings of
   [288]Israel to strengthen their grip over the northern valleys and the
   [289]Shephelah at a time of maximal expansion (see [290]2 Kgs
   14:25).^[291][2] The Tel 'Esur complex is the first official presence
   along the Wadi Ara pass, proof for the interest of the kings of the
   [292]northern kingdom in this thoroughfare.^[293][2]

   The tower measures c. 13 by 13 metres, with thick outer walls
   indicative of a considerable height and a certain martial look,
   although its location at the foot of the mound and the comparatively
   modest size exclude a military purpose.^[294][2] The entire structure
   is more likely to have had a mixed role, both practical and political
   as a built statement of royal power and control over the newly acquired
   territory.^[295][2] The long, tripartite building adjoining the tower
   was most probably a warehouse where local produce or different
   commodities were collected, although some researchers see in such
   structures stables, barracks, or marketplaces.^[296][2] A typical
   structure for the Iron Age, such entrepôts are all located next to
   major trade routes and are commonly found at bottlenecks along the
   borders of Israel and Judah, with the one at Tel 'Esur being again
   uniquely placed further in from the border.^[297][2] Its large size and
   internal separation through solid walls, rather than columns,
   indicatess that the even larger such structure found at Hazor had
   served as its prototype.^[298][2]

   The building complex was in use for half a century at most, being
   emptied of goods and abandoned in the mid-8th century, a decade or two
   before the destructive campaign of [299]Tiglath-Pileser III in [300]732
   BCE.^[301][2] A possible cause might have been Israel's deteriorating
   relations with [302]Assyria, with the nearby Israelite city of [303]Dor
   being another example of early abandonment, rather than the more common
   case of settlements evacuated out of fear during the actual approach of
   the Assyrian army, or the many destroyed by it.^[304][2]

   The site was later occupied in the Persian, Roman, and Byzantine
   periods.^[305][36]^[306][37]

Preservation

   [[307]edit]

   In October 2019, according to [308]Haaretz, En Esur was slated to be
   paved over by a planned highway interchange^[309][16]^[310][23] for the
   new town of [311]Harish, with the [312]Smithsonian magazine writing
   that it will be re-covered, but that the interchange would be built
   "high over the ruins".^[313][38] All findings were photographed and
   computer-processed, the 3D documentation of the site allowing
   archaeologists to continue studying it after it has been covered
   over.^[314][38] The [315]Agence France-Presse has reported that the
   road plans have been modified in order to protect the archaeological
   site.^[316][3]

Online excavation reports (HA-ESI)

   [[492]edit]

   Excavation reports published by the Israel Antiquities Authority (IAA)
   in [493]Hadashot Arkheologiyot – Excavations and Surveys in Israel
   (HA-ESI)

'En Esur (Asawir)

   [[494]edit]
     *

   Yannai, Eli (2014). [495]"'En Esur (Asawir): Preliminary Report
   (14/09/2014)". Hadashot Arkheologiyot. 126. Pottery Neolithic (Wadi
   Rabah) and Early Chalcolithic findings on smaller area than the large
   Early Bronze Age I city.

     Elad, Itai; Paz, Yitzhak (2018). [496]"'En Esur (Asawir): Preliminary
   Report (16/07/2018)". Hadashot Arkheologiyot. 130.
   [497]JSTOR [498]26691671. January–December 2017 extensive excavation.
   General presentation, settlement history. Mainly Early Chalcolithic
   (post-[499]Wadi Rabah culture), Late Chalcolithic (Ghassulian culture),
   Early Bronze Age IA (densely built round or elliptical structures with
   installations, drainage channel) and IB (densely built, dozens of
   rectangular structures with rounded or square corners, network of
   streets).

     Elad, Itai; Paz, Yitzhak; Shalem, Dina (2018). [500]"'En Esur
   (Asawir), Area M: Preliminary Report (31/12/2018)". Hadashot
   Arkheologiyot. 130. March–July 2018 salvage excavation. Mainly Early
   Chalcolithic (post-[501]Wadi Rabah culture; architecture, drainage
   channels, burials), Late Chalcolithic (Ghassulian culture, copper
   artifacts), and Early Bronze Age IB (sparser early, and architecturally
   dense later phase with streets and dwellings).

     Elad, Itai; Paz, Yitzhak; Shalem, Dina (2019). [502]"'En Esur
   (Asawir), Area O: Preliminary Report (07/07/2019)". Hadashot
   Arkheologiyot. 131. August–October 2018 salvage excavation at the
   center of the proto-historic site near the two springs. Settlement
   nucleus with full occupation sequence (at least 3 m deep). Mainly Early
   Chalcolithic (settlement, post-[503]Wadi Rabah culture), Late
   Chalcolithic (dwellings, [504]Ghassulian culture pottery), Early Bronze
   IA (continued habitation; dwellings and silos on c. 10–20 dunams) and
   Early Bronze IB (site grows to 600 dunams, higher occupation density,
   urban centre).

     Elad, Itai; Paz, Yitzhak; Shalem, Dina (2020). [505]"'En Esur
   (Asawir), Areas K and L: Preliminary Report (20/05/2020)". Hadashot
   Arkheologiyot. 132. 2018 large salvage excavation. Remains and finds
   from the Pottery Neolithic ([506]Yarmukian pottery), Early and Late
   Chalcolithic (building remains, installations, sherds), Early Bronze
   Age IB (dwellings, silos), and Roman periods.

     Elad, Itai; Paz, Yitzhak; Shalem, Dina (2020). [507]"'En Esur
   (Asawir), Area N: Preliminary Report (26/10/2020)". Hadashot
   Arkheologiyot. 132. 2018–2019 trial and salvage excavation in Area N,
   which extends along the southwestern part of the proto-historic site of
   'En Esur. Early Chalcolithic cultic area with animal bones, figurine.
   Early Bronze IB settlement (silos, streets, compounds, public areas and
   buildings, fortification walls) including a temple with a large
   monolithic stone basin, figurines and a figurative seal impression.
   Intermediate Bronze Age remains.

Tel Esur

   [[508]edit]

     *

   Sa'id, 'Abed a-Salam (2011). [509]"Tel Esur: Final Report
   (17/04/2011)". Hadashot Arkheologiyot. 123. November 2008 trial
   excavation: large Byzantine building, some Bronze Age finds in alluvium
   from the tell.

     Oren, Eliran; Yaroshevich, Alla (2013). [510]"Tel Esur, Survey Along
   Highway 65: Final Report (13/06/2013)". Hadashot Arkheologiyot. 125.
   January 2011 survey.

     Bar, Shay (2016). [511]"Tel Esur 2010–2012: Preliminary Report
   (07/08/2016)". Hadashot Arkheologiyot. 128. Has details on many
   settlement periods including Middle Bronze (main phase, including MB
   IIA city fortifications), Iron Age II, Hellenistic, and Early Roman.

Horbat Gilan

   [[512]edit]

   Horbat Gilan: site about 1 km east of Tel Esur, containing burial caves
   which are part of the Early Bronze Age necropolis of En Esur.
     *

   Milevski, Ianir (2005). [513]"Horbat Gilan: Final Report (15/05/2005)".
   Hadashot Arkheologiyot. 117. May 2004 trial excavation, minor
   [514]ex-situ ceramic finds.

     Gorzalczany, Amir (2005). [515]"Horbat Gilan (South), 18/12/2005".
   Hadashot Arkheologiyot. 117. 2002–2003 salvage excavation 300 m south
   of Horbat Gilan. Quarries, possibly Roman and Byzantine (4th–5th c.
   CE), maybe initiated much earlier.

     Mahajna, Shireen (2006). [516]"Horbat Gilan (South), 10/09/2006".
   Hadashot Arkheologiyot. 118. 2004, two trial excavations. Burial caves
   (Early and Intermediate Bronze Age).

     Gorzalczany, Amir; Jacob, Sharvit (2007). [517]"Horbat Gilan
   (16/10/2007)". Hadashot Arkheologiyot. 119. 2003 salvage excavation
   near Horbat Gilan, inside Tel Esur cemetery. Burial caves (Early Bronze
   IB with rich grave good findings), Roman or Byzantine quarries (4th–5th
   c. CE).

     Dagan, Yehuda; Sadeh, Shelley (2008). [518]"Horbat Gilan (South),
   13/01/2008". Hadashot Arkheologiyot. 120. 2002 and 2003 seasons of
   salvage excavations. Early Bronze Age burial caves, part of Tel Esur
   necropolis, with a rich array of grave goods, and Roman or Byzantine
   quarries.

     'Ouda, Ahmed (2008). [519]"Horbat Gilan: Final Report (11/09/2008)".
   Hadashot Arkheologiyot. 120. August 2002 trial excavation. Late Roman
   and Byzantine settlement remains (4th–5th centuries CE).

     Massarwa, Abdallah (2019). [520]"Horbat Gilan (South): Final Report
   (27/08/2019)". Hadashot Arkheologiyot. 131. July 2016 trial excavation.
   Winepress (no date), quarry (4th–5th c. CE), cupmark (no date).
