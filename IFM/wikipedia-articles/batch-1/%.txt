   The percent sign % (sometimes per cent sign in [98]British English) is
   the symbol used to indicate a [99]percentage, a number or [100]ratio as
   a [101]fraction of 100. Related signs include the [102]permille (per
   thousand) sign ‰ and the [103]permyriad (per ten thousand) sign ‱ (also
   known as a [104]basis point), which indicate that a number is divided
   by one thousand or ten thousand, respectively. Higher proportions use
   [105]parts-per notation.

Correct style

   [[106]edit]

Form and spacing

   [[107]edit]

   English style guides prescribe writing the percent sign following the
   number without any space between (e.g. 50%).^[108][sources 1] However,
   the [109]International System of Units and [110]ISO 31-0 standard
   prescribe a space between the number and percent
   sign,^[111][8]^[112][9]^[113][10] in line with the general practice of
   using a [114]non-breaking space between a [115]numerical value and its
   corresponding [116]unit of measurement.

   Other languages have other rules for spacing in front of the percent
   sign:
     * In [117]Czech and in [118]Slovak, the percent sign is spaced with a
       non-breaking space if the number is used as a noun.^[119][11] In
       Czech, no space is inserted if the number is used as an adjective
       (e.g. "a 50% increase"),^[120][12] whereas Slovak uses
       a non-breaking space in this case as well.^[121][13]
     * In [122]Croatian, the percent sign is spaced^[123][14] with a
       non-breaking space.
     * In [124]Finnish, the percent sign is always spaced, and a [125]case
       suffix can be attached to it using the [126]colon (e.g. 50 %:n
       kasvu 'an increase of 50%').^[127][15]
     * In [128]French, the percent sign must be spaced with a non-breaking
       space.^[129][16]^[130][17]
     * According to the Real Academia Española, in [131]Spanish, the
       percent sign should be spaced now, despite the fact that it is not
       the linguistic norm.^[132][18] Despite that, in North American
       Spanish ([133]Mexico and the [134]US), several style guides and
       institutions either recommend the percent sign be written following
       the number without any space between or do so in their own
       publications in accordance with common usage in that
       region.^[135][19]^[136][20]
     * In [137]Russian, the percent sign is rarely spaced, contrary to the
       guidelines of the GOST 8.417-2002 state standard.
     * In [138]Chinese, the percent sign is almost never spaced, probably
       because Chinese does not use spaces to separate characters or words
       at all.^[[139]citation needed]
     * According to the [140]Swedish Language Council, the percent sign
       should be preceded by a space in [141]Swedish, as all other units.
     * In [142]German, the space is prescribed by the regulatory body in
       the national standard [143]DIN 5008.
     * In [144]Turkish and some other [145]Turkic languages, the percent
       sign precedes rather than follows the number, without an
       intervening space.
     * In [146]Persian texts, the percent sign may either precede or
       follow the number, in either case without a space.
     * In [147]Arabic, the percent sign follows the number; as Arabic is
       written from [148]right to left, this means that the percent sign
       is to the left of the number, usually without a space.
     * In [149]Hebrew, the percent sign is written to the right of the
       number, just as in English, without an intervening space. This is
       because numbers in Hebrew (which otherwise is written from right to
       left) are written from left to right, as in English.
     * In [150]Dutch, the official rule ([151]NBN Z 01-002) is to place a
       space between the number and the sign (e.g. "een stijging van
       50 %"), but most of the time, the space is missing (e.g. "een
       stijging van 50%").^[152][21]

Usage in text

   [[153]edit]

   It is often recommended that the percent sign only be used in tables
   and other places with space restrictions. In running text, it should be
   spelled out as percent or per cent (often in newspapers). For example,
   not "Sales increased by 24% over 2006" but "Sales increased by 24
   percent over 2006".^[154][22]^[155][23]^[156][24]

Evolution

   [[157]edit]

   Prior to 1425, there is no known evidence of a special symbol being
   used for percentages. The [158]Italian term per cento, "for a hundred",
   was used as well as several different abbreviations (e.g. "per 100", "p
   100", "p cento"). Examples of this can be seen in the 1339 arithmetic
   text (author unknown) depicted below.^[159][25] The letter p with its
   [160]descender crossed by a horizontal or diagonal stroke (to indicate
   abbreviation) [161]conventionally stood for per, por, par, or pur in
   Medieval and Renaissance [162]palaeography.^[163][26]

          [164]1339 arithmetic text 1339 arithmetic text in Rara
          Arithmetica, p. 437

   At some point, a scribe used the abbreviation pc with a tiny loop or
   circle (depicting the ending -o used in [165]Italian ordinals, as in
   primo, secondo; it is analogous to the English -th as in 25th). This
   appears in some additional pages of a 1425 text which were probably
   added around 1435.^[166][27]

          [167][290px-Percent_sign_in_1425.png] 1425 arithmetic text in
          Rara Arithmetica, p. 440^[168][28]

   The pc with a loop eventually evolved into a horizontal fraction sign
   by 1650 (see below for an example in a 1684 text)^[169][29] and
   thereafter lost the per.^[170][28]

          [171]1684 arithmetic text 1684 arithmetic text in Rara
          Arithmetica, p. 441

   In 1925, D. E. Smith wrote, "The [172]solidus form ([173] A modern
   percentage sign, a diagonal line with a zero on either side ) is
   modern."^[174][30]

Encodings

   [[175]edit]

Unicode

   [[176]edit]

   The [177]Unicode code points are:
     * U+0025 % PERCENT SIGN (HTML &#37;, &percnt;^[178][31]),
     * U+FF05 ％ [179]FULLWIDTH PERCENT SIGN
     * U+FE6A ﹪ [180]SMALL PERCENT SIGN
     * U+066A ٪ ARABIC PERCENT SIGN, which has the circles replaced by
       square dots set on edge, the shape of the digit 0 in [181]Eastern
       Arabic numerals.
     * U+332B ㌫ SQUARE PAASENTO, パーセント (pāsento, from English "percent")
       in one square character.

ASCII

   [[182]edit]

   The [183]ASCII code for the percent [184]character is 37, or 0x25 in
   [185]hexadecimal.

Other uses

   [[186]edit]

In computers

   [[187]edit]

   Names for the percent sign include percent sign (in [188]ITU-T),
   [189]mod, grapes (in [190]hacker [191]jargon),^[[192]citation needed]
   and the humorous double-oh-seven (in [193]INTERCAL).

   In [194]computing, the percent [195]character is also used for the
   [196]modulo operation in [197]programming languages that derive their
   syntax from the [198]C programming language, which in turn acquired
   this usage from the earlier [199]B.^[200][32]

   In the textual representation of [201]URIs, a % immediately followed by
   a 2-digit [202]hexadecimal number denotes an octet specifying (part of)
   a character that might otherwise not be allowed in URIs (see
   [203]percent-encoding).

   In [204]SQL, the percent sign is a [205]wildcard character in "LIKE"
   expressions, for example SELECT * FROM table WHERE fullname LIKE
   'Lisa %' will fetch all records whose names start with "
   Lisa ".

   In [206]TeX (and therefore also in [207]LaTeX) and [208]PostScript, and
   in [209]GNU Octave and [210]MATLAB,^[211][33] a % denotes a line
   [212]comment.

   In [213]BASIC, [214]Visual Basic, [215]ASP, and [216]VBA a trailing %
   after a variable name marks it as an [217]integer.

   In [218]ASP, the percent sign can be used to indicate the start and end
   of the ASP code <%...... %>

   In [219]Perl % is the [220]sigil for [221]hashes.

   In many programming languages' string formatting operations (performed
   by functions such as [222]printf and [223]scanf), the percent sign
   denotes parts of the template string that will be replaced with
   arguments. (See [224]printf format string.) In [225]Python and
   [226]Ruby the percent sign is also used as the string formatting
   operator.^[227][34]^[228][35]^[229][36]

   In the [230]command processors [231]COMMAND.COM (DOS) and [232]CMD.EXE
   (OS/2 and Windows),
   %1, %2,... stand for the first, second,... parameters of a [233]batch
   file. %0 stands for the specification of the batch file itself as typed
   on the command line. The % sign is also used similarly in the FOR
   command. %VAR1% represents the value of an [234]environment variable
   named VAR1. Thus: set PATH=c:\;%PATH% sets a new value for PATH, that
   being the old value preceded by "c:\;". Because these uses give the
   percent sign special meaning, the sequence %% (two percent signs) is
   used to represent a literal percent sign, so that: set
   PATH=c:\;%%PATH%% would set PATH to the literal value "c:\;%PATH%".

   In the [235]C Shell, % is part of the default [236]command prompt.

In linguistics

   [[237]edit]

   In linguistics, the percent sign is prepended to an example sentence or
   other [238]string to show that it is judged [239]well-formed
   (grammatical) by some speakers and ill-formed by others. This may be
   due to differences in [240]dialect or even individual
   [241]idiolects.^[242][37]^[243][38] This use is similar to those of the
   [244]asterisk to mark ill-formed strings, the [245]question mark to
   mark strings where well-formedness is unclear, and the [246]number sign
   to mark strings that are syntactically well-formed but semantically or
   pragmatically nonsensical.
