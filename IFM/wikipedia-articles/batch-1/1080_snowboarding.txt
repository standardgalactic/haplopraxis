   1080° Snowboarding^[105][a] is a [106]snowboarding video game developed
   and published by [107]Nintendo for the [108]Nintendo 64 in 1998. In the
   game, players control one of five snowboarders from a [109]third-person
   perspective, using a combination of buttons to jump and perform tricks
   across eight [110]levels.

   1080° was announced in November 1997 and developed over nine months. It
   received critical acclaim and won an [111]Interactive Achievement Award
   from the [112]Academy of Interactive Arts & Sciences. The game sold
   over two million units. A [113]sequel, [114]1080° Avalanche, was
   released for the [115]GameCube in November 2003. The game was
   re-released for the [116]Wii in 2008 and for the [117]Wii U
   [118]Virtual Console in 2016. It was also re-released on the
   [119]Nintendo Switch Online + Expansion Pack in 2023.

Gameplay

   [[120]edit]
   A screenshot from a match race in 1080° Snowboarding

   Players control snowboarders in various modes. 1080° features two trick
   modes (Trick Attack and Contest),^[121][2] three race modes (Race, Time
   Attack, and 2 Players),^[122][3] a Training mode, and an Options
   mode.^[123][4] The objective of the game is either to reach a level's
   finish line as quickly as possible or to achieve the highest score by
   executing trick combinations.^[124][5]

   In 1080°'s two trick modes, Trick Attack and Contest, players earn
   points by completing tricks.^[125][6] In Contest mode, players perform
   tricks and snowboard past flags to gain points. In Trick Attack mode,
   players must perform a series of tricks throughout a designated level.
   The game includes 24 tricks and 5 secret tricks, all executed through
   combinations of circular movements of the [126]control stick, along
   with the R, Z, and B buttons. Points are awarded based on the
   complexity of the tricks, combos, and the time required.^[127][6] The
   two categories of tricks are grab tricks, where the snowboarder grabs
   the board in a specific manner, and spin tricks, where the snowboarder
   spins the board a certain number of degrees.^[128][5] The 1080° spin,
   the game's most complex trick, requires nine distinct actions to
   perform.^[129][7]

   1080° features three race modes. In these modes, players can achieve
   victory by selecting alternate routes within a course and balancing the
   snowboarder after jumps to avoid losing speed.^[130][8] Although tricks
   are scored in race modes, they do not contribute to winning.^[131][8]

   In Match Race mode, the player competes in a series of races against
   AI-controlled snowboarders.^[132][9]^[133][10] The game tracks the
   player’s time throughout the level, and the player is assigned a damage
   meter that fills if the snowboarder falls or is knocked over. The
   difficulty level in Match Races can be set to normal, hard, or expert,
   affecting both the complexity and the number of races.^[134][10] If the
   player fails to defeat an AI competitor, they must retire. The player
   is given three chances to beat the AI before the game
   ends.^[135][8]^[136][11]

   Players can initially choose from five snowboarders: two from Japan,
   and one each from Canada, the United States, and the United Kingdom.
   Each snowboarder has unique abilities and is suited for different
   levels and modes, as they vary in statistics such as technique, speed,
   and weight.^[137][12]^[138][13]

   Three additional snowboarders can be unlocked by completing certain
   game levels and modes.^[139][13] Players also start with eight
   snowboards available for every character, with one additional snowboard
   unlockable later in the game. Each snowboard is tailored to different
   situations, as they offer varying strengths in areas such as balance
   and edge control.^[140][13]

Development and release

   [[141]edit]

   1080°'s release was announced on 21 November 1997 at Nintendo's
   [142]Space World trade show, with its working title previously being
   Vertical Edge Snowboarding.^[143][14] 1080° was one of several
   snowboarding games released for the Nintendo 64 in 1998, alongside
   titles like [144]Big Mountain 2000 and [145]Snowboard Kids.^[146][14]
   Before its official release, journalists were able to play 1080° at the
   January 1998 Nintendo Gamers' Summit.^[147][15]

   1080° was directed by Masamachi Abe and Misthuro Tanako, programmed by
   English developers [148]Giles Goddard and Colin Reed, and developed and
   published by [149]Nintendo. The game was produced by [150]Shigeru
   Miyamoto.^[151][16] Abe had previously directed [152]Tekken 3 for
   [153]Namco, while Goddard had programmed the Mario face in [154]Super
   Mario 64,^[155][17] which was released two years earlier to critical
   and commercial success. Reed had previously programmed [156]Stunt Race
   FX. According to Miyamoto, the game "came about because I like skiing.
   I was thinking about making a skiing game after completing [157]Wave
   Race [64]. However, the current trend seems to be toward snowboarding.
   With snowboarding, it seems that you can go places that you can't with
   skis; for example, in between trees."^[158][18]

   Development for 1080° took place at Nintendo's headquarters in
   [159]Kyoto, Japan.^[160][19] During development, programmers Goddard
   and Reed used a technique called "skinning" to eliminate joints between
   the polygons that made up the characters. They combined standard
   animation with [161]inverse kinematics to create characters whose
   responses during collisions varied depending on the object hit, the
   direction of the collision, and the speed of impact.^[162][20] All of
   the characters' movements were created using [163]motion
   capture.^[164][10]

   [165]Tommy Hilfiger outfits and Lamar snowboards appear in 1080° as
   part of its [166]product placement.^[167][5] The game's soundtrack,
   composed by Kenta Nagata, features a mix of "techno and rappy beats"
   with "thrashy, foozed-out vocals."^[168][5]^[169][16]

   Development for 1080° started around April or May 1997 and continued
   into early 1998.^[170][20]^[171][21] The game was released in Japan on
   28 February 1998^[172][22] and in North America on 1 April. Nintendo
   prioritized releasing 1080° in Japan while there was still snow on the
   ground.^[173][18] The European release was delayed to align with the
   winter season, hoping to boost sales.^[174][23] 1080° was eventually
   released on 9 October 1998 in Europe and the [175]PAL regions.

   1080° Snowboarding received "generally favorable reviews," according to
   [209]Metacritic, falling just two points short of "universal
   acclaim".^[210][24] In Japan, [211]Famitsu awarded it a score of 31 out
   of 40.^[212][29] [213]Nintendo Power gave a favorable review of the
   Japanese import over a month before the game's U.S. release.^[214][39]
   Josh Smith from [215]GameSpot called it "one of the best values in both
   sports and racing gaming".^[216][31] The game was widely recognized as
   a leader among snowboarding titles at the time.^[217][40] [218]Edge
   described it as the "most convincing video game emulation of the
   snowboarding experience so far," noting its serious tone, which
   contrasted with other Nintendo games.^[219][26]

   The game's graphics were highly praised for their quality on the
   [220]Nintendo 64.^[221][26]^[222][41] Smith lauded the graphics for
   their crispness, smoothness, and detail, with minimal polygon
   dropout.^[223][31] Reviewers also praised the game's camera work,
   realistic physics model,^[224][31] and its depiction of speed. The
   game's snow effects were particularly highlighted, as sun reflections
   varied appropriately between fluffy and packed snow, which also behaved
   differently in-game.^[225][5] Minor graphical issues, such as
   occasional pop-up, misplaced shadows, and slight lag when racers passed
   through trees, were noted but considered
   insignificant.^[226][5]^[227][31]

   Although Edge gave 1080° Snowboarding a generally positive review, it
   found faults with the game's AI, accusing it of "cheating" due to its
   tendency to allow CPU opponents to quickly catch up near the end of
   races. The review also criticized the AI's simplicity, noting that the
   CPU followed a "limited series of predetermined routes," making it
   possible for players to predict when the AI would fall over, which
   allowed them to pass with little satisfaction.^[228][26] Edge also
   criticized the delay in the game's PAL release, calling it "frankly
   ludicrous" and suggested that, with Nintendo's limited number of major
   releases at the time, any quality title was likely to top the charts
   easily.^[229][27]

   Other publications were more enthusiastic. [230]Next Generation stated
   that 1080° Snowboarding set the standard for the snowboarding
   genre,^[231][36] while [232]Hyper compared it to how Wave Race 64
   revolutionized water-based racing, praising its ability to capture the
   excitement of snowboarding.^[233][32] Similarly, [234]GamePro urged
   gamers to "snap [the game] up as soon as it's out."^[235][42]

   The control scheme was another point of praise. Shawn Sackenheim of
   [236]AllGame considered the game's "highly technical" controls to be a
   strength, despite their initial difficulty.^[237][43] [238]Computer and
   Video Games reviewer Alex Huhtala agreed, praising the controls and
   dismissing the notion of difficulty, stating they were so
   well-implemented that "you’re able to play perfectly well with just one
   hand on the stick and Z button."^[239][41] GameSpot also complimented
   the "thoroughly involving" controls, emphasizing the crouch move for
   tight turns.^[240][31]

   The game's music was widely appreciated, with [241]IGN describing it as
   "a shining example of what can be achieved on the format,"^[242][5]
   while Sackenheim called it "one of the best N64 soundtracks to
   date."^[243][43] Both reviewers also praised the sound effects.

   In a 2006 retrospective, [244]Official Nintendo Magazine hailed 1080°
   Snowboarding for having the best video game representation of snow at
   the time and commended its sound effects and handling. Steve Jarratt
   described it as a "stunt-free but fast and fun" experience,
   highlighting its multiplayer quality.^[245][44] The magazine ranked it
   as the 87th best game available on Nintendo platforms, noting its
   realism as a snowboarding game.^[246][45]

   1080° Snowboarding received praise for its soundtrack, particularly its
   use of [247]drum and bass music. In a 2023 retrospective, [248]NME's
   Mat Ombler commended the game's soundtrack, noting that it helped
   introduce underground electronic club music, including drum and bass,
   to a broader audience, similar to the impact of the [249]Wipeout
   series.^[250][46]

   The game also earned critical accolades, winning the [251]AIAS'
   [252]Console Sports Game of the Year award at the [253]2nd Annual
   Interactive Achievement Awards.^[254][47] It was also nominated for
   Best Nintendo 64 Game at the 1998 [255]CNET Gamecenter Awards, but lost
   to [256]The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time.^[257][48]

   Commercially, 1080° Snowboarding was a significant success in the
   United States. According to [258]PC Data, the game sold 817,529 units
   and generated $40.9 million in revenue by the end of 1998, making it
   the seventh-best-selling Nintendo 64 game of the year.^[259][49] Over
   its lifetime, the game sold 1,230,000 units in the U.S. and over 23,000
   in Japan.^[260][50] However, it did not surpass the commercial success
   of the developers' previous game, Wave Race 64, which sold 1,950,000
   units in the U.S. and 154,000 in Japan.^[261][50]

   1080° Snowboarding was later re-released on the [262]Wii's [263]Virtual
   Console service in 2008 and on the [264]Wii U in 2016.^[265][51] It was
   also made available on the [266]Nintendo Switch Online + Expansion Pack
   on December 7, 2023.^[267][52]

Sequel

   [[268]edit]

   [269]1080° Avalanche, the sequel to 1080° Snowboarding, was released
   for the [270]GameCube in 2003. Unlike its predecessor, the sequel
   received mixed reviews. Greg Kasavin of [271]GameSpot provided a more
   critical assessment, citing "frame rate issues and limited gameplay" as
   major drawbacks that detracted from the experience.^[272][53] Despite
   its graphical improvements and new features, 1080° Avalanche was unable
   to match the critical success of the original 1080° Snowboarding.
