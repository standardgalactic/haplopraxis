   The ampersand, also known as the and sign, is the [136]logogram &,
   representing the [137]conjunction "and". It originated as a
   [138]ligature of the letters of the word et ([139]Latin for
   "and").^[140][1]

Etymology

   [[141]edit]

     Ampersand: the sign &; the name being a [142]corruption of 'and
     [143]per se = and'; i.e. '& by itself = and'. The sign derives from
     the scribes' [144]ligature for the [145]Latin: et; in certain italic
     versions, the letters e and t are clearly distinguishable.

   — Geoffrey Glaister, Glossary of the Book^[146][2]

   Traditionally in English, when spelling aloud, any letter that could
   also be used as a word in itself ("A", "I", and "[147]O") was referred
   to by the [148]Latin expression [149]per se ('by itself'), as in "per
   se A" or "A per se A".^[150][3]^[151][4]^[152][5] The character &, when
   used by itself as opposed to more extended forms such as &c., was
   similarly referred to as "and per se and".^[153][6]^[154][7] This last
   phrase was routinely slurred to "ampersand", and the term had entered
   common English usage by 1837.^[155][4]^[156][8]^[157][9]

   It has been [158]falsely claimed that [159]André-Marie Ampère used the
   symbol in his widely read publications and that people began calling
   the new shape "Ampère's and".^[160][10]

   The ampersand can be traced back to the 1st century AD and the old
   [171]Roman cursive, in which the letters E and T occasionally were
   written together to form a [172]ligature (Evolution of the ampersand –
   figure 1). In the later and more flowing New Roman Cursive, ligatures
   of all kinds were extremely common; figures 2 and 3 from the middle of
   4th century are examples of how the et-ligature could look in this
   script. During the later development of the Latin script leading up to
   [173]Carolingian minuscule (9th century) the use of ligatures in
   general diminished. The et-ligature, however, continued to be used and
   gradually became more stylized and less revealing of its origin
   (figures 4–6).^[174][11]

   The modern [175]italic type ampersand is a kind of "[176]et" ligature
   that goes back to the cursive scripts developed during the
   [177]Renaissance. After the advent of [178]printing in Europe in 1455,
   printers made extensive use of both the italic and Roman ampersands.
   Since the ampersand's roots go back to Roman times, many languages that
   use a variation of the [179]Latin alphabet make use of it.

   The ampersand often appeared as a character at the end of the Latin
   alphabet, as for example in [180]Byrhtferð's list of letters from
   1011.^[181][12] Similarly, & was regarded as the 27th letter of the
   [182]English alphabet, as taught to children in the US and elsewhere.
   An example may be seen in M. B. Moore's 1863 book The Dixie Primer, for
   the Little Folks.^[183][13] In her 1859 novel [184]Adam Bede,
   [185]George Eliot refers to this when she makes Jacob Storey say: "He
   thought it [Z] had only been put to finish off th' alphabet like;
   though ampusand would ha' done as well, for what he could
   see."^[186][14] The popular nursery rhyme [187]Apple Pie ABC finishes
   with the lines "X, Y, Z, and ampersand, All wished for a piece in
   hand".

Similar characters

   [[188]edit]
   [189][170px-Pay_and_Display_sign_with_Tironian_et_for_Irish_agus.jpg]
   Bilingual Irish street sign, with parallel agus and ampersand.

   In [190]Irish and [191]Scottish Gaelic, the character ⁊ (U+204A
   ⁊ [192]TIRONIAN SIGN ET) is used in place of the ampersand. This
   character is a survival of [193]Tironian notes, a [194]medieval
   [195]shorthand system. This character is known as the Tironian Et in
   English, the agus in Irish, and the agusan in Scottish Gaelic.

   The [196]logical conjunction symbol, ∧, is often pronounced "and," but
   is not related to the ampersand.

Writing the ampersand

   [[197]edit]

   In everyday [198]handwriting, the ampersand is sometimes simplified in
   design as a large lowercase [199]epsilon Ɛ or a reversed numeral 3,
   superimposed by a vertical line.^[200][15] The ampersand is also
   sometimes shown as an epsilon with a vertical line above and below it
   or a dot above and below it.^[201][15]

   The [202]plus sign + (itself based on an et-ligature^[203][16]) is
   often informally used in place of an ampersand, sometimes with an added
   loop and resembling ɬ.^[[204]citation needed] Other times it is a
   single stroke with a diagonal line connecting the bottom to the left
   side. This was a version of shorthand for ampersand, and the stroke
   economy of this version provided ease of writing for workers while also
   assuring the character was distinct from other numeric or alphabetic
   symbols.

Usage

   [[211]edit]

   Ampersands are commonly seen in business names formed from a
   partnership of two or more people, such as [212]Johnson & Johnson,
   [213]Dolce & Gabbana, [214]Marks & Spencer, and [215]Tiffany & Co., as
   well as some abbreviations containing the word and, such as AT&T
   ([216]American Telephone and Telegraph), [217]A&P (supermarkets),
   [218]P&O (originally "Peninsular and Oriental", shipping and logistics
   company), R&D ([219]research and development), D&B ([220]drum and
   bass), D&D ([221]Dungeons & Dragons), R&B ([222]rhythm and blues), B&B
   ([223]bed and breakfast), and P&L ([224]profit and
   loss).^[225][17]^[226][18]

   In [227]film credits for stories, [228]screenplays, etc., & indicates a
   closer collaboration than and. The ampersand is used by the
   [229]Writers Guild of America to denote two writers collaborating on a
   specific script, rather than one writer rewriting another's work. In
   screenplays, two authors joined with & collaborated on the script,
   while two authors joined with and worked on the script at different
   times and may not have consulted each other at all.^[230][19]^[231][20]
   In the latter case, they both contributed enough significant material
   to the screenplay to receive credit but did not work together. As a
   result, both & and and may appear in the same credit, as appropriate to
   how the writing proceeded.

   In [232]APA style, the ampersand is used when citing sources in text
   such as (Jones & Jones, 2005). In the list of references, an ampersand
   precedes the last author's name when there is more than one
   author.^[233][21] (This does not apply to [234]MLA style, which calls
   for the "and" to be spelled.^[235][22])

   The phrase [236]et cetera ("and the rest"), usually written as etc. can
   be abbreviated &c. representing the combination et + c(etera).

   The ampersand can be used to indicate that the "and" in a listed item
   is a part of the item's name and not a separator (e.g. "Rock, pop,
   rhythm & blues and hip hop").^[[237]citation needed]

   The ampersand may still be used as an abbreviation for "and" in
   informal writing regardless of how "and" is used.

Computing

   [[238]edit]

Encoding and display

   [[239]edit]

   The [240]character in [241]Unicode is U+0026
   & AMPERSAND (&amp;, &AMP;); this is inherited from the same value in
   [242]ASCII.

   Apart from this, Unicode also has the following variants:
     * U+FE60 ﹠ SMALL AMPERSAND (fullwidth CJK ideograph for East Asian
       typography)
     * U+FF06 ＆ FULLWIDTH AMPERSAND (in block [243]Halfwidth and Fullwidth
       Forms)
     * U+214B ⅋ TURNED AMPERSAND (in block [244]Letterlike Symbols)
     * U+1F670 🙰 SCRIPT LIGATURE ET ORNAMENT
     * U+1F671 🙱 HEAVY SCRIPT LIGATURE ET ORNAMENT
     * U+1F672 🙲 LIGATURE OPEN ET ORNAMENT
     * U+1F673 🙳 HEAVY LIGATURE OPEN ET ORNAMENT
     * U+1F674 🙴 HEAVY AMPERSAND ORNAMENT
     * U+1F675 🙵 SWASH AMPERSAND ORNAMENT

   The last six of these are carryovers from the [245]Wingdings fonts, and
   are meant only for backward compatibility with those fonts.

   On the [246]QWERTY [247]keyboard layout, the ampersand is
   ⇧ Shift+7. It is almost always available on keyboard layouts, sometimes
   on
   ⇧ Shift+6 or
   ⇧ Shift+8. On the [248]AZERTY keyboard layout,

   & is an unmodified keystroke, positioned above A.

   In [249]URLs, the ampersand must be replaced by %26 when representing a
   string character to avoid interpretation as a [250]URL syntax
   character.

Programming languages

   [[251]edit]

   This section needs additional citations for [252]verification. Please
   help [253]improve this article by [254]adding citations to reliable
   sources in this section. Unsourced material may be challenged and
   removed.
   Find sources: [255]"Ampersand" – [256]news · [257]newspapers ·
   [258]books · [259]scholar · [260]JSTOR (July 2020) ([261]Learn how and
   when to remove this message)

   In the 20th century, following the development of [262]formal logic,
   the ampersand became a commonly used logical notation for the
   [263]binary operator or [264]sentential connective [265]AND. This usage
   was adopted in computing.

   Many languages with syntax derived from [266]C, including [267]C++,
   [268]Perl,^[269][23] and more differentiate between:
     * & for [270]bitwise AND (4 & 2) is zero, (4 & 5) is 4.
     * && for [271]short-circuit [272]logical AND (4 && 2) is
       true.^[273][a]

   In [274]C, [275]C++,^[276][24] and [277]Go,^[278][25] a prefix & is a
   unary operator denoting the [279]address in memory of the argument,
   e.g. &x, &func, &a[3].

   In [280]C++ and [281]PHP, unary prefix & before a [282]formal parameter
   of a [283]function denotes [284]pass-by-reference.^[285][26]^[286][27]

   In [287]Pascal, the & as the first character of an identifier prevents
   the compiler from treating it as a keyword, thus [288]escaping it.

   In [289]Fortran, the ampersand forces the compiler to treat two lines
   as one. This is accomplished by placing an ampersand at the end of the
   first line and at the beginning of the second line.^[290][28]

   In many implementations of [291]ALGOL 60 the ampersand denotes the tens
   exponent of a real number.^[[292]citation needed]

   In [293]Common Lisp, the ampersand is the prefix for lambda list
   keywords.^[294][29]

   Ampersand is the [295]string concatenation operator in many [296]BASIC
   dialects, [297]AppleScript, [298]Lingo, [299]HyperTalk, and
   [300]FileMaker.^[[301]citation needed] In [302]Ada it applies to all
   one-dimensional arrays, not just strings.^[[303]citation needed]

   [304]BASIC-PLUS on the [305]DEC [306]PDP-11 uses the ampersand as a
   short form of the verb PRINT.^[[307]citation needed]

   [308]Applesoft BASIC used the ampersand as an internal command, not
   intended to be used for general programming, that invoked a
   [309]machine language program in the computer's
   [310]ROM.^[[311]citation needed]

   In some versions of BASIC, unary suffix & denotes a variable is of type
   long, or 32 [312]bits in length.^[[313]citation needed]

   The ampersand was occasionally used as a prefix to denote a
   [314]hexadecimal number, such as &FF for decimal 255, for instance in
   [315]BBC BASIC.^[[316]citation needed] (The modern convention is to use
   "x" as a prefix to denote hexadecimal, thus xFF.) Some other languages,
   such as the [317]Monitor built into ROM on the [318]Commodore 128, used
   it to indicate [319]octal instead, a convention that spread throughout
   the Commodore community and is now used in the [320]VICE
   emulator.^[321][30]

   In [322]MySQL, & has dual roles. As well as a logical AND, it serves as
   the bitwise operator of an intersection between elements.^[323][31]

   [324]Dyalog APL uses ampersand similarly to [325]Unix shells, spawning
   a separate [326]green thread upon application of a
   function.^[[327]citation needed]

   In more recent years, the ampersand has made its way into the
   [328]Haskell standard library, representing flipped [329]function
   application: x & f means the same thing as f x.^[330][32]

   [331]Perl uses the ampersand as a [332]sigil to refer to subroutines:
     * In Perl 4 and earlier, it was effectively required to call
       user-defined subroutines^[333][33]
     * In Perl 5, it can still be used to modify the way user-defined
       subroutines are called^[334][34]
     * In [335]Raku (formerly known as Perl 6), the ampersand [336]sigil
       is only used when referring to a subroutine as an object, never
       when calling it^[337][35]

   In MASM 80x86 Assembly Language, & is the Substitution Operator, which
   tells the assembler to replace a macro parameter or text macro name
   with its actual value.^[338][36]

   Ampersand is the name of a reactive programming language, which uses
   [339]relation algebra to specify [340]information systems.^[341][37]

Text markup

   [[342]edit]

   In [343]SGML, [344]XML, and [345]HTML, the ampersand is used to
   introduce an [346]SGML entity, such as &nbsp; (for non-breaking space)
   or &alpha; (for the Greek letter α). The HTML and XML encoding for the
   ampersand character is the entity &amp;.^[347][38] This can create a
   problem known as [348]delimiter collision when converting text into one
   of these markup languages. For instance, when putting URLs or other
   material containing ampersands into XML format files such as [349]RSS
   files the & must be replaced with &amp; or they are considered not well
   formed, and computers will be unable to read the files correctly. SGML
   derived the use from [350]IBM Generalized Markup Language, which was
   one of many IBM-mainframe languages to use the ampersand to signal a
   text substitution, eventually going back to [351]System/360 macro
   assembly language.

   In the plain [352]TeX [353]markup language, the ampersand is used to
   mark [354]tabstops. The ampersand itself can be applied in TeX with \&.
   The [355]Computer Modern fonts replace it with an "E.T." symbol in the
   cmti# (text italic) fonts, so it can be entered as {\it\&} in running
   text when using the default (Computer Modern) fonts.^[356][39]

   In [357]Microsoft Windows menus, labels, and other captions, the
   ampersand is used to denote the next letter as a keyboard shortcut
   (called an "Access key" by Microsoft).^[358][40] For instance setting a
   button label to "&Print" makes it display as Print and for
   [359]Alt+P to be a shortcut equivalent to pressing that button. A
   double ampersand is needed in order to display a real ampersand. This
   convention originated in the first WIN32 api, and is used in
   [360]Windows Forms,^[361][40] (but not WPF, which uses underscore _ for
   this purpose) and is also copied into many other toolkits on multiple
   operating systems. Sometimes this causes problems similar to other
   programs that fail to sanitize markup from user input, for instance
   [362]Navision databases have trouble if this character is in either
   "Text" or "Code" fields.

Unix shells

   [[363]edit]

   Some Unix shells use the ampersand as a [364]metacharacter:

   Some Unix shells, like the [365]POSIX standard sh shell, use an
   ampersand to [366]execute a process in the background and to duplicate
   [367]file descriptors.
     * In [368]Bash, the ampersand can separate words, control the command
       history, duplicate file descriptors, perform logical operations,
       control jobs, and participate in [369]regular
       expressions.^[370][41]

Web standards

   [[371]edit]

   The generic [372]URL (Uniform Resource Locator) syntax allows for a
   [373]query string to be appended to a file name in a web address so
   that additional information can be passed to a script; the
   [374]question mark, or query mark, ?, is used to indicate the start of
   a query string.^[375][42] A query string is usually made up of a number
   of different [376]name–value pairs, each separated by the ampersand
   symbol, &. For example,
   http://www.example.org/list.php?order=ascending&year=2025.
