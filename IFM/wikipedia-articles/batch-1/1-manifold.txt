   From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

   Mathematical idealization of the trace left by a moving point
   For other uses, see [138]Curve (disambiguation).
   [139][220px-Parabola.svg.png] A [140]parabola, one of the simplest
   curves, after (straight) lines

   In [141]mathematics, a curve (also called a curved line in older texts)
   is an object similar to a [142]line, but that does not have to be
   [143]straight.

   Intuitively, a curve may be thought of as the trace left by a moving
   [144]point. This is the definition that appeared more than 2000 years
   ago in [145]Euclid's Elements: "The [curved] line^[146][a] is […] the
   first species of quantity, which has only one dimension, namely length,
   without any width nor depth, and is nothing else than the flow or run
   of the point which […] will leave from its imaginary moving some
   vestige in length, exempt of any width."^[147][1]

   This definition of a curve has been formalized in modern mathematics
   as: A curve is the [148]image of an [149]interval to a [150]topological
   space by a [151]continuous function. In some contexts, the function
   that defines the curve is called a parametrization, and the curve is a
   [152]parametric curve. In this article, these curves are sometimes
   called topological curves to distinguish them from more constrained
   curves such as [153]differentiable curves. This definition encompasses
   most curves that are studied in mathematics; notable exceptions are
   [154]level curves (which are [155]unions of curves and isolated
   points), and [156]algebraic curves (see below). Level curves and
   algebraic curves are sometimes called [157]implicit curves, since they
   are generally defined by [158]implicit equations.

   Nevertheless, the class of topological curves is very broad, and
   contains some curves that do not look as one may expect for a curve, or
   even cannot be drawn. This is the case of [159]space-filling curves and
   [160]fractal curves. For ensuring more regularity, the function that
   defines a curve is often supposed to be [161]differentiable, and the
   curve is then said to be a [162]differentiable curve.

   A [163]plane algebraic curve is the [164]zero set of a [165]polynomial
   in two [166]indeterminates. More generally, an [167]algebraic curve is
   the zero set of a finite set of polynomials, which satisfies the
   further condition of being an [168]algebraic variety of [169]dimension
   one. If the coefficients of the polynomials belong to a [170]field k,
   the curve is said to be defined over k. In the common case of a
   [171]real algebraic curve, where k is the field of [172]real numbers,
   an algebraic curve is a finite union of topological curves. When
   [173]complex zeros are considered, one has a complex algebraic curve,
   which, from the [174]topological point of view, is not a curve, but a
   [175]surface, and is often called a [176]Riemann surface. Although not
   being curves in the common sense, algebraic curves defined over other
   fields have been widely studied. In particular, algebraic curves over a
   [177]finite field are widely used in modern [178]cryptography.

History

   [[179]edit]
   [180][225px-Newgrange_Entrance_Stone.jpg] [181]Megalithic art from
   [182]Newgrange showing an early interest in curves

   Interest in curves began long before they were the subject of
   mathematical study. This can be seen in numerous examples of their
   decorative use in art and on everyday objects dating back to
   prehistoric times.^[183][2] Curves, or at least their graphical
   representations, are simple to create, for example with a stick on the
   sand on a beach.

   Historically, the term line was used in place of the more modern term
   curve. Hence the terms straight line and right line were used to
   distinguish what are today called lines from curved lines. For example,
   in Book I of [184]Euclid's Elements, a line is defined as a
   "breadthless length" (Def. 2), while a straight line is defined as "a
   line that lies evenly with the points on itself" (Def. 4). Euclid's
   idea of a line is perhaps clarified by the statement "The extremities
   of a line are points," (Def. 3).^[185][3] Later commentators further
   classified lines according to various schemes. For example:^[186][4]
     * Composite lines (lines forming an angle)
     * Incomposite lines
          + Determinate (lines that do not extend indefinitely, such as
            the circle)
          + Indeterminate (lines that extend indefinitely, such as the
            straight line and the parabola)

   [187][225px-Conic_sections_with_plane.svg.png] The curves created by
   slicing a cone ([188]conic sections) were among the curves studied in
   ancient [189]Greek mathematics.

   The Greek [190]geometers had studied many other kinds of curves. One
   reason was their interest in solving geometrical problems that could
   not be solved using standard [191]compass and straightedge
   construction. These curves include:
     * The conic sections, studied in depth by [192]Apollonius of Perga
     * The [193]cissoid of Diocles, studied by [194]Diocles and used as a
       method to [195]double the cube.^[196][5]
     * The [197]conchoid of Nicomedes, studied by [198]Nicomedes as a
       method to both double the cube and to [199]trisect an
       angle.^[200][6]
     * The [201]Archimedean spiral, studied by [202]Archimedes as a method
       to trisect an angle and [203]square the circle.^[204][7]
     * The [205]spiric sections, sections of [206]tori studied by
       [207]Perseus as sections of cones had been studied by Apollonius.

   [208][225px-Folium_Of_Descartes.svg.png] Analytic geometry allowed
   curves, such as the [209]Folium of Descartes, to be defined using
   equations instead of geometrical construction.

   A fundamental advance in the theory of curves was the introduction of
   [210]analytic geometry by [211]René Descartes in the seventeenth
   century. This enabled a curve to be described using an equation rather
   than an elaborate geometrical construction. This not only allowed new
   curves to be defined and studied, but it enabled a formal distinction
   to be made between [212]algebraic curves that can be defined using
   [213]polynomial equations, and [214]transcendental curves that cannot.
   Previously, curves had been described as "geometrical" or "mechanical"
   according to how they were, or supposedly could be, generated.^[215][2]

   Conic sections were applied in [216]astronomy by [217]Kepler. Newton
   also worked on an early example in the [218]calculus of variations.
   Solutions to variational problems, such as the [219]brachistochrone and
   [220]tautochrone questions, introduced properties of curves in new ways
   (in this case, the [221]cycloid). The [222]catenary gets its name as
   the solution to the problem of a hanging chain, the sort of question
   that became routinely accessible by means of [223]differential
   calculus.

   In the eighteenth century came the beginnings of the theory of plane
   algebraic curves, in general. Newton had studied the [224]cubic curves,
   in the general description of the real points into 'ovals'. The
   statement of [225]Bézout's theorem showed a number of aspects which
   were not directly accessible to the geometry of the time, to do with
   singular points and complex solutions.

   Since the nineteenth century, curve theory is viewed as the special
   case of dimension one of the theory of [226]manifolds and
   [227]algebraic varieties. Nevertheless, many questions remain specific
   to curves, such as [228]space-filling curves, [229]Jordan curve theorem
   and [230]Hilbert's sixteenth problem.

   cross itself and has no missing points" (a continuous
   non-self-intersecting curve).^[245][8]

   {\displaystyle X} is at least three-dimensional; a skew curve is a
   space curve which lies in no plane. These definitions of plane, space
   and skew curves apply also to [249]real algebraic curves, although the
   above definition of a curve does not apply (a real algebraic curve may
   be [250]disconnected).
   [251][220px-Fractal_dragon_curve.jpg] A [252]dragon curve with a
   positive area

   A plane simple closed curve is also called a Jordan curve. It is also
   defined as a non-self-intersecting [253]continuous loop in the
   plane.^[254][9] The [255]Jordan curve theorem states that the [256]set
   complement in a plane of a Jordan curve consists of two [257]connected
   components (that is the curve divides the plane in two non-intersecting
   [258]regions that are both connected). The bounded region inside a
   Jordan curve is known as Jordan domain.

   The definition of a curve includes figures that can hardly be called
   curves in common usage. For example, the image of a curve can cover a
   [259]square in the plane ([260]space-filling curve), and a simple curve
   may have a positive area.^[261][10] [262]Fractal curves can have
   properties that are strange for the common sense. For example, a
   fractal curve can have a [263]Hausdorff dimension bigger than one (see
   [264]Koch snowflake) and even a positive area. An example is the
   [265]dragon curve, which has many other unusual properties.

Differentiable arc

   [[272]edit]
   "Arc (geometry)" redirects here. For the use in finite projective
   geometry, see [273]Arc (projective geometry). For other uses, see
   [274]Arc (disambiguation).

   In [275]Euclidean geometry, an arc (symbol: ⌒) is a [276]connected
   subset of a [277]differentiable curve.

   Arcs of [278]lines are called [279]segments, [280]rays, or [281]lines,
   depending on how they are bounded.

   A common curved example is an arc of a [282]circle, called a
   [283]circular arc.

   In a [284]sphere (or a [285]spheroid), an arc of a [286]great circle
   (or a [287]great ellipse) is called a great arc.

Algebraic curve

   [[319]edit]

   Main article: [320]Algebraic curve

   Algebraic curves are the curves considered in [321]algebraic geometry.
   A plane algebraic curve is the [322]set of the points of coordinates x,
   y such that f(x, y) = 0, where f is a polynomial in two variables
   defined over some field F. One says that the curve is defined over F.
   Algebraic geometry normally considers not only points with coordinates
   in F but all the points with coordinates in an [323]algebraically
   closed field K.

   If C is a curve defined by a polynomial f with coefficients in F, the
   curve is said to be defined over F.

   In the case of a curve defined over the [324]real numbers, one normally
   considers points with [325]complex coordinates. In this case, a point
   with real coordinates is a real point, and the set of all real points
   is the real part of the curve. It is therefore only the real part of an
   algebraic curve that can be a topological curve (this is not always the
   case, as the real part of an algebraic curve may be disconnected and
   contain isolated points). The whole curve, that is the set of its
   complex point is, from the topological point of view a surface. In
   particular, the nonsingular complex projective algebraic curves are
   called [326]Riemann surfaces.

   The points of a curve C with coordinates in a field G are said to be
   rational over G and can be denoted C(G). When G is the field of the
   [327]rational numbers, one simply talks of rational points. For
   example, [328]Fermat's Last Theorem may be restated as: For n > 2,
   every rational point of the [329]Fermat curve of degree n has a zero
   coordinate.

   Algebraic curves can also be space curves, or curves in a space of
   higher dimension, say n. They are defined as [330]algebraic varieties
   of [331]dimension one. They may be obtained as the common solutions of
   at least n–1 polynomial equations in n variables. If n–1 polynomials
   are sufficient to define a curve in a space of dimension n, the curve
   is said to be a [332]complete intersection. By eliminating variables
   (by any tool of [333]elimination theory), an algebraic curve may be
   projected onto a [334]plane algebraic curve, which however may
   introduce new singularities such as [335]cusps or [336]double points.

   A plane curve may also be completed to a curve in the [337]projective
   plane: if a curve is defined by a polynomial f of total degree d, then
   w^df(u/w, v/w) simplifies to a [338]homogeneous polynomial g(u, v, w)
   of degree d. The values of u, v, w such that g(u, v, w) = 0 are the
   homogeneous coordinates of the points of the completion of the curve in
   the projective plane and the points of the initial curve are those such
   that w is not zero. An example is the Fermat curve u^n + v^n = w^n,
   which has an affine form x^n + y^n = 1. A similar process of
   homogenization may be defined for curves in higher dimensional spaces.

   Except for [339]lines, the simplest examples of algebraic curves are
   the [340]conics, which are nonsingular curves of degree two and
   [341]genus zero. [342]Elliptic curves, which are nonsingular curves of
   genus one, are studied in [343]number theory, and have important
   applications to [344]cryptography.
