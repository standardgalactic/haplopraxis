   Haumea ([284]minor-planet designation: 136108 Haumea) is a [285]dwarf
   planet located [286]beyond [287]Neptune's orbit.^[288][25] It was
   discovered in 2004 by a team headed by [289]Mike Brown of [290]Caltech
   at the [291]Palomar Observatory, and formally announced in 2005 by a
   team headed by [292]José Luis Ortiz Moreno at the [293]Sierra Nevada
   Observatory in [294]Spain, who had discovered it that year in precovery
   images taken by the team in 2003. From that announcement, it received
   the provisional designation 2003 EL61.

   On 17 September 2008, it was named after [295]Haumea, the Hawaiian
   goddess of childbirth, under the expectation by the [296]International
   Astronomical Union (IAU) that it would prove to be a dwarf planet.
   Nominal estimates make it the [297]third-largest known trans-Neptunian
   object, after [298]Eris and [299]Pluto, and approximately the size of
   Uranus's moon [300]Titania. Precovery images of Haumea have been
   identified back to 22 March 1955.^[301][9]

   Haumea's mass is about one-third that of Pluto and 1/1400 that of
   [302]Earth. Although its shape has not been directly observed,
   calculations from its [303]light curve are consistent with it being a
   [304]Jacobi ellipsoid (the shape it would be if it were a dwarf
   planet), with its major [305]axis twice as long as its minor. In
   October 2017, astronomers announced the discovery of a [306]ring system
   around Haumea, representing the first ring system discovered for a
   [307]trans-Neptunian object and a dwarf planet.

   Haumea's gravity was until recently thought to be sufficient for it to
   have relaxed into [308]hydrostatic equilibrium, though that is now
   unclear. Haumea's elongated shape together with its rapid
   [309]rotation, rings, and high [310]albedo (from a surface of
   crystalline water ice), are thought to be the consequences of a
   [311]giant collision, which left Haumea the largest member of a
   [312]collisional family (the [313]Haumea family) that includes several
   large [314]trans-Neptunian objects and Haumea's two known moons,
   [315]Hiʻiaka and Namaka.

History

   [[316]edit]

Discovery

   [[317]edit]

   Main article: [318]Controversy over the discovery of Haumea

   Two teams claim credit for the discovery of Haumea. A team consisting
   of [319]Mike Brown of Caltech, [320]David Rabinowitz of Yale
   University, and [321]Chad Trujillo of [322]Gemini Observatory in Hawaii
   discovered Haumea on 28 December 2004, on images they had taken on 6
   May 2004. On 20 July 2005, they published an online abstract of a
   report intended to announce the discovery at a conference in September
   2005.^[323][26]

   At around this time, [324]José Luis Ortiz Moreno and his team at the
   Instituto de Astrofísica de Andalucía at Sierra Nevada Observatory in
   Spain found Haumea on images taken on 7–10 March 2003.^[325][27] Ortiz
   emailed the [326]Minor Planet Center with their discovery on the night
   of 27 July 2005.^[327][27]

   Brown initially conceded discovery credit to Ortiz,^[328][28] but came
   to suspect the Spanish team of fraud upon learning that the Spanish
   observatory had accessed Brown's observation logs the day before the
   discovery announcement, a fact that they did not disclose in the
   announcement as would be customary. Those logs included enough
   information to allow the Ortiz team to [329]precover Haumea in their
   2003 images, and they were accessed again just before Ortiz scheduled
   telescope time to obtain confirmation images for a second announcement
   to the MPC on 29 July. Ortiz later admitted he had accessed the Caltech
   observation logs but denied any wrongdoing, stating he was merely
   verifying whether they had discovered a new object.^[330][29]

   IAU protocol is that discovery credit for a [331]minor planet goes to
   whoever first submits a report to the MPC ([332]Minor Planet Center)
   with enough positional data for a decent determination of its orbit,
   and that the credited discoverer has priority in choosing a name.
   However, the IAU announcement on 17 September 2008, that Haumea had
   been named by a dual committee established for bodies expected to be
   dwarf planets, did not mention a discoverer. The location of discovery
   was listed as the Sierra Nevada Observatory of the Spanish
   team,^[333][30]^[334][31] but the chosen name, Haumea, was the Caltech
   proposal. Ortiz's team had proposed "[335]Ataecina", the ancient
   Iberian goddess of spring;^[336][27] as a [337]chthonic deity, it would
   have been appropriate for a [338]plutino, which Haumea was not.

Name and symbol

   [[339]edit]

   Until it was given a permanent name, the Caltech discovery team used
   the nickname "[340]Santa" among themselves, because they had discovered
   Haumea on 28 December 2004, just after Christmas.^[341][32] The Spanish
   team were the first to file a claim for discovery to the [342]Minor
   Planet Center, in July 2005. On 29 July 2005, Haumea was given the
   [343]provisional designation 2003 EL[61], based on the date of the
   Spanish discovery image. On 7 September 2006, it was numbered and
   admitted into the official minor planet catalog as (136108)
   2003 EL[61].

   Following [344]guidelines established at the time by the IAU that
   [345]classical Kuiper belt objects be given names of mythological
   beings associated with creation,^[346][33] in September 2006 the
   Caltech team submitted formal names from [347]Hawaiian mythology to the
   IAU for both (136108) 2003 EL[61] and its moons, in order "to pay
   homage to the place where the satellites were discovered".^[348][34]
   The names were proposed by [349]David Rabinowitz of the Caltech
   team.^[350][25] [351]Haumea is the matron goddess of the island of
   [352]Hawaiʻi, where the [353]Mauna Kea Observatory is located. In
   addition, she is identified with [354]Papa, the goddess of the earth
   and wife of [355]Wākea (space),^[356][35] which, at the time, seemed
   appropriate because Haumea was thought to be composed almost entirely
   of solid rock, without the thick ice mantle over a small rocky core
   typical of other known Kuiper belt objects.^[357][36]^[358][37] Lastly,
   Haumea is the goddess of fertility and childbirth, with many children
   who sprang from different parts of her body;^[359][35] this corresponds
   to the swarm of icy bodies thought to have broken off the main body
   during an ancient collision.^[360][37] The two known moons, also
   believed to have formed in this manner,^[361][37] are thus named after
   two of Haumea's daughters, [362]Hiʻiaka and [363]Nāmaka.^[364][36]

   The proposal by the Ortiz team, Ataecina, did not meet IAU naming
   requirements, because the names of [365]chthonic deities were reserved
   for stably [366]resonant trans-Neptunian objects such as [367]plutinos
   that resonate 3:2 with Neptune, whereas Haumea was in an intermittent
   7:12 resonance and so by some definitions was not a resonant body. The
   naming criteria would be clarified in late 2019, when the IAU decided
   that chthonic figures were to be used specifically for plutinos. (See
   [368]Ataecina § Dwarf planet.)

   A [369]planetary symbol for Haumea, ⟨[370] 🝻 ⟩, is included in
   [371]Unicode at U+1F77B.^[372][38] Planetary symbols are no longer much
   used in astronomy, and 🝻 is mostly used by astrologers,^[373][39] but
   has also been used by NASA.^[374][40] The symbol was designed by Denis
   Moskowitz, a software engineer in Massachusetts; it combines and
   simplifies Hawaiian petroglyphs meaning 'woman' and
   'childbirth'.^[375][41]

Orbit

   [[376]edit]

   [377][330px-Quaoar_Haumea_Makemake_orbits_2018.png] Haumea's orbit
   outside of Neptune is similar to [378]Makemake's. The positions are as
   of January 1, 2018.

   Haumea has an [379]orbital period of 284 Earth years, a [380]perihelion
   of 35 [381]AU, and an [382]orbital inclination of 28°.^[383][9] It
   passed [384]aphelion in early 1992, and is currently more than 50 AU
   from the Sun.^[385][23] It will come to perihelion in 2133.^[386][10]
   Haumea's orbit has a slightly greater [387]eccentricity than that of
   the other members of [388]its collisional family. This is thought to be
   due to Haumea's weak 7:12 orbital resonance with Neptune gradually
   modifying its initial orbit over the course of a billion
   years,^[389][37]^[390][42] through the [391]Kozai effect, which allows
   the exchange of an orbit's inclination for increased
   eccentricity.^[392][37]^[393][43]^[394][44]

   With a [395]visual magnitude of 17.3,^[396][23] Haumea is the
   [397]third-brightest object in the Kuiper belt after Pluto and
   [398]Makemake, and easily observable with a large amateur
   telescope.^[399][45] However, because the planets and most [400]small
   Solar System bodies share a [401]common orbital alignment from their
   [402]formation in the [403]primordial disk of the Solar System, most
   early surveys for distant objects focused on the projection on the sky
   of this common plane, called the [404]ecliptic.^[405][46] As the region
   of sky close to the ecliptic became well explored, later sky surveys
   began looking for objects that had been dynamically excited into orbits
   with higher inclinations, as well as more distant objects, with slower
   [406]mean motions across the sky.^[407][47]^[408][48] These surveys
   eventually covered the location of Haumea, with its high orbital
   inclination and current position far from the ecliptic.

   Haumea is thought to be in an intermittent 7:12 [415]orbital resonance
   with Neptune.^[416][37] Its [417]ascending node Ω precesses with a
   period of about 4.6 million years, and the resonance is broken twice
   per precession cycle, or every 2.3 million years, only to return a
   hundred thousand years or so later.^[418][5] As this is not a simple
   resonance, [419]Marc Buie qualifies it as non-resonant.^[420][49]

Rotation

   [[421]edit]

   Haumea displays large fluctuations in brightness over a period of 3.9
   hours, which can only be explained by a rotational period of this
   length.^[422][50] This is faster than any other known equilibrium body
   in the [423]Solar System, and indeed faster than any other known body
   larger than 100 km in diameter.^[424][45] While most rotating bodies in
   equilibrium are flattened into [425]oblate spheroids, Haumea rotates so
   quickly that it is distorted into a triaxial [426]ellipsoid. If Haumea
   were to rotate much more rapidly, it would distort itself into a
   dumbbell shape and split in two.^[427][25] This rapid rotation is
   thought to have been caused by the impact that created its satellites
   and collisional family.^[428][37]

   The plane of Haumea's [429]equator is oriented nearly edge-on from
   Earth at present and is also slightly offset to the orbital planes of
   its [430]ring and its outermost moon [431]Hiʻiaka. Although initially
   assumed to be coplanar to Hiʻiaka's orbital plane by Ragozzine and
   Brown in 2009, their models of the collisional formation of Haumea's
   satellites consistently suggested Haumea's equatorial plane to be at
   least aligned with Hiʻiaka's orbital plane by approximately
   1°.^[432][15] This was supported with observations of a [433]stellar
   occultation by Haumea in 2017, which revealed the presence of a ring
   approximately coincident with the plane of Hiʻiaka's orbit and Haumea's
   equator.^[434][12] A mathematical analysis of the occultation data by
   Kondratyev and Kornoukhov in 2018 placed constraints on the relative
   inclination angles of Haumea's equator to the orbital planes of its
   ring and Hiʻiaka, which were found to be inclined 3.2°±1.4° and
   2.0°±1.0° relative to Haumea's equator, respectively.^[435][17]

Physical characteristics

   [[436]edit]

Size, shape, and composition

   [[437]edit]

   The size of a Solar System object can be deduced from its [438]optical
   magnitude, its distance, and its [439]albedo. Objects appear bright to
   Earth observers either because they are large or because they are
   highly reflective. If their reflectivity (albedo) can be ascertained,
   then a rough estimate can be made of their size. For most distant
   objects, the albedo is unknown, but Haumea is large and bright enough
   for its [440]thermal emission to be measured, which has given an
   approximate value for its albedo and thus its size.^[441][51] However,
   the calculation of its dimensions is complicated by its rapid rotation.
   The [442]rotational physics of [443]deformable bodies predicts that
   over as little as a hundred days,^[444][45] a body rotating as rapidly
   as Haumea will have been distorted into the [445]equilibrium form of a
   [446]triaxial ellipsoid. It is thought that most of the fluctuation in
   Haumea's brightness is caused not by local differences in albedo but by
   the alternation of the side view and ends view as seen from
   Earth.^[447][45]

   The rotation and amplitude of Haumea's [448]light curve were argued to
   place strong constraints on its composition. If Haumea were in
   [449]hydrostatic equilibrium and had a low [450]density like Pluto,
   with a thick mantle of [451]ice over a small [452]rocky core, its rapid
   rotation would have elongated it to a greater extent than the
   fluctuations in its brightness allow. Such considerations constrained
   its density to a range of 2.6–3.3 g/cm^3.^[453][52]^[454][45] By
   comparison, the Moon, which is rocky, has a density of 3.3 g/cm^3,
   whereas Pluto, which is typical of icy objects in the Kuiper belt, has
   a density of 1.86 g/cm^3. Haumea's possible high density covered the
   values for [455]silicate minerals such as [456]olivine and
   [457]pyroxene, which make up many of the [458]rocky objects in the
   Solar System. This also suggested that the bulk of Haumea was rock
   covered with a relatively thin layer of ice. A thick ice mantle more
   typical of Kuiper belt objects may have been blasted off during the
   impact that formed the Haumean collisional family.^[459][37]

   Because Haumea has moons, the mass of the system can be calculated from
   their orbits using [460]Kepler's third law. The result is 4.2×10^21 kg,
   28% the mass of the Plutonian system and 6% that of the [461]Moon.
   Nearly all of this mass is in Haumea.^[462][15]^[463][53] Several
   ellipsoid-model calculations of Haumea's dimensions have been made. The
   first model produced after Haumea's discovery was calculated from
   [464]ground-based observations of Haumea's [465]light curve at
   [466]optical wavelengths: it provided a total length of 1,960 to
   2,500 km and a [467]visual [468]albedo (p[v]) greater than
   0.6.^[469][45] The most likely shape is a triaxial ellipsoid with
   approximate dimensions of 2,000 × 1,500 × 1,000 km, with an albedo of
   0.71.^[470][45] Observations by the [471]Spitzer Space Telescope gave a
   diameter of 1,150+250
   −100 km and an albedo of 0.84+0.1
   −0.2, from [472]photometry at [473]infrared wavelengths of 70
   μm.^[474][51] Subsequent light-curve analyses have suggested an
   equivalent circular diameter of 1,450 km.^[475][54] In 2010 an analysis
   of measurements taken by [476]Herschel Space Telescope together with
   the older Spitzer Telescope measurements yielded a new estimate of the
   equivalent diameter of Haumea—about 1300 km.^[477][55] These
   independent size estimates overlap at an average [478]geometric mean
   diameter of roughly 1,400 km. In 2013 the Herschel Space Telescope
   measured Haumea's equivalent circular diameter to be roughly 1,240+69
   −58 km.^[479][56]

   The calculated ellipsoid shape of Haumea, 1,960×1,518×996 km (assuming
   an [480]albedo of 0.73). At the left are the minimum and maximum
   equatorial silhouettes (1,960×996 and 1,518×996 km); at the right is
   the view from the [481]pole (1,960×1,518 km).
   Haumea rapidly rotates in just under 4 hours, causing it to be
   elongated. Haumea exhibits distinguishable variations in colour as it
   rotates, indicative of a dark red spot on its surface as depicted here.

   However the observations of a [482]stellar occultation in January 2017
   cast a doubt on all those conclusions. The measured shape of Haumea,
   while elongated as presumed before, appeared to have significantly
   larger dimensions – according to the data obtained from the occultation
   Haumea is approximately the diameter of Pluto along its longest axis
   and about half that at its poles.^[483][12] The resulting density
   calculated from the observed shape of Haumea was about 1.8 g/cm^3 –
   more in line with densities of other large TNOs. This resulting shape
   appeared to be inconsistent with a homogenous body in hydrostatic
   equilibrium,^[484][12] though Haumea appears to be one of the largest
   trans-Neptunian objects discovered nonetheless,^[485][51] smaller than
   [486]Eris, [487]Pluto, similar to [488]Makemake, and possibly
   [489]Gonggong, and larger than [490]Sedna, [491]Quaoar, and [492]Orcus.

   A 2019 study attempted to resolve the conflicting measurements of
   Haumea's shape and density using [493]numerical modeling of Haumea as a
   differentiated body. It found that dimensions of ≈ 2,100 × 1,680 ×
   1,074 km (modeling the long axis at intervals of 25 km) were a best-fit
   match to the observed shape of Haumea during the 2017 occultation,
   while also being consistent with both surface and core scalene
   ellipsoid shapes in hydrostatic equilibrium.^[494][11] The revised
   solution for Haumea's shape implies that it has a core of approximately
   1,626 × 1,446 × 940 km, with a relatively high density of ≈
   2.68 g/cm^3, indicative of a composition largely of hydrated silicates
   such as [495]kaolinite. The core is surrounded by an icy mantle that
   ranges in thickness from about 70 km at the poles to 170 km along its
   longest axis, comprising up to 17% of Haumea's mass. Haumea's mean
   density is estimated at ≈ 2.018 g/cm^3, with an albedo of ≈
   0.66.^[496][11]

Surface

   [[497]edit]

   In 2005, the [498]Gemini and [499]Keck telescopes obtained [500]spectra
   of Haumea which showed strong crystalline [501]water ice features
   similar to the surface of Pluto's moon [502]Charon.^[503][20] This is
   peculiar, because crystalline ice forms at temperatures above 110 K,
   whereas Haumea's surface temperature is below 50 K, a temperature at
   which [504]amorphous ice is formed.^[505][20] In addition, the
   structure of crystalline ice is unstable under the constant rain of
   [506]cosmic rays and energetic particles from the Sun that strike
   trans-Neptunian objects.^[507][20] The timescale for the crystalline
   ice to revert to amorphous ice under this bombardment is on the order
   of ten million years,^[508][57] yet trans-Neptunian objects have been
   in their present cold-temperature locations for timescales of billions
   of years.^[509][42]

   Radiation damage should also redden and darken the surface of
   trans-Neptunian objects where the common surface materials of
   [510]organic ices and [511]tholin-like compounds are present, as is the
   case with Pluto. Therefore, the spectra and [512]colour suggest Haumea
   and its family members have undergone recent resurfacing that produced
   fresh ice. However, no plausible resurfacing mechanism has been
   suggested.^[513][22]

   Haumea is as bright as snow, with an albedo in the range of 0.6–0.8,
   consistent with crystalline ice.^[514][45] Other large TNOs such as
   [515]Eris appear to have albedos as high or higher.^[516][58] Best-fit
   modeling of the surface spectra suggested that 66% to 80% of the
   Haumean surface appears to be pure crystalline water ice, with one
   contributor to the high albedo possibly [517]hydrogen cyanide or
   [518]phyllosilicate clays.^[519][20] Inorganic cyanide salts such as
   copper potassium cyanide may also be present.^[520][20]

   However, further studies of the visible and near infrared spectra
   suggest a homogeneous surface covered by an intimate 1:1 mixture of
   amorphous and crystalline ice, together with no more than 8% organics.
   The absence of ammonia hydrate excludes [521]cryovolcanism and the
   observations confirm that the collisional event must have happened more
   than 100 million years ago, in agreement with the dynamic
   studies.^[522][59] The absence of measurable [523]methane in the
   spectra of Haumea is consistent with a warm [524]collisional history
   that would have removed such [525]volatiles,^[526][20] in contrast to
   [527]Makemake.^[528][60]

   In addition to the large fluctuations in Haumea's light curve due to
   the body's shape, which affect all [529]colours equally, smaller
   independent colour variations seen in both visible and near-infrared
   wavelengths show a region on the surface that differs both in colour
   and in albedo.^[530][61]^[531][62] More specifically, a large dark red
   area on Haumea's bright white surface was seen in September 2009,
   possibly an impact feature, which indicates an area rich in minerals
   and organic (carbon-rich) compounds, or possibly a higher proportion of
   crystalline ice.^[532][50]^[533][63] Thus Haumea may have a mottled
   surface reminiscent of Pluto, if not as extreme.

Ring

   [[534]edit]

   [535][220px-Haumea_rotation_with_ring.gif] Haumea's 3.9155-hour
   rotation within its discovered ring

   A stellar occultation observed on 21 January 2017, and described in an
   October 2017 [536]Nature article indicated the presence of a [537]ring
   around Haumea. This represents the first ring system discovered for a
   TNO.^[538][12]^[539][64] The ring has a radius of about 2,287 km, a
   width of ~70 km and an opacity of 0.5. It is well within Haumea's
   [540]Roche limit, which would be at a radius of about 4,400 km if it
   were spherical (being nonspherical pushes the limit out
   farther).^[541][12]

   The ring plane is inclined 3.2°±1.4° with respect to Haumea's
   equatorial plane and approximately coincides with the orbital plane of
   its larger, outer moon Hiʻiaka.^[542][12]^[543][65] The ring is also
   close to the 1:3 [544]orbit-spin resonance with Haumea's rotation
   (which is at a radius of 2,285 ± 8 km from Haumea's center). The ring
   is estimated to contribute 5% to the total brightness of
   Haumea.^[545][12]

   In a study about the [546]dynamics of ring particles published in 2019,
   Othon Cabo Winter and colleagues have shown that the 1:3 resonance with
   Haumea's rotation is [547]dynamically unstable, but that there is a
   stable region in the [548]phase space consistent with the location of
   Haumea's ring. This indicates that the ring particles originate on
   circular, periodic orbits that are close to, but not inside, the
   resonance.^[549][66]

Satellites

   [[550]edit]

   Main article: [551]Moons of Haumea

   [552][220px-Haumea-moons-hubble.gif] Haumea and its orbiting moons,
   imaged by [553]Hubble in 2008. [554]Hiʻiaka is the brighter, outermost
   moon, while [555]Namaka is the fainter, inner moon.

   Two small [556]satellites have been discovered orbiting Haumea,
   (136108) Haumea I [557]Hiʻiaka and (136108) Haumea II
   [558]Namaka.^[559][30] Darin Ragozzine and Michael Brown discovered
   both in 2005, through observations of Haumea using the [560]W. M. Keck
   Observatory.

   Hiʻiaka, at first nicknamed "[561]Rudolph" by the Caltech
   team,^[562][67] was discovered 26 January 2005.^[563][53] It is the
   outer and, at roughly 310 km in diameter, the larger and brighter of
   the two, and orbits Haumea in a nearly circular path every
   49 days.^[564][68] Strong absorption features at 1.5 and 2
   [565]micrometres in the [566]infrared spectrum are consistent with
   nearly pure crystalline water ice covering much of the
   surface.^[567][69] The unusual spectrum, along with similar absorption
   lines on Haumea, led Brown and colleagues to conclude that capture was
   an unlikely model for the system's formation, and that the Haumean
   moons must be fragments of Haumea itself.^[568][42]

   Namaka, the smaller, inner satellite of Haumea, was discovered on 30
   June 2005,^[569][70] and nicknamed "[570]Blitzen". It is a tenth the
   mass of Hiʻiaka, orbits Haumea in 18 days in a highly elliptical,
   [571]non-Keplerian orbit, and as of 2008^[572][update] is inclined 13°
   from the larger moon, which [573]perturbs its orbit.^[574][71] The
   relatively large eccentricities together with the mutual inclination of
   the orbits of the satellites are unexpected as they should have been
   damped by the [575]tidal effects. A relatively recent passage by a 3:1
   resonance with Hiʻiaka might explain the current excited orbits of the
   Haumean moons.^[576][72]

   From around 2008 to 2011,^[577][73] the orbits of the Haumean moons
   appeared almost exactly edge-on from Earth, with Namaka periodically
   [578]occulting Haumea.^[579][74] Observation of such transits would
   have provided precise information on the size and shape of Haumea and
   its moons,^[580][75] as happened in [581]the late 1980s with Pluto and
   Charon.^[582][76] The tiny change in brightness of the system during
   these occultations would have required at least a
   [583]medium-[584]aperture [585]professional telescope for
   detection.^[586][75]^[587][77] Hiʻiaka last occulted Haumea in 1999, a
   few years before discovery, and will not do so again for some 130
   years.^[588][78] However, in a situation unique among [589]regular
   satellites, Namaka's orbit was being greatly [590]torqued by Hiʻiaka,
   which preserved the viewing angle of Namaka–Haumea transits for several
   more years.^[591][71]^[592][75]^[593][77] One occultation event was
   observed on 19 June 2009, from the [594]Pico dos Dias Observatory in
   Brazil.^[595][79]

Collisional family

   [[605]edit]

   Main article: [606]Haumea family

   Haumea is the largest member of its [607]collisional family, a group of
   astronomical objects with similar physical and orbital characteristics
   thought to have formed when a larger progenitor was shattered by an
   impact.^[608][37] This family is the first to be identified among TNOs
   and includes—beside Haumea and its moons—[609](55636) 2002 TX300
   (≈364 km), [610](24835) 1995 SM55 (≈174 km), [611](19308) 1996 TO66
   (≈200 km), [612](120178) 2003 OP32 (≈230 km), and [613](145453) 2005
   RR43 (≈252 km).^[614][6] Brown and colleagues proposed that the family
   were a direct product of the impact that removed Haumea's ice
   mantle,^[615][37] but a second proposal suggests a more complicated
   origin: that the material ejected in the initial collision instead
   coalesced into a large moon of Haumea, which was later shattered in a
   second collision, dispersing its shards outwards.^[616][84] This second
   scenario appears to produce a dispersion of velocities for the
   fragments that is more closely matched to the measured velocity
   dispersion of the family members.^[617][84]

   The presence of the collisional family could imply that Haumea and its
   "offspring" might have originated in the [618]scattered disc. In
   today's sparsely populated Kuiper belt, the chance of such a collision
   occurring over the age of the Solar System is less than 0.1
   percent.^[619][85] The family could not have formed in the denser
   primordial Kuiper belt because such a close-knit group would have been
   disrupted by [620]Neptune's migration into the belt—the believed cause
   of the belt's current low density.^[621][85] Therefore, it appears
   likely that the dynamic scattered disc region, in which the possibility
   of such a collision is far higher, is the place of origin for the
   object that generated Haumea and its kin.^[622][85]

   Because it would have taken at least a billion years for the group to
   have diffused as far as it has, the collision which created the Haumea
   family is believed to have occurred very early in the Solar System's
   history.^[623][6]

Exploration

   [[624]edit]

   [625][220px-Haumea_New_Horizons.gif] Haumea imaged by the [626]New
   Horizons spacecraft on 6 October 2007

   Haumea was observed from afar by the [627]New Horizons spacecraft in
   October 2007, January 2017, and May 2020, from distances of 49 AU,
   59 AU, and 63 AU, respectively.^[628][19] The spacecraft's outbound
   trajectory permitted observations of Haumea at high [629]phase angles
   that are otherwise unobtainable from Earth, enabling the determination
   of the light scattering properties and [630]phase curve behavior of
   Haumea's surface.^[631][19]

   Joel Poncy and colleagues calculated that a flyby mission to Haumea
   could take 14.25 years using a gravity assist from Jupiter, based on a
   launch date of 25 September 2025. Haumea would be 48.18 AU from the Sun
   when the spacecraft arrives. A flight time of 16.45 years can be
   achieved with launch dates on 1 November 2026, 23 September 2037, and
   29 October 2038.^[632][86] Haumea could become a target for an
   exploration mission,^[633][87] and an example of this work is a
   preliminary study on a probe to Haumea and its moons (at
   35–51 AU).^[634][88] Probe mass, power source, and propulsion systems
   are key technology areas for this type of mission.^[635][87]
