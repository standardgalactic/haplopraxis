   Chemical compound

   1,2,4-Trimethylbenzene, also known as pseudocumene, is an [135]organic
   compound with the chemical formula C[6]H[3](CH[3])[3]. Classified as an
   [136]aromatic hydrocarbon, it is a flammable colorless liquid with a
   strong odor. It is nearly insoluble in water but soluble in organic
   solvents. It occurs naturally in [137]coal tar and [138]petroleum
   (about 3%). It is one of the three [139]isomers of
   [140]trimethylbenzene.

History

   [[141]edit]

   In 1849, [142]Charles Blachford Mansfield rectified [143]coal tar and
   identified fractions which he hypothesized to be [144]cumole and
   [145]cymole. The latter fraction boiled slightly above 170°C and had
   specific density of 0.857.^[146][3]

   In 1862, [147]Warren De la Rue and Hugo Müller (1833-1915) proposed the
   term pseudocumole for the fractions heavier than [148]xylole.^[149][4]

   When three years later American chemist [150]Cyrus Warren (1824-1891)
   attempted to reproduce Mansfield's results, he determined that the oil
   boiling at 170° has the same formula as cumole, not cymole, and
   suggested to name it isocumole.^[151][5]

   The structure of the compound was determined by Th. Ernst and
   [152]Wilhelm Rudolph Fittig, who first prepared it from
   [153]bromoxylene and [154]iodomethane in 1866 by a [155]Wurtz–Fittig
   reaction developed two years earlier.^[156][6]

   In the next year, Fittig et al. adopted the pseudocumol
   terminology,^[157][7] in 1869 Fittig and B. Wackenroder proved that the
   fraction is a mixture of [158]mesitylene with another trimethylbenzene,
   for which the name of pseudocumol was retained,^[159][8] and in 1886
   [160]Oscar Jacobsen [[161]de] showed that the [162]third
   trimethylbenzene he discovered earlier is also present.^[163][9]

Production

   [[164]edit]

   Industrially, it is isolated from the C[9] [165]aromatic hydrocarbon
   fraction during [166]petroleum distillation. Approximately 40% of this
   fraction is 1,2,4-trimethylbenzene. It is also generated by methylation
   of toluene and xylenes and the [167]disproportionation of xylene over
   aluminosilicate catalysts.^[168][10]

Uses

   [[169]edit]

   Pseudocumene is a precursor to [170]mellitic anhydride, from which high
   performance polymers are made. It is also used as a sterilizing agent
   and in the making of dyes, perfumes and resins. Another use is as an
   [171]antiknock agent,^[172][11] since its research and motor
   [173]octane numbers are well above 100.^[174][12]^: Fig.
   II.1 ^[175][13]

   In automobile fuel it is a minor additive, with its share in US
   [176]gasoline rising from 0.03–0.5% in early 1990s^[177][14] to
   1.1–2.6% in 2011.^[178][15] It may be a major component of some
   [179]avgas formulations.^[180][16]

Scintillator

   [[181]edit]

   1,2,4-Trimethylbenzene dissolved in [182]mineral oil is used as a
   liquid [183]scintillator^[184][17] in particle physics experiments such
   as [185]NOνA and [186]Borexino.
