   Infinite series summable to 1
   [74][220px-Eye_of_Horus_square.png] First six summands drawn as
   portions of a square.[75][220px-Geometric_Segment.svg.png] The
   geometric series on the real line.

   In [76]mathematics, the [77]infinite series ⁠1/2⁠ + ⁠1/4⁠ +
   ⁠1/8⁠ +
   ⁠1/16⁠ + ··· is an elementary example of a [78]geometric series that
   [79]converges absolutely. The sum of the series is 1. In [80]summation
   notation, this may be expressed as

History

   [[88]edit]

Zeno's paradox

   [[89]edit]

   This series was used as a representation of two of [90]Zeno's
   paradoxes.^[91][2] For example, in the [92]paradox of Achilles and the
   Tortoise, the warrior Achilles was to race against a tortoise. The
   track is 100 meters long. Achilles could run at 10 m/s, while the
   tortoise only 5. The tortoise, with a 10-meter advantage, Zeno argued,
   would win. Achilles would have to move 10 meters to catch up to the
   tortoise, but the tortoise would already have moved another five meters
   by then. Achilles would then have to move 5 meters, where the tortoise
   would move 2.5 meters, and so on. Zeno argued that the tortoise would
   always remain ahead of Achilles. Similarly, Zeno's [93]dichotomy
   paradox arises from the supposition that to move a certain distance,
   one would have to move half of it, then half of the remaining distance,
   and so on, therefore having infinitely many time intervals.^[94][2]

   In both cases, each time interval is a term of this [95]infinite
   geometric series, and so even in the limit of infinite terms it would
   sum to a finite total time. This is sometimes considered to resolve
   Zeno's paradoxes.^[96][3] However, insofar as Zeno was concerned with
   the problems of division of a continuum into an [97]actual infinity of
   sub-parts, rather than the problem of their sum, it may not address the
   philosophical heart of Zeno's argument.^[98][4]

The Eye of Horus

   [[99]edit]

   The parts of the [100]Eye of Horus were once thought to represent the
   first six summands of the series.^[101][5]

In Zhuangzi

   [[102]edit]

   A version of the series appears in the ancient [103]Taoist book
   [104]Zhuangzi. The miscellaneous chapters "All Under Heaven" include
   the following sentence: "Take a [105]chi long stick and remove half
   every day, in a myriad ages it will not be exhausted."^[[106]citation
   needed]
