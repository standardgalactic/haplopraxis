   0 (zero) is a [298]number representing an empty [299]quantity. Adding
   (or subtracting) 0 to any number leaves that number unchanged; in
   mathematical terminology, 0 is the [300]additive identity of the
   [301]integers, [302]rational numbers, [303]real numbers, and
   [304]complex numbers, as well as other [305]algebraic structures.
   Multiplying any number by 0 results in 0, and consequently
   [306]division by zero has [307]no meaning in [308]arithmetic.

   As a [309]numerical digit, 0 plays a crucial role in [310]decimal
   notation: it indicates that the [311]power of ten corresponding to the
   place containing a 0 does not contribute to the total. For example,
   "205" in decimal means two hundreds, no tens, and five ones. The same
   principle applies in [312]place-value notations that uses a base other
   than ten, such as [313]binary and [314]hexadecimal. The modern use of 0
   in this manner derives from [315]Indian mathematics that was
   transmitted to Europe via [316]medieval Islamic mathematicians and
   popularized by [317]Fibonacci. It was independently used by the
   [318]Maya.

   Common [319]names for the number 0 in English include zero, nought,
   naught ([320]/nɔːt/), and nil. In contexts where at least one adjacent
   digit distinguishes it from the [321]letter O, the number is sometimes
   pronounced as oh or o ([322]/oʊ/). Informal or [323]slang terms for 0
   include zilch and zip. Historically, ought, aught ([324]/ɔːt/), and
   cipher have also been used.

Etymology

   Main articles: [325]Names for the number 0 and [326]Names for the
   number 0 in English

   The word zero came into the English language via French zéro from the
   [327]Italian zero, a contraction of the Venetian zevero form of Italian
   zefiro via ṣafira or ṣifr.^[328][1] In pre-Islamic time the word ṣifr
   (Arabic صفر) had the meaning "empty".^[329][2] Sifr evolved to mean
   zero when it was used to translate śūnya ([330]Sanskrit: शून्य) from
   India.^[331][2] The first known English use of zero was in
   1598.^[332][3]

   The Italian mathematician [333]Fibonacci (c. 1170 – c. 1250), who grew
   up in North Africa and is credited with introducing the decimal system
   to Europe, used the term zephyrum. This became zefiro in Italian, and
   was then contracted to zero in Venetian. The Italian word [334]zefiro
   was already in existence (meaning "west wind" from Latin and Greek
   [335]Zephyrus) and may have influenced the spelling when transcribing
   Arabic ṣifr.^[336][4]

Modern usage

   Depending on the context, there may be different words used for the
   number zero, or the concept of zero. For the simple notion of lacking,
   the words "[337]nothing" (although this is not accurate) and "none" are
   often used. The British English words [338]"nought" or "naught", and
   "[339]nil" are also synonymous.^[340][5]^[341][6]

   It is often called "oh" in the context of reading out a string of
   digits, such as [342]telephone numbers, [343]street addresses,
   [344]credit card numbers, [345]military time, or years. For example,
   the [346]area code 201 may be pronounced "two oh one", and the year
   1907 is often pronounced "nineteen oh seven". The presence of other
   digits, indicating that the string contains only numbers, avoids
   confusion with the letter O. For this reason, systems that include
   strings with both letters and numbers (such as [347]Canadian postal
   codes) may exclude the use of the letter O.^[[348]citation needed]

   Slang words for zero include "zip", "zilch", "nada", and
   "scratch".^[349][7] In the context of sports, "nil" is sometimes used,
   especially in [350]British English. Several sports have specific words
   for a score of zero, such as "[351]love" in [352]tennis – from French
   l'œuf, "the egg" – and "[353]duck" in [354]cricket, a shortening of
   "duck's egg". "Goose egg" is another general slang term used for
   zero.^[355][7]

History

Ancient Near East

                                                                       nfr
                                                   heart with [356]trachea
                                                 beautiful, pleasant, good

   F35

   Ancient [357]Egyptian numerals were of [358]base 10.^[359][8] They used
   [360]hieroglyphs for the digits and were not [361]positional. In
   [362]one papyrus written around 1770 BC, a scribe recorded daily
   incomes and expenditures for the [363]pharaoh's court, using the
   [364]nfr hieroglyph to indicate cases where the amount of a foodstuff
   received was exactly equal to the amount disbursed. Egyptologist
   [365]Alan Gardiner suggested that the nfr hieroglyph was being used as
   a symbol for zero. The same symbol was also used to indicate the base
   level in drawings of tombs and pyramids, and distances were measured
   relative to the base line as being above or below this line.^[366][9]

   By the middle of the 2nd millennium BC, [367]Babylonian mathematics had
   a sophisticated [368]base 60 positional numeral system. The lack of a
   positional value (or zero) was indicated by a space between
   [369]sexagesimal numerals. In a tablet unearthed at [370]Kish (dating
   to as early as 700 BC), the scribe Bêl-bân-aplu used three hooks as a
   [371]placeholder in the same [372]Babylonian system.^[373][10] By 300
   BC, a punctuation symbol (two slanted wedges) was repurposed as a
   placeholder.^[374][11]^[375][12]

   The Babylonian positional numeral system differed from the later
   [376]Hindu–Arabic system in that it did not explicitly specify the
   magnitude of the leading sexagesimal digit, so that for example the
   lone digit 1 ([377] [20px-Babylonian_1.svg.png] ) might represent any
   of 1, 60, 3600 = 60^2, etc., similar to the significand of a
   [378]floating-point number but without an explicit exponent, and so
   only distinguished implicitly from context. The zero-like placeholder
   mark was only ever used in between digits, but never alone or at the
   end of a number.^[379][13]

Pre-Columbian Americas

   [380][220px-Cero_maya.svg.png] Maya numeral zero

   The [381]Mesoamerican Long Count calendar developed in south-central
   Mexico and Central America required the use of zero as a placeholder
   within its [382]vigesimal (base-20) positional numeral system. Many
   different glyphs, including the partial [383]quatrefoil were used as a
   zero symbol for these Long Count dates, the earliest of which (on Stela
   2 at Chiapa de Corzo, [384]Chiapas) has a date of
   36 BC.^[385][a]^[386][14]

   Since the eight earliest Long Count dates appear outside the Maya
   homeland,^[387][15] it is generally believed that the use of zero in
   the Americas predated the Maya and was possibly the invention of the
   [388]Olmecs.^[389][16] Many of the earliest Long Count dates were found
   within the Olmec heartland, although the Olmec civilization ended by
   the 4th century BC,^[390][17] several centuries before the earliest
   known Long Count dates.^[391][18]

   Although zero became an integral part of [392]Maya numerals, with a
   different, empty [393]tortoise-like "[394]shell shape" used for many
   depictions of the "zero" numeral, it is assumed not to have influenced
   [395]Old World numeral systems.^[[396]citation needed]

   [397]Quipu, a knotted cord device, used in the [398]Inca Empire and its
   predecessor societies in the [399]Andean region to record accounting
   and other digital data, is encoded in a [400]base ten positional
   system. Zero is represented by the absence of a knot in the appropriate
   position.^[401][19]

Classical antiquity

   The [402]ancient Greeks had no symbol for zero (μηδέν, pronounced
   'midén'), and did not use a digit placeholder for it.^[403][20]
   According to mathematician [404]Charles Seife, the ancient Greeks did
   begin to adopt the Babylonian placeholder zero for their work in
   [405]astronomy after 500 BC, representing it with the lowercase Greek
   letter ό (όμικρον: [406]omicron). However, after using the Babylonian
   placeholder zero for astronomical calculations they would typically
   convert the numbers back into [407]Greek numerals. Greeks seemed to
   have a philosophical opposition to using zero as a number.^[408][21]
   Other scholars give the Greek partial adoption of the Babylonian zero a
   later date, with neuroscientist Andreas Nieder giving a date of after
   400 BC and mathematician Robert Kaplan dating it after the
   [409]conquests of Alexander.^[410][22]^[411][23]

   Greeks seemed unsure about the status of zero as a number. Some of them
   asked themselves, "How can not being be?", leading to philosophical
   and, by the [412]medieval period, religious arguments about the nature
   and existence of zero and the [413]vacuum. The [414]paradoxes of
   [415]Zeno of Elea depend in large part on the uncertain interpretation
   of zero.^[416][24]
   [417]Fragment of papyrus with clear Greek script, lower-right corner
   suggests a tiny zero with a double-headed arrow shape above it Example
   of the early Greek symbol for zero (lower right corner) from a
   2nd-century papyrus

   By AD 150, [418]Ptolemy, influenced by [419]Hipparchus and the
   [420]Babylonians, was using a symbol for zero (—°)^[421][25]^[422][26]
   in his work on [423]mathematical astronomy called the Syntaxis
   Mathematica, also known as the [424]Almagest.^[425][27] This
   [426]Hellenistic zero was perhaps the earliest documented use of a
   numeral representing zero in the Old World.^[427][28] Ptolemy used it
   many times in his Almagest (VI.8) for the magnitude of [428]solar and
   [429]lunar eclipses. It represented the value of both [430]digits and
   [431]minutes of immersion at first and last contact. Digits varied
   continuously from 0 to 12 to 0 as the Moon passed over the Sun (a
   triangular pulse), where twelve digits was the [432]angular diameter of
   the Sun. Minutes of immersion was tabulated from 0′0″ to 31′20″ to
   0′0″, where 0′0″ used the symbol as a placeholder in two positions of
   his [433]sexagesimal positional numeral system,^[434][b] while the
   combination meant a zero angle. Minutes of immersion was also a
   continuous function ⁠1/12⁠ 31′20″ √d(24−d)
   (a triangular pulse with [435]convex sides), where d was the digit
   function and 31′20″ was the sum of the radii of the Sun's and Moon's
   discs.^[436][29] Ptolemy's symbol was a placeholder as well as a number
   used by two continuous mathematical functions, one within another, so
   it meant zero, not none. Over time, Ptolemy's zero tended to increase
   in size and lose the [437]overline, sometimes depicted as a large
   elongated 0-like omicron "Ο" or as omicron with overline "ō" instead of
   a dot with overline.^[438][30]

   The earliest use of zero in the calculation of the [439]Julian Easter
   occurred before AD 311, at the first entry in a table of [440]epacts as
   preserved in an [441]Ethiopic document for the years 311 to 369, using
   a [442]Ge'ez word for "none" (English translation is "0" elsewhere)
   alongside Ge'ez numerals (based on Greek numerals), which was
   translated from an equivalent table published by the [443]Church of
   Alexandria in [444]Medieval Greek.^[445][31] This use was repeated in
   525 in an equivalent table, that was translated via the Latin nulla
   ("none") by [446]Dionysius Exiguus, alongside [447]Roman
   numerals.^[448][32] When division produced zero as a remainder, nihil,
   meaning "nothing", was used. These medieval zeros were used by all
   future medieval [449]calculators of Easter. The initial "N" was used as
   a zero symbol in a table of Roman numerals by [450]Bede—or his
   colleagues—around AD 725.^[451][33]

   In most [452]cultures, 0 was identified before the idea of negative
   things (i.e., quantities less than zero) was accepted.^[[453]citation
   needed]

China

   [454]Five illustrated boxes from left to right contain a T-shape, an
   empty box, three vertical bars, three lower horizontal bars with an
   inverted wide T-shape above, and another empty box. Numerals underneath
   left to right are six, zero, three, nine, and zero This is a depiction
   of zero expressed in Chinese [455]counting rods, based on the example
   provided by A History of Mathematics. An empty space is used to
   represent zero.^[456][34]

   The [457]Sūnzĭ Suànjīng, of unknown date but estimated to be dated from
   the 1st to 5th centuries AD, describe how the 4th century BC Chinese
   [458]counting rods system enabled one to perform decimal calculations.
   As noted in the [459]Xiahou Yang Suanjing (425–468 AD), to multiply or
   divide a number by 10, 100, 1000, or 10000, all one needs to do, with
   rods on the counting board, is to move them forwards, or back, by 1, 2,
   3, or 4 places.^[460][35] The rods gave the decimal representation of a
   number, with an empty space denoting zero.^[461][34]^[462][36] The
   counting rod system is a [463]positional notation
   system.^[464][37]^[465][38]

   Zero was not treated as a number at that time, but as a "vacant
   position".^[466][39] [467]Qín Jiǔsháo's 1247 [468]Mathematical Treatise
   in Nine Sections is the oldest surviving Chinese mathematical text
   using a round symbol ‘〇’ for zero.^[469][40] The origin of this symbol
   is unknown; it may have been produced by modifying a square
   symbol.^[470][41] Chinese authors had been familiar with the idea of
   negative numbers by the [471]Han dynasty (2nd century AD), as seen in
   [472]The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art.^[473][42]

India

   [474]Pingala (c. 3rd or 2nd century BC),^[475][43] a [476]Sanskrit
   prosody scholar,^[477][44] used [478]binary sequences, in the form of
   short and long syllables (the latter equal in length to two short
   syllables), to identify the possible valid Sanskrit [479]meters, a
   notation similar to [480]Morse code.^[481][45] Pingala used the
   [482]Sanskrit word [483]śūnya explicitly to refer to zero.^[484][43]
   [485][220px-Bakhshali_manuscript_zero_detail.jpg] Bakhshali manuscript,
   with the numeral "zero" represented by a black dot; its date is
   uncertain.^[486][46]

   The concept of zero as a written digit in the decimal place value
   notation was developed in [487]India.^[488][47] A symbol for zero, a
   large dot likely to be the precursor of the still-current hollow
   symbol, is used throughout the [489]Bakhshali manuscript, a practical
   manual on arithmetic for merchants.^[490][48] In 2017, researchers at
   the [491]Bodleian Library reported [492]radiocarbon dating results for
   three samples from the manuscript, indicating that they came from three
   different centuries: from AD 224–383, AD 680–779, and AD 885–993. It is
   not known how the [493]birch bark fragments from different centuries
   forming the manuscript came to be packaged together. If the writing on
   the oldest birch bark fragments is as old as those fragments, it
   represents South Asia's oldest recorded use of a zero symbol. However,
   it is possible that the writing dates instead to the time period of the
   youngest fragments, AD 885–993. The latter dating has been argued to be
   more consistent with the sophisticated use of zero within the document,
   as portions of it appear to show zero being employed as a number in its
   own right, rather than only as a positional
   placeholder.^[494][46]^[495][49]^[496][50]

   The [497]Lokavibhāga, a [498]Jain text on [499]cosmology surviving in a
   medieval Sanskrit translation of the [500]Prakrit original, which is
   internally dated to AD 458 ([501]Saka era 380), uses a decimal
   [502]place-value system, including a zero. In this text, [503]śūnya
   ("void, empty") is also used to refer to zero.^[504][51]

   The [505]Aryabhatiya (c. 499), states sthānāt sthānaṁ daśaguṇaṁ syāt
   "from place to place each is ten times the
   preceding".^[506][52]^[507][53]^[508][54]

   Rules governing the use of zero appeared in [509]Brahmagupta's
   [510]Brahmasputha Siddhanta (7th century), which states the sum of zero
   with itself as zero, and incorrectly describes [511]division by zero in
   the following way:^[512][55]^[513][56]

     A positive or negative number when divided by zero is a fraction
     with the zero as denominator. Zero divided by a negative or positive
     number is either zero or is expressed as a fraction with zero as
     numerator and the finite quantity as denominator. Zero divided by
     zero is zero.

Epigraphy

   Sambor Inscription
   The oldest, firmly dated use of zero as a decimal figure, found on the
   Sambor Inscription. The number "605" is written in [514]Khmer numerals
   (top), referring to the year it was made: [515]605 Saka era (683 CE).
   The fragment, inscribed in [516]Old Khmer, was once part of a temple
   doorway, and was found in [517]Kratié province, [518]Cambodia.

   A black dot is used as a decimal placeholder in the [519]Bakhshali
   manuscript, portions of which date from AD 224–993.^[520][46]

   There are numerous copper plate inscriptions, with the same small O in
   them, some of them possibly dated to the 6th century, but their date or
   authenticity may be open to doubt.^[521][10]

   A stone tablet found in the ruins of a temple near Sambor on the
   [522]Mekong, [523]Kratié Province, [524]Cambodia, includes the
   inscription of "605" in [525]Khmer numerals (a set of numeral glyphs
   for the [526]Hindu–Arabic numeral system). The number is the year of
   the inscription in the [527]Saka era, corresponding to a date of AD
   683.^[528][57]

   The first known use of special [529]glyphs for the decimal digits that
   includes the indubitable appearance of a symbol for the digit zero, a
   small circle, appears on a stone inscription found at the
   [530]Chaturbhuj Temple, Gwalior, in India, dated AD
   876.^[531][58]^[532][59]

Middle Ages

Transmission to Islamic culture

   See also: [533]History of the Hindu–Arabic numeral system

   The [534]Arabic-language inheritance of science was largely
   [535]Greek,^[536][60] followed by Hindu influences.^[537][61] In 773,
   at [538]Al-Mansur's behest, translations were made of many ancient
   treatises including Greek, Roman, Indian, and others.

   In AD 813, astronomical tables were prepared by a [539]Persian
   mathematician, [540]Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī, using Hindu
   numerals;^[541][61] and about 825, he published a book synthesizing
   Greek and Hindu knowledge and also contained his own contribution to
   mathematics including an explanation of the use of zero.^[542][62] This
   book was later translated into [543]Latin in the 12th century under the
   title Algoritmi de numero Indorum. This title means "al-Khwarizmi on
   the Numerals of the Indians". The word "Algoritmi" was the translator's
   Latinization of Al-Khwarizmi's name, and the word "[544]Algorithm" or
   "[545]Algorism" started to acquire a meaning of any arithmetic based on
   decimals.^[546][61]

   [547]Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Khwarizmi, in 976, stated that if no number
   appears in the place of tens in a calculation, a little circle should
   be used "to keep the rows". This circle was called ṣifr.^[548][63]

Transmission to Europe

   The [549]Hindu–Arabic numeral system (base 10) reached Western Europe
   in the 11th century, via [550]Al-Andalus, through Spanish [551]Muslims,
   the [552]Moors, together with knowledge of [553]classical astronomy and
   instruments like the [554]astrolabe. [555]Gerbert of Aurillac is
   credited with reintroducing the lost teachings into Catholic Europe.
   For this reason, the numerals came to be known in Europe as "Arabic
   numerals". The Italian mathematician [556]Fibonacci or Leonardo of Pisa
   was instrumental in bringing the system into European mathematics in
   1202, stating:

     After my father's appointment by [557]his homeland as state official
     in the customs house of [558]Bugia for the Pisan merchants who
     thronged to it, he took charge; and in view of its future usefulness
     and convenience, had me in my boyhood come to him and there wanted
     me to devote myself to and be instructed in the study of calculation
     for some days. There, following my introduction, as a consequence of
     marvelous instruction in the art, to the nine digits of the Hindus,
     the knowledge of the art very much appealed to me before all others,
     and for it I realized that all its aspects were studied in Egypt,
     Syria, Greece, Sicily, and Provence, with their varying methods; and
     at these places thereafter, while on business. I pursued my study in
     depth and learned the give-and-take of disputation. But all this
     even, and the [559]algorism, as well as the art of [560]Pythagoras,
     I considered as almost a mistake in respect to the method of the
     [561]Hindus [Modus Indorum]. Therefore, embracing more stringently
     that method of the Hindus, and taking stricter pains in its study,
     while adding certain things from my own understanding and inserting
     also certain things from the niceties of [562]Euclid's geometric
     art. I have striven to compose this book in its entirety as
     understandably as I could, dividing it into fifteen chapters. Almost
     everything which I have introduced I have displayed with exact
     proof, in order that those further seeking this knowledge, with its
     pre-eminent method, might be instructed, and further, in order that
     the [563]Latin people might not be discovered to be without it, as
     they have been up to now. If I have perchance omitted anything more
     or less proper or necessary, I beg indulgence, since there is no one
     who is blameless and utterly provident in all things. The nine
     Indian figures are: 9 8 7 6 5 4 3 2 1. With these nine figures, and
     with the sign 0 ... any number may be written.^[564][64]

   From the 13th century, manuals on calculation (adding, multiplying,
   extracting roots, etc.) became common in Europe where they were called
   algorismus after the Persian mathematician [565]al-Khwārizmī. One
   popular manual was written by [566]Johannes de Sacrobosco in the early
   1200s and was one of the earliest scientific books to be [567]printed,
   in 1488.^[568][65]^[569][66] The practice of calculating on paper using
   Hindu–Arabic numerals only gradually displaced calculation by abacus
   and recording with [570]Roman numerals.^[571][67] In the 16th century,
   Hindu–Arabic numerals became the predominant numerals used in
   Europe.^[572][65]

Mathematics

   See also: [587]Null (mathematics)

   The concept of zero plays multiple roles in mathematics: as a digit, it
   is an important part of positional notation for representing numbers,
   while it also plays an important role as a number in its own right in
   many algebraic settings.

As a digit

   Main article: [588]Positional notation

   In positional number systems (such as the usual [589]decimal notation
   for representing numbers), the digit 0 plays the role of a placeholder,
   indicating that certain powers of the base do not contribute. For
   example, the decimal number 205 is the sum of two hundreds and five
   ones, with the 0 digit indicating that no tens are added. The digit
   plays the same role in [590]decimal fractions and in the [591]decimal
   representation of other real numbers (indicating whether any tenths,
   hundredths, thousandths, etc., are present) and in bases other than 10
   (for example, in binary, where it indicates which powers of 2 are
   omitted).^[592][69]

Elementary algebra

   [593][310px-Number_line_with_numbers_-3_to_3.svg.png] A [594]number
   line from −3 to 3, with 0 in the middle

   The number 0 is the smallest [595]nonnegative integer, and the largest
   nonpositive integer. The [596]natural number following 0 is 1 and no
   natural number precedes 0. The number 0 [597]may or may not be
   considered a natural number,^[598][70]^[599][71] but it is an
   [600]integer, and hence a [601]rational number and a [602]real
   number.^[603][72] All rational numbers are [604]algebraic numbers,
   including 0. When the real numbers are extended to form the
   [605]complex numbers, 0 becomes the [606]origin of the complex plane.

   The number 0 can be regarded as neither positive nor negative^[607][73]
   or, alternatively, both positive and negative^[608][74] and is usually
   displayed as the central number in a [609]number line. Zero is
   [610]even^[611][75] (that is, a multiple of 2), and is also an
   [612]integer multiple of any other integer, rational, or real number.
   It is neither a [613]prime number nor a [614]composite number: it is
   not prime because prime numbers are greater than 1 by definition, and
   it is not composite because it cannot be expressed as the product of
   two smaller natural numbers.^[615][76] (However, the [616]singleton set
   {0} is a [617]prime ideal in the [618]ring of the integers.)

   The following are some basic rules for dealing with the number 0. These
   rules apply for any real or complex number x, unless otherwise stated.
     * [619]Addition: x + 0 = 0 + x = x. That is, 0 is an [620]identity
       element (or neutral element) with respect to addition.
     * [621]Subtraction: x − 0 = x and 0 − x = −x.
     * [622]Multiplication: x · 0 = 0 · x = 0.
     * [623]Division:

   ⁠0/x⁠ = 0, for nonzero x. But ⁠x/0⁠ is [624]undefined, because 0 has no
   [625]multiplicative inverse (no real number multiplied by 0 produces
   1), a consequence of the previous rule.^[626][77]

     [627]Exponentiation: x^0 = ⁠x/x⁠ = 1, except that [628]the case x = 0
   is considered undefined in some contexts. For all positive real x, 0^x
   = 0.

   The expression
   ⁠0/0⁠, which may be obtained in an attempt to determine the limit of an
   expression of the form ⁠f(x)/g(x)⁠ as a result of applying the [629]lim
   operator independently to both operands of the fraction, is a so-called
   "[630]indeterminate form". That does not mean that the limit sought is
   necessarily undefined; rather, it means that the limit of ⁠f(x)/g(x)⁠,
   if it exists, must be found by another method, such as [631]l'Hôpital's
   rule.^[632][78]

   The sum of 0 numbers (the [633]empty sum) is 0, and the product of 0
   numbers (the [634]empty product) is 1. The [635]factorial 0! evaluates
   to 1, as a special case of the empty product.^[636][79]

Other uses in mathematics

   [637][90px-Nullset.svg.png] The empty set has zero elements

   The role of 0 as the smallest counting number can be generalized or
   extended in various ways. In [638]set theory, 0 is the [639]cardinality
   of the [640]empty set (notated as "{ }", "
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
   displaystyle="true" scriptlevel="0"> <mi mathvariant="normal">∅</mi>
   </mstyle> </mrow> <annotation
   encoding="application/x-tex">{\displaystyle \emptyset }</annotation>
   </semantics> :MATH]
   {\displaystyle \emptyset } ", or "∅"): if one does not have any apples,
   then one has 0 apples. In fact, in certain axiomatic developments of
   mathematics from set theory, 0 is [641]defined to be the empty
   set.^[642][80] When this is done, the empty set is the [643]von Neumann
   cardinal assignment for a set with no elements, which is the empty set.
   The cardinality function, applied to the empty set, returns the empty
   set as a value, thereby assigning it 0 elements.

   Also in set theory, 0 is the lowest [644]ordinal number, corresponding
   to the empty set viewed as a [645]well-ordered set. In [646]order
   theory (and especially its subfield [647]lattice theory), 0 may denote
   the [648]least element of a [649]lattice or other [650]partially
   ordered set.

   The role of 0 as additive identity generalizes beyond elementary
   algebra. In [651]abstract algebra, 0 is commonly used to denote a
   [652]zero element, which is the [653]identity element for addition (if
   defined on the structure under consideration) and an [654]absorbing
   element for multiplication (if defined). (Such elements may also be
   called [655]zero elements.) Examples include identity elements of
   [656]additive groups and [657]vector spaces. Another example is the
   zero function (or zero map) on a domain D. This is the [658]constant
   function with 0 as its only possible output value, that is, it is the
   function f defined by f(x) = 0 for all x in D. As a function from the
   real numbers to the real numbers, the zero function is the only
   function that is both [659]even and [660]odd.

   The number 0 is also used in several other ways within various branches
   of mathematics:
     * A [661]zero of a function f is a point x in the domain of the
       function such that f(x) = 0.
     * In [662]propositional logic, 0 may be used to denote the [663]truth
       value false.
     * In [664]probability theory, 0 is the smallest allowed value for the
       probability of any event.^[665][81]
     * [666]Category theory introduces the idea of a [667]zero object,
       often denoted 0, and the related concept of [668]zero morphisms,
       which generalize the zero function.^[669][82]

Physics

   The value zero plays a special role for many physical quantities. For
   some quantities, the zero level is naturally distinguished from all
   other levels, whereas for others it is more or less arbitrarily chosen.
   For example, for an [670]absolute temperature (typically measured in
   [671]kelvins), [672]zero is the lowest possible value. ([673]Negative
   temperatures can be defined for some physical systems, but
   negative-temperature systems are not actually colder.) This is in
   contrast to temperatures on the Celsius scale, for example, where zero
   is arbitrarily defined to be at the [674]freezing point of
   water.^[675][83]^[676][84] Measuring sound intensity in [677]decibels
   or [678]phons, the zero level is arbitrarily set at a reference
   value—for example, at a value for the threshold of hearing. In
   [679]physics, the [680]zero-point energy is the lowest possible energy
   that a [681]quantum mechanical [682]physical system may possess and is
   the energy of the [683]ground state of the system.

Computer science

   Modern computers store information in [684]binary, that is, using an
   "alphabet" that contains only two symbols, usually chosen to be "0" and
   "1". Binary coding is convenient for [685]digital electronics, where
   "0" and "1" can stand for the absence or presence of electrical current
   in a wire.^[686][85] [687]Computer programmers typically use
   [688]high-level programming languages that are more easily intelligible
   to humans than the [689]binary instructions that are directly executed
   by the [690]central processing unit. 0 plays various important roles in
   high-level languages. For example, a [691]Boolean variable stores a
   value that is either true or false, and 0 is often the numerical
   representation of false.^[692][86]

   0 also plays a role in [693]array indexing. The most common practice
   throughout human history has been to start counting at one, and this is
   the practice in early classic programming languages such as
   [694]Fortran and [695]COBOL.^[696][87] However, in the late 1950s
   [697]LISP introduced [698]zero-based numbering for arrays while
   [699]Algol 58 introduced completely flexible basing for array
   subscripts (allowing any positive, negative, or zero integer as base
   for array subscripts), and most subsequent programming languages
   adopted one or other of these positions.^[[700]citation needed] For
   example, the elements of an array are numbered starting from 0 in
   [701]C, so that for an array of n items the sequence of array indices
   runs from 0 to n−1.^[702][88]

   There can be confusion between 0- and 1-based indexing; for example,
   Java's [703]JDBC indexes parameters from 1 although [704]Java itself
   uses 0-based indexing.^[705][89]

   In C, a [706]byte containing the value 0 serves to indicate where a
   [707]string of characters ends. Also, 0 is a standard way to refer to a
   [708]null pointer in code.^[709][90]

   In databases, it is possible for a field not to have a value. It is
   then said to have a [710]null value.^[711][91] For numeric fields it is
   not the value zero. For text fields this is not blank nor the empty
   string. The presence of null values leads to [712]three-valued logic.
   No longer is a condition either true or false, but it can be
   undetermined. Any computation including a null value delivers a null
   result.^[713][92]

   In mathematics, there is no "positive zero" or "negative zero" distinct
   from zero; both −0 and +0 represent exactly the same number. However,
   in some computer hardware [714]signed number representations, zero has
   two distinct representations, a positive one grouped with the positive
   numbers and a negative one grouped with the negatives. This kind of
   dual representation is known as [715]signed zero, with the latter form
   sometimes called negative zero. These representations include the
   [716]signed magnitude and [717]ones' complement binary integer
   representations (but not the [718]two's complement binary form used in
   most modern computers), and most [719]floating-point number
   representations (such as [720]IEEE 754 and [721]IBM S/390
   floating-point formats).^[[722]citation needed]

   An [723]epoch, in computing terminology, is the date and time
   associated with a zero timestamp. The [724]Unix epoch begins the
   midnight before the first of January
   1970.^[725][93]^[726][94]^[727][95] The [728]Classic Mac OS epoch and
   [729]Palm OS epoch begin the midnight before the first of January
   1904.^[730][96]

   Many [731]APIs and [732]operating systems that require applications to
   return an integer value as an [733]exit status typically use zero to
   indicate success and non-zero values to indicate specific [734]error or
   warning conditions.^[735][97]^[[736]citation needed]

   Programmers often use a [737]slashed zero to avoid confusion with the
   letter "[738]O".^[739][98]

Other fields

Biology

   In [740]comparative zoology and [741]cognitive science, recognition
   that some animals display awareness of the concept of zero leads to the
   conclusion that the capability for numerical abstraction arose early in
   the [742]evolution of species.^[743][99]

Dating systems

   Main article: [744]Year zero

   In the [745]BC [746]calendar era, the year 1 BC is the first year
   before AD 1; there is not a [747]year zero. By contrast, in
   [748]astronomical year numbering, the year 1 BC is numbered 0, the year
   2 BC is numbered −1, and so forth.^[749][100]
