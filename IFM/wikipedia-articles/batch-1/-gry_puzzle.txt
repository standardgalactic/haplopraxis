   From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
   Word puzzle

   The -gry puzzle is a popular [61]word puzzle that asks for the third
   English word that ends with the letters -gry other than angry and
   hungry. Specific wording varies substantially, but the puzzle has no
   clear answer, as there are no other common English words that end in
   -gry.^[62][1]^[63][2] Interpretations of the puzzle suggest it is
   either an answerless hoax; a [64]trick question; a sincere question
   asking for an obscure word; or a corruption of a more straightforward
   puzzle, which may have asked for words containing gry (such as
   [65]gryphon). Of these, countless trick question variants and obscure
   English words (or [66]nonce words) have been proposed. The lack of a
   conclusive answer has ensured the enduring popularity of the puzzle,
   and it has become one of the most frequently asked word
   puzzles.^[67][3]^[68][4]^[69][5]

   The ultimate origin and original form of the puzzle is unknown, but it
   was popularized in 1975, starting in the New York area, and has
   remained popular into the 21st century. Various [70]similar puzzles
   exist, though these have straightforward answers. The most notable is
   "words ending in -dous", which has been popular since the 1880s.

Answers

   [[71]edit]

   Various proposed answers exist, stating that the question is one of the
   following:
     * A [72]hoax – there is no answer, and its purpose (or effect) is to
       frustrate.^[73][6]
     * A [74]trick question, with various answers depending on precise
       wording.
     * A sincere question asking for an obscure word, most often proposed
       as [75]aggry or [76]puggry. This does not apply to wordings that
       explicitly ask for a very common word.
     * A corruption of a more straightforward word puzzle, namely a word
       containing the sequence "gry", though not necessarily at the (tail)
       end, in which case the answer is [77]gryphon which is uncommon but
       in use.^[78][7]^[79][8]^[80][9]

   This topic is a source of lively interest, both to lovers of word
   puzzles and lovers of words. For both groups, much of the appeal lies
   in the [81]quest, either to trace the origin of the puzzle or compile a
   complete list of words ending in -gry.

   More recently, the word hangry—a [82]portmanteau of 'hungry' and
   'angry'—has been used to refer to an irritable state induced by lack of
   food.^[83][10] Oxford Dictionaries (controlled by, but less restrictive
   than, the [84]Oxford English Dictionary) added hangry on 27 August
   2015,^[85][11]^[86][12]^[87][13] and the full Oxford English Dictionary
   added hangry in 2018.^[88][14]

History

   [[89]edit]

   There are anecdotal reports of various forms of the puzzle dating to
   the 1950s or earlier; the ultimate origin is presumably an [90]oral
   tradition or a lost book of puzzles.^[91][15] However, the first
   documented evidence is from early 1975 in the [92]New York metropolitan
   area, and the puzzle rapidly gained popularity in this year. The most
   likely source is the talk show of [93]Bob Grant, from some program in
   early or mid March 1975.

   [94]Merriam-Webster, publishers of the leading American dictionaries,
   first heard of this puzzle in a letter dated March 17, 1975, from
   Patricia Lasker of [95]Brooklyn, New York. Lasker says her plant
   manager heard the question on an unnamed [96]quiz show. Since that time
   Merriam-Webster has received about four letters each year^[97][3]
   asking the question.

   The puzzle first appears in print in Anita Richterman's "Problem Line"
   column in [98]Newsday on April 29, 1975. One "M.Z." from [99]Wantagh,
   New York states that the problem was asked on a TV quiz program.
   Richterman states that she asked a learned professor of English for
   help when she first received the inquiry, and he did not respond for
   over a month. This agrees with the Merriam-Webster report, suggesting a
   quiz show in early or mid March 1975.

   In Anita Richterman's column on May 9, 1975, several correspondents
   reported that they had heard the puzzle on the [100]Bob Grant radio
   talk show on [101]WMCA in New York City. This suggests either that the
   earlier claims of a (TV) quiz show confused a talk show with a quiz
   show, or that there was another unspecified quiz show that was then
   repeated by Grant.^[102][16] The majority of readers gave the answer
   "[103]gry", an obsolete unit of measure invented by [104]John Locke. It
   is unclear whether this was the answer given on the Grant show, or what
   the precise wording had been.

   By fall 1975 the puzzle had reached the Delaware Valley, again
   apparently by radio, by which time the puzzle seems to have mutated to
   a form in which the missing word is an adjective that describes the
   state of the world.^[105][17]

   The puzzle has had occasional bouts of popularity: after its initial
   popularity in 1975, it was popular in 1978, then again in
   1995–1996.^[106][18]

Reports of earlier versions

   [[107]edit]

   The most credible report of an early version was given on
   [108]Stumpers-L,^[109][19] which reported a trick question formulation
   from an eight-page pamphlet entitled Things to Think About, probably
   dating to the 1940s:

     One enterprising reference librarian found an eight-page pamphlet
     (no copyright date, but from the appearance probably printed in the
     1940s) entitled Things to Think About. The booklet was filled with
     riddles, including the following:

     There are three words in the English language that end with -gry.
     Two of these are angry and hungry. The third word is a very common
     word, and you use it often. If you have read what I have told you,
     you will see that I have given you the third word. What is the third
     word? Think very carefully.

     Three! The question has nothing to do with angry, hungry, or any of
     the many other obscure words that end in -gry, it is a simple
     question asking you what the third word in the sentence is. As you
     take tests, remember this.

Alternative versions

   [[110]edit]

Trick versions

   [[111]edit]
    1. This version only works when spoken: There are three words in
       English that end in a "gree". The first two are "angry" and
       "hungry", and if you've listened closely, you'll agree that I've
       already told you the third one.^[112][3]^[113][20]
          + The answer is "agree".
    2. There are three words in the English language that end with the
       letters 'g', 'r', and 'y'. Two are "hungry" and "angry". The third
       word is something everyone uses every day. Everyone knows what the
       third word means. What is the third word?^[114][3]^[115][21]
          + The answer is "energy". The riddle says that the word ends in
            the letters g-r-y; it says nothing about the order of the
            letters. Many words end with "-rgy", but energy is something
            everyone uses every day.
    3. There are at least three words in the English language that end in
       "g" or "y". One of them is "hungry", and another one is "angry".
       There is a third word, a short one, which you probably say every
       day. If you are listening carefully to everything I say, you just
       heard me say it three times. What is it?^[116][3]^[117][22]
          + The answer is "say". This version depends upon the listener
            confusing the spoken word "or" and the spoken letter "r".
    4. There are three words in the English language that end in "gry".
       Two words that end in "gry" are "hungry" and "angry". Everyone
       knows what the third word means, and everyone uses them every day.
       If you listened very carefully, I have already stated to you what
       the third word is. The three words that solve this riddle
       are...?^[118][3]^[119][23]
          + The answer is the three-word sentence "I am hungry". This
            version asks for three words that end in "gry", not three
            words each of which ends in "gry".
    5. I know two words that end in "gry". Neither one is angry or hungry.
       What are they?^[120][3]^[121][24]
          + The answer is "angry" and "hungry". Since these are words,
            they are not capable of being angry or hungry.
    6. Give me three English words, commonly spoken, ending in
       g-r-y.^[122][3]^[123][24]
          + There are many possible answers, such as "Beg for mercy", or
            "Bring your money".
    7. There are three words in the English language that end g-r-y. One
       is angry and another is hungry. The third word is something that
       "everyone" uses. If you have listened carefully, I have already
       told you what it is.^[124][3]
          + The answer is "every". First word is "fuming" which ends with
            "g". The word "fuming" is angry (when personified). Second
            word is "eager" which ends with "r". The word "eager" is
            hungry (when personified). Third word is "every" which ends
            with "y". The word "everyone" uses the word "every".

Meta-puzzle versions

   [[125]edit]

   The remaining versions are a form of meta-puzzle, in the sense that
   they make no use of the actual letters "gry" themselves, which
   therefore are a [126]red herring. The red herring only works because
   there is another puzzle that does use these letters (even though that
   puzzle has no good answer).
    1. Think of words ending in "gry". "Angry" and "hungry" are two of
       them. There are only three words in "the English language". What is
       the third word? Hint: The word is something that everyone uses
       every day. If you have listened carefully, I have already told you
       what it is.^[127][3]^[128][25]
          + The answer is "language", and the logic is as follows: There
            are only three words in "the English language"; the third word
            is "language". Since this version requires quotation marks
            around the phrase, "the English language", the written version
            gives away the trick.
    2. Angry and hungry are two words in the English language that end in
       "gry". "What" is the third word. The word is something that
       everyone uses every day. If you have listened carefully, I have
       already told you what it is.^[129][3]^[130][21]
          + The answer is "what". But again, the quotation marks spoil the
            puzzle when it is printed.
    3. There are three words in the English language that end with "gry".
       Two of these are "angry" and "hungry". The third word is a very
       common word, and you use it often. If you have read what I have
       told you, you will see that I have given you the third word. What
       is the third word? Think very carefully.^[131][3]^[132][26]
          + The answer is "three", the third word in the paragraph. The
            rest of the paragraph is a red herring.
    4. There are three words in the English language that end in "gry".
       The first "one" is "hungry", the second "one" is "angry", what is
       the third "one"? If you have read this carefully I have given a
       clue.^[133][3]^[134][27]
          + The answer is the word "one", which is the third "one". Again
            the quotation marks ruin the written puzzle, so this version
            is usually written without the quotation marks and with the
            word "one" capitalized.

Similar puzzles

   [[135]edit]

   There are numerous similar puzzles, giving letter sequences that rarely
   occur in words.^[136][28] The most-notable of these is the -dous puzzle
   of finding words ending in -dous, which was popular in the 1880s. This
   took various forms, sometimes simply listing all words or all common
   words,^[137][29]^[138][30] sometimes being posed as a riddle, giving
   the three common words, [139]tremendous, [140]stupendous, and
   [141]hazardous, and requesting the rarer fourth, which is
   [142]jeopardous. This form originated in 1883, with an A.A. of Glasgow
   writing to [143]George Augustus Henry Sala in his "Echoes of the Week"
   column in the [144]Illustrated London News.^[145][31]^[146][32] This
   question has had enduring popularity,^[147][33] even inspiring a
   contest,^[148][34] though the words have proven less stable: today
   jeopardous is considered too rare, and the formerly unpopular
   [149]horrendous has taken its place; this change occurred as early as
   1909.^[150][35] At times other words such as [151]hybridous have been
   accepted.^[152][34] Today [153]hazardous is typically the omitted word,
   and differs from the others in being a visible compound [154]hazard +
   [155]-ous. This puzzle has continued in popularity through the end of
   the 20th century,^[156][36]^[157][37] with recent versions giving it as
   an alternative to the gry puzzle.^[158][38] There is a Russian puzzle
   which goes: "There are three words in the Russian language which end in
   -zo. Two of them are zhelezo "iron" and puzo "belly". What is the third
   word?" There is quite a handful of other nouns that end in -zo, in the
   Russian language, but most of them are fairly obscure terms like авизо,
   abbreviations or proper names like Кензо. Another similar one is words
   ending in -cion, of which the common words are [159]coercion,
   [160]scion, and [161]suspicion.^[162][29]^[163][35]

   The most similar to the gry puzzle in form is to find three words that
   contain the letter sequence shion, to which the answer is [164]cushion,
   [165]fashion, and [166]parishioner; this is typically stated by giving
   cushion and fashion, and requesting the third word, namely
   parishioner.^[167][28]^[168][39] This can be modified to finding words
   ending with -shion, in which case the answer is the obsolete word
   [169]parishion, which is a synonymous variant of parishioner. This has
   not been nearly as popular as the gry puzzle.

Solution techniques

   [[170]edit]

   The standard way to solve such puzzles is to use a [171]reverse
   dictionary, or to perform an exhaustive search through a dictionary,
   either manually, which is tedious and error-prone, or using computer
   tools such as [172]grep, which requires an electronic word list. At the
   origin of the gry puzzle, the standard reverse dictionary in modern
   English was the "Air Force Reverse Dictionary" (formally the Normal and
   Reverse Word List, compiled under the direction of A. F. Brown), which
   did not have additional answers for gry. The most plausible answer at
   the time was [173]meagry, found in the [174]Oxford English
   Dictionary.^[175][17] A more elaborate strategy is to list words that
   have endings similar to gry, such as -gary, and then search a larger
   dictionary for obsolete variants ending in -gry, for example begry for
   beggary.^[176][40]

   From around 1980 electronic word lists became widely available on
   [177]Unix systems, and searching for answers to the gry puzzle was an
   occasional benchmark; this also turned up gryphon in some cases, if
   match is not required to be at the end.^[178][7] This is now easily
   done in milliseconds on modern personal computers:
grep gry$ /usr/share/dict/words  # Search for words ending in gry
grep gry /usr/share/dict/words  # Search for words containing gry
