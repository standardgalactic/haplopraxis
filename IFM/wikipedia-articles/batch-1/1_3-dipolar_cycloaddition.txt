   The 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition is a [112]chemical reaction between a
   [113]1,3-dipole and a dipolarophile to form a five-membered ring. The
   earliest 1,3-dipolar cycloadditions were described in the late 19th
   century to the early 20th century, following the discovery of
   1,3-dipoles. [114]Mechanistic investigation and [115]synthetic
   application were established in the 1960s, primarily through the work
   of [116]Rolf Huisgen.^[117][1]^[118][2] Hence, the reaction is
   sometimes referred to as the Huisgen cycloaddition (this term is often
   used to specifically describe the [119]1,3-dipolar cycloaddition
   between an organic [120]azide and an [121]alkyne to generate
   [122]1,2,3-triazole). 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition is an important route
   to the [123]regio- and [124]stereoselective synthesis of five-membered
   [125]heterocycles and their ring-opened acyclic derivatives. The
   dipolarophile is typically an alkene or alkyne, but can be other pi
   systems. When the dipolarophile is an alkyne, aromatic rings are
   generally produced.
   [126][lossy-page1-517px-Example_of_1%2C3-dipolar_cycloaddition.tif.jpg]

Mechanistic overview

   [[127]edit]

   Originally two proposed mechanisms describe the 1,3-dipolar
   cycloaddition: first, the concerted [128]pericyclic [129]cycloaddition
   mechanism, proposed by Rolf Huisgen;^[130][3] and second, the stepwise
   mechanism involving a [131]diradical [132]intermediate, proposed by
   Firestone.^[133][4] After much debate, the former proposal is now
   generally accepted^[134][5]—the 1,3-dipole reacts with the
   dipolarophile in a [135]concerted, often asynchronous, and
   [136]symmetry-allowed [π]4[s] + [π]2[s] fashion through a thermal
   six-electron [137]Huckel aromatic transition state. However, a few
   examples exist of a stepwise mechanism for the catalyst-free
   1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reactions of thiocarbonyl ylides,^[138][6]
   and nitrile oxides^[139][7]

   dipole and a dipolarophile to give a five-membered heterocycle, through
   a six-electron transition state. Note that the red curly arrows are
   conventionally used to denote the reaction process but do not
   necessarily represent the actual flow of electrons. The generic
   mechanism of a 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition between a dipole and a
   dipolarophile to give a five-membered heterocycle, through a
   six-electron transition state. Note that the red curly arrows are
   conventionally used to denote the reaction process but do not
   necessarily represent the actual flow of electrons.

Pericyclic mechanism

   [[141]edit]

   Huisgen investigated a series of cycloadditions between the 1,3-dipolar
   [142]diazo compounds and various dipolarophilic [143]alkenes.^[144][3]
   The following observations support the concerted pericyclic mechanism,
   and refute the stepwise diradical or the stepwise polar pathway.
     * [145]Substituent effects: Different substituents on the dipole do
       not exhibit a large effect on the cycloaddition rate, suggesting
       that the reaction does not involve a charge-separated intermediate.
     * [146]Solvent effects: Solvent polarity has little effect on the
       cycloaddition rate, in line with the pericyclic mechanism where
       polarity does not change much in going from the reactants to the
       transition state.
     * [147]Stereochemistry: 1,3-dipolar cycloadditions are always
       [148]stereospecific with respect to the dipolarophile (i.e.,
       [149]cis-alkenes giving syn-products), supporting the concerted
       pericyclic mechanism in which two [150]sigma bonds are formed
       simultaneously.
     * [151]Thermodynamic parameters: 1,3-dipolar cycloadditions have an
       unusually large negative [152]entropy of activation similar to that
       of the [153]Diels-Alder reaction, suggesting that the
       [154]transition state is highly ordered, which is a signature of
       concerted pericyclic reactions.

1,3-Dipole

   [[155]edit]
   [156][lossy-page1-220px-18_species_of_second-row_1%2C3-dipoles.tif.jpg]
   Structure and nomenclature of all second-row 1,3-dipoles consisting of
   carbon, nitrogen and oxygen centers. The dipoles are categorized as
   allyl-type or propargyl/allenyl-type based on the geometry of the
   central atom.

   A 1,3-dipole is an organic molecule that can be represented as either
   an [157]allyl-type or a [158]propargyl/[159]allenyl-type
   [160]zwitterionic octet/sextet structures. Both types of 1,3-dipoles
   share four electrons in the π-system over three atoms. The allyl-type
   is bent whereas the propargyl/allenyl-type is linear in
   [161]geometry.^[162][8] 1,3-Dipoles containing higher-row elements such
   as [163]sulfur or [164]phosphorus are also known, but are utilized less
   routinely.

   [165]Resonance structures can be drawn to [166]delocalize both negative
   and positive charges onto any terminus of a 1,3-dipole (see the scheme
   below). A more accurate method to describe the electronic distribution
   on a 1,3-dipole is to assign the major resonance contributor based on
   experimental or theoretical data, such as [167]dipole moment
   measurements^[168][9] or computations.^[169][10] For example,
   [170]diazomethane bears the largest negative character at the terminal
   nitrogen atom, while [171]hydrazoic acid bears the largest negative
   character at the internal nitrogen atom.
   [172]Calculated major resonance structures of diazomethane and
   hydrazoic acid (doi = 10.1021/ja00475a007) Calculated major resonance
   structures of diazomethane and hydrazoic acid (doi =
   10.1021/ja00475a007)

   Consequently, this ambivalence means that the ends of a 1,3-dipole can
   be treated as both [173]nucleophilic and [174]electrophilic at the same
   time. The extent of nucleophilicity and electrophilicity at each end
   can be evaluated using the [175]frontier molecular orbitals, which can
   be obtained computationally. In general, the atom that carries the
   largest orbital coefficient in the [176]HOMO acts as the nucleophile,
   whereas that in the LUMO acts as the electrophile. The most
   nucleophilic atom is usually, but not always, the most electron-rich
   atom.^[177][11]^[178][12]^[179][13] In 1,3-dipolar cycloadditions,
   identity of the dipole-dipolarophile pair determines whether the HOMO
   or the LUMO character of the 1,3-dipole will dominate (see discussion
   on frontier molecular orbitals below).

Dipolarophile

   [[180]edit]

   The most commonly used dipolarophiles are alkenes and alkynes.
   [181]Heteroatom-containing dipolarophiles such as [182]carbonyls and
   [183]imines can also undergo 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition. Other examples
   of dipolarophiles include [184]fullerenes and [185]nanotubes, which can
   undergo 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition with [186]azomethine ylide in the
   [187]Prato reaction.

Solvent effects

   [[188]edit]

   1,3-Dipolar cycloadditions experience very little solvent effect
   because both the reactants and the transition states are generally
   non-polar. For example, the rate of reaction between phenyl
   diazomethane and [189]ethyl acrylate or [190]norbornene (see scheme
   below) changes only slightly upon varying solvents from cyclohexane to
   methanol.^[191][14]
   [192]Effect of solvent polarity on 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition
   reactions(doi:10.3987/S(N)-1978-01-0109.) Effect of solvent polarity on
   1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reactions(doi:10.3987/S(N)-1978-01-0109.)

   Lack of solvent effects in 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition is clearly
   demonstrated in the reaction between enamines and dimethyl
   diazomalonate (see scheme below).^[193][15] The polar reaction,
   N-cyclopentenyl [194]pyrrolidine nucleophilic addition to the diazo
   compound, proceeds 1,500 times faster in polar [195]DMSO than in
   non-polar [196]decalin. On the other hand, a close analog of this
   reaction, N-cyclohexenyl pyrrolidine 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition to
   dimethyl diazomalonate, is sped up only 41-fold in DMSO relative to
   decalin.

Frontier molecular orbital theory

   [[198]edit]
   [199]Orbital overlaps in types I, II and III 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition.
   Orbital overlaps in types I, II and III 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition.

   1,3-Dipolar cycloadditions are pericyclic reactions, which obey the
   [200]Dewar-Zimmerman rules and the [201]Woodward–Hoffmann rules. In the
   Dewar-Zimmerman treatment, the reaction proceeds through a 5-center,
   zero-node, 6-electron Huckel transition state for this particular
   molecular orbital diagram. However, each orbital can be randomly
   assigned a sign to arrive at the same result. In the Woodward–Hoffmann
   treatment, frontier molecular orbitals (FMO) of the 1,3-dipole and the
   dipolarophile overlap in the symmetry-allowed [π]4[s] + [π]2[s] manner.
   Such orbital overlap can be achieved in three ways: type I, II and
   III.^[202][16] The dominant pathway is the one which possesses the
   smallest HOMO-LUMO energy gap.

Type I

   [[203]edit]

   The dipole has a high-lying [204]HOMO which overlaps with LUMO of the
   dipolarophile. A dipole of this class is referred to as a
   HOMO-controlled dipole or a nucleophilic dipole, which includes
   [205]azomethine ylide, [206]carbonyl ylide, [207]nitrile ylide,
   [208]azomethine imine, [209]carbonyl imine and [210]diazoalkane. These
   dipoles add to electrophilic alkenes readily. Electron-withdrawing
   groups (EWG) on the dipolarophile would accelerate the reaction by
   lowering the LUMO, while electron-donating groups (EDG) would
   decelerate the reaction by raising the HOMO. For example, the
   reactivity scale of diazomethane against a series of dipolarophiles is
   shown in the scheme below. Diazomethane reacts with the electron-poor
   ethyl acrylate more than a million times faster than the electron rich
   butyl vinyl ether.^[211][17]

   This type resembles the normal-electron-demand Diels-Alder reaction, in
   which the diene HOMO combines with the dienophile LUMO.
   [212]doi:10.1016/S0040-4039(01)92781-9
   doi:10.1016/S0040-4039(01)92781-9

Type II

   [[213]edit]

   HOMO of the dipole can pair with LUMO of the dipolarophile;
   alternatively, HOMO of the dipolarophile can pair with LUMO of the
   dipole. This two-way interaction arises because the energy gap in
   either direction is similar. A dipole of this class is referred to as a
   HOMO-LUMO-controlled dipole or an ambiphilic dipole, which includes
   [214]nitrile imide, [215]nitrone, [216]carbonyl oxide, [217]nitrile
   oxide, and [218]azide. Any substituent on the dipolarophile would
   accelerate the reaction by lowering the energy gap between the two
   interacting orbitals; i.e., an EWG would lower the LUMO while an EDG
   would raise the HOMO. For example, azides react with various
   electron-rich and electron-poor dipolarophile with similar reactivities
   (see reactivity scale below).^[219][18]
   [220]doi:10.1021/ja01016a011 doi:10.1021/ja01016a011

Type III

   [[221]edit]

   The dipole has a low-lying LUMO which overlaps with HOMO of the
   dipolarophile (indicated by red dashed lines in the diagram). A dipole
   of this class is referred to as a LUMO-controlled dipole or an
   electrophilic dipole, which includes [222]nitrous oxide and [223]ozone.
   EWGs on the dipolarophile decelerate the reaction, while EDGs
   accelerate the reaction. For example, ozone reacts with the
   electron-rich 2-methylpropene about 100,000 times faster than the
   electron-poor tetrachloroethene (see reactivity scale below).^[224][19]

   This type resembles the [225]inverse electron-demand Diels-Alder
   reaction, in which the diene LUMO combines with the dienophile HOMO.
   [226]doi:10.1021/ja01016a011 doi:10.1021/ja01016a011

Reactivity

   [[227]edit]

   Concerted processes such as the 1,3-cycloaddition require a highly
   ordered transition state (high negative entropy of activation) and only
   moderate enthalpy requirements. Using competition reaction experiments,
   relative rates of addition for different cycloaddition reactions have
   been found to offer general findings on factors in reactivity.
     * Conjugation, especially with aromatic groups, increases the rate of
       reaction by stabilization of the transition state. During the
       transition, the two sigma bonds are being formed at different
       rates, which may generate partial charges in the transition state
       that can be stabilized by charge distribution into conjugated
       substituents.
     * More polarizable dipolarophiles are more reactive because diffuse
       electron clouds are better suited to initiate the flow of
       electrons.
     * Dipolarophiles with high angular strain are more reactive due to
       increased energy of the ground state.
     * Increased steric hindrance in the transition state as a result of
       unhindered reactants dramatically lowers the reaction rate.
     * Hetero-dipolarophiles add more slowly, if at all, compared to
       C,C-diapolarophiles due to a lower gain in sigma bond energy to
       offset the loss of a pi bond during the transition state.
     * Isomerism of the dipolarophile affects reaction rate due to
       sterics. trans-isomers are more reactive (trans-stilbene will add
       diphenyl(nitrile imide) 27 times faster than cis-stilbene) because
       during the reaction, the 120° bond angle shrinks to 109°, bringing
       eclipsing cis-substituents towards each other for increased steric
       clash.

          [228]See Huisgen reference doi:10.1002/anie.196306331. See
          Huisgen reference [229]doi:[230]10.1002/anie.196306331.

Stereospecificity

   [[231]edit]

   1,3-dipolar cycloadditions usually result in [232]retention of
   configuration with respect to both the 1,3-dipole and the
   dipolarophile. Such high degree of stereospecificity is a strong
   support for the concerted over the stepwise reaction mechanisms. As
   mentioned before, many examples show that the reactions were stepwise,
   thus, presenting partial or no stereospecificity.

   trans-substituents end up trans in the resulting five-membered cyclic
   compound (see scheme below).^[234][20]

With respect to dipole

   [[236]edit]

   Generally, the stereochemistry of the dipole is not of major concern
   because only few dipoles could form [237]stereogenic centers, and
   resonance structures allow bond rotation which scrambles the
   stereochemistry. However, the study of azomethine ylides has verified
   that cycloaddition is also stereospecific with respect to the dipole
   component. [238]Diastereopure azomethine ylides are generated by
   [239]electrocyclic ring opening of [240]aziridines, and then rapidly
   trapped with strong dipolarophiles before bond rotation can take place
   (see scheme below).^[241][21]^[242][22] If weaker dipolarophiles are
   used, bonds in the dipole have the chance to rotate, resulting in
   impaired cycloaddition stereospecificity.

   These results altogether confirm that 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition is
   stereospecific, giving retention of both the 1,3-dipole and the
   dipolarophile.
   [243]doi:10.1021/ja00983a052 doi:10.1021/ja00983a052

Diastereoselectivity

   [[244]edit]

   When two or more [245]stereocenters are generated during the reaction,
   diastereomeric transition states and products can be obtained. In the
   Diels-Alder cycloaddition, the [246]endo [247]diastereoselectivity due
   to [248]secondary orbital interactions is usually observed. In
   1,3-dipolar cycloadditions, however, two forces influence the
   diastereoselectivity: the attractive [249]π-interaction (resembling
   secondary orbital interactions in the Diels-Alder cycloaddition) and
   the repulsive [250]steric interaction. Unfortunately, these two forces
   often cancel each other, causing poor diastereoselection in 1,3-dipolar
   cycloaddition.

   Examples of substrate-controlled diastereoselective 1,3-dipolar
   cycloadditions are shown below. First is the reaction between
   benzonitrile N-benzylide and [251]methyl acrylate. In the transition
   state, the phenyl and the methyl ester groups stack to give the
   cis-substitution as the exclusive final [252]pyrroline product. This
   favorable π-interaction offsets the steric repulsion between the phenyl
   and the methyl ester groups.^[253][23] Second is the reaction between
   nitrone and [254]dihydrofuran. The exo-selectivity is achieved to
   minimize steric repulsion.^[255][24] Last is the intramolecular
   azomethine ylide reaction with alkene. The diastereoselectivity is
   controlled by the formation of a less strained cis-[256]fused ring
   system.^[257][25]
   [258]doi:10.1021/ja00731a056 doi:10.1021/ja00731a056

Directed 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition

   [[259]edit]

   Trajectory of the cycloaddition can be controlled to achieve a
   diastereoselective reaction. For example, metals can [260]chelate to
   the dipolarophile and the incoming dipole and direct the cycloaddition
   selectively on one face. The example below shows addition of nitrile
   oxide to an [261]enantiomerically pure [262]allyl alcohol in the
   presence of a magnesium ion. The most stable [263]conformation of the
   alkene places the [264]hydroxyl group above the plane of the alkene.
   The magnesium then chelates to the hydroxyl group and the oxygen atom
   of nitrile oxide. The cycloaddition thus comes from the top face
   selectively.^[265][26]
   [266][lossy-page1-450px-Directed_dipolar_cycloaddition.tif.jpg]

   Such diastereodirection has been applied in the synthesis of
   [267]epothilones.^[268][27]
   [269][lossy-page1-600px-Use_of_directed_cycloaddition_in_Epothilones_sy
   nthesis.tif.jpg]

Regioselectivity

   [[270]edit]

   For asymmetric dipole-dipolarophile pairs, two [271]regioisomeric
   products are possible. Both [272]electronic/stereoelectronic and steric
   factors contribute to the regioselectivity of 1,3-dipolar
   cycloadditions.^[273][28]

Electronic/stereoelectronic effect

   [[274]edit]

   The dominant electronic interaction is the combination between the
   largest HOMO and the largest LUMO. Therefore, regioselectivity is
   governed by the atoms that bear the largest orbital HOMO and LUMO
   coefficients.^[275][29]^[276][30]

   For example, consider the cycloaddition of diazomethane to three
   dipolarophiles: [277]methyl acrylate, [278]styrene or [279]methyl
   cinnamate. The carbon of diazomethane bears the largest HOMO, while the
   end olefinic carbons of methyl acrylate and styrene bear the largest
   LUMO. Hence, cycloaddition gives the substitution at the C-3 position
   regioselectively. For methyl cinnamate, the two substituents (Ph v.s.
   COOMe) compete at withdrawing electrons from the alkene. The carboxyl
   is the better electron-withdrawing group, causing the β-carbon to be
   most electrophilic. Thus, cycloaddition yields the [280]carboxyl group
   on C-3 and the [281]phenyl group on C-4 regioselectively.
   [282]doi:10.1021/ja00444a013 and doi:10.1021/ja00436a062
   doi:10.1021/ja00444a013 and doi:10.1021/ja00436a062

Steric effect

   [[283]edit]

   Steric effects can either cooperate or compete with the aforementioned
   electronic effects. Sometimes steric effects completely outweighs the
   electronic preference, giving the opposite regioisomer
   exclusively.^[284][31]

   For example, diazomethane generally adds to methyl acrylate to give
   3-carboxyl [285]pyrazoline. However, by putting more steric demands
   into the system, we start to observe the isomeric 4-carboxyl
   pyrazolines. The ratio of these two regioisomers depends on the steric
   demands. At the extreme, increasing the size from [286]hydrogen to
   [287]t-butyl shifts the regioselectivity from 100% 3-carboxyl to 100%
   4-carboxyl substitution.^[288][32]^[289][33]

   [291]ISBN [292]0-471-08364-X. and Koszinowski, J. (1980) (PhD Thesis)

Synthetic applications

   [[293]edit]

   1,3-dipolar cycloadditions are important ways toward the synthesis of
   many important 5-membered heterocycles such as [294]triazoles,
   [295]furans, [296]isoxazoles, [297]pyrrolidines, and others.
   Additionally, some cycloadducts can be cleaved to reveal the linear
   skeleton, providing another route toward the synthesis of
   [298]aliphatic compounds. These reactions are tremendously useful also
   because they are stereospecific, diastereoselective and regioselective.
   Several examples are provided below.

Nitrile oxides

   [[299]edit]

   1,3-dipolar cycloaddition with nitrile oxides is a widely used
   masked-[300]aldol reaction. Cycloaddition between a nitrile oxide and
   an alkene yields the cyclic isoxazoline product, whereas the reaction
   with an alkyne yields the isoxazole. Both isoxazolines and isoxazoles
   can be cleaved by [301]hydrogenation to reveal aldol-type
   β-hydroxycarbonyl or [302]Claisen-type β-dicarbonyl products,
   respectively.

Carbonyl ylides

   [[305]edit]

   1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reactions have emerged as powerful tools in
   the synthesis of complex cyclic scaffolds and molecules for medicinal,
   biological, and mechanistic studies. Among them, [3+2]
   [306]cycloaddition reactions involving carbonyl ylides have extensively
   been employed to generate oxygen-containing five-membered cyclic
   molecules.^[307][35]

Preparation of carbonyl ylides for 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reactions

   [[308]edit]

   [309]Ylides are regarded as positively charged [310]heteroatoms
   connected to negatively charged carbon atoms, which include ylides of
   [311]sulfonium, [312]thiocarbonyl, [313]oxonium, [314]nitrogen, and
   [315]carbonyl.^[316][36] Several methods exist for generating carbonyl
   ylides, which are necessary intermediates for generating
   oxygen-containing five-membered ring structures, for [3+2]
   cycloaddition reactions.

Synthesis of carbonyl ylides from diazomethane derivatives by photocatalysis

   [[317]edit]

   One of the earliest examples of carbonyl ylide [318]synthesis involves
   [319]photocatalysis.^[320][37] [321]Photolysis of
   diazotetrakis(trifluoromethyl)cyclopentadiene* (DTTC) in the presence
   of [322]tetramethylurea can generate the carbonyl ylide by an
   [323]intermolecular [324]nucleophilic attack and subsequent
   [325]aromatization of the DTTC moiety.^[326][37] This was isolated and
   characterized by [327]X-ray crystallography due to the stability
   imparted by aromaticity, [328]electron withdrawing trifluoromethyl
   groups, and the [329]electron donating dimethylamine groups. Stable
   carbonyl ylide [330]dipoles can then be used in [3+2] cycloaddition
   reactions with dipolarophiles.

   105, 5929. Scheme 1. Photolysis of DTTC in the presence of
   tetramethylurea. Modified from Janulis, E. P.; Arduengo, A. J. J. Am.
   Chem. Soc. 1983, 105, 5929.

   Another early example of carbonyl ylide synthesis by photocatalysis was
   reported by Olah et al.^[332][38] Dideuteriodiazomethane was photolysed
   in the presence of [333]formaldehyde to generate the
   dideuterioformaldehyde carbonyl ylide.

Synthesis of carbonyl ylides from hydroxypyrones by proton transfer

   [[335]edit]

   Carbonyl ylides can be synthesized by [336]acid catalysis of
   hydroxy-3-pyrones in the absence of a metal [337]catalyst.^[338][39] An
   initial [339]tautomerization occurs, followed by [340]elimination of
   the [341]leaving group to aromatize the [342]pyrone ring and to
   generate the carbonyl ylide. A cycloaddition reaction with a
   dipolarophile lastly forms the oxacycle. This approach is less widely
   employed due to its limited utility and requirement for pyrone
   skeletons.

   carbonyl ylides from hydroxy-3-pyrones. Modified from Sammes, P. G.;
   Street, L. J. J. Chem. Soc., Chem. Commun. 1982, 1056.

   5-hydroxy-4-pyrones can also be used to synthesize carbonyl ylides by
   an [344]intramolecular [345]hydrogen transfer.^[346][40] After hydrogen
   transfer, the carbonyl ylide can then react with dipolarophiles to form
   oxygen-containing rings.

Synthesis of α-halocarbonyl ylides from dihalocarbenes

   [[348]edit]

   Dihalocarbenes have also been employed to generate carbonyl ylides,
   exploiting the electron withdrawing nature of
   dihalocarbenes.^[349][41]^[350][42]^[351][43] Both
   [352]phenyl(trichloromethyl)mercury and phenyl(tribromomethyl)mercury
   are sources [353]dichlorocarbenes and [354]dibromocarbenes,
   respectively. The carbonyl ylide can be generated upon reaction of the
   dihalocarbenes with [355]ketones or [356]aldehydes. However, the
   synthesis of α-halocarbonyl ylides can also undesirably lead to the
   loss of [357]carbon monoxide and the generation of the deoxygenation
   product.

Synthesis of carbonyl ylides from diazomethane derivatives by metal catalysis

   [[359]edit]

   A universal approach for generating carbonyl ylides involves [360]metal

Mechanism of the 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reaction mediated by metal
catalysis of diazocarbonyl compounds

   [[369]edit]

   The universality and extensive use of 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition
   reactions mediated by metal catalysis of diazocarbonyl molecules, for
   synthesizing oxygen-containing five-membered rings, has spurred
   significant interest into its mechanism. Several groups have
   investigated the [370]mechanism to expand the scope of synthetic
   molecules with respect to [371]regio- and [372]stereo-selectivity.
   However, due to the high turn over frequencies of these reactions, the
   intermediates and mechanism remains elusive. The generally accepted
   mechanism, developed by characterization of stable ruthenium-carbenoid
   complexes^[373][47] and rhodium metallocarbenes,^[374][48] involves an
   initial formation of a metal-carbenoid complex from the [375]diazo
   compound. Elimination of nitrogen gas then affords a metallocarbene. An
   intramolecular nucleophilic attack by the carbonyl oxygen regenerates
   the metal catalyst and forms the carbonyl ylide. The carbonyl ylide can
   then react with an alkene or alkyne, such as dimethyl
   acetylenedicarboxylate (DMAD) to generate the oxacycle.
   [376]Scheme 7. Accepted mechanism of the 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition
   reaction mediated by metal catalysis (example dirhodium catalyst) of
   diazocarbonyl compounds. Modified from M. Hodgson, D.; H. Labande, A.;
   Muthusamy, S. In Organic Reactions; John Wiley & Sons, Inc.: 2004.
   Scheme 7. Accepted mechanism of the 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reaction
   mediated by metal catalysis (example dirhodium catalyst) of
   diazocarbonyl compounds. Modified from M. Hodgson, D.; H. Labande, A.;
   Muthusamy, S. In Organic Reactions; John Wiley & Sons, Inc.: 2004.

   However, it is uncertain whether the metallocarbene intermediate
   generates the carbonyl ylide. In some cases, metallocarbenes can also
   react directly with dipolarophiles.^[377][49] In these cases, the
   metallocarbene, such as the dirhodium(II)tetracarboxylate carbene, is
   stabilized through [378]hyperconjugative metal [379]enolate-type
   interactions.^[380][50]^[381][51]^[382][52]^[383][53] Subsequent
   1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reaction occurs through a transient
   metal-complexed carbonyl ylide. Therefore, a persistent metallocarbene
   can influence the stereoselectivity and regioselectivity of the
   1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reaction based on the stereochemistry and
   size of the metal [384]ligands.
   [385]The dirhodium(II)tetracarboxylate metallocarbene stabilized by
   πC-Rh→πC=O hyperconjugation. Modified from M. Hodgson, D.; H. Labande,
   A.; Muthusamy, S. In Organic Reactions; John Wiley & Sons, Inc.: 2004.
   The dirhodium(II)tetracarboxylate metallocarbene stabilized by
   πC-Rh→πC=O hyperconjugation. Modified from M. Hodgson, D.; H. Labande,
   A.; Muthusamy, S. In Organic Reactions; John Wiley & Sons, Inc.: 2004.

   The mechanism of the 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reaction between the
   carbonyl ylide dipole and [386]alkynyl or [387]alkenyl dipolarophiles
   has been extensively investigated with respect to regioselectivity and
   stereoselectivity. As [388]symmetric dipolarophiles have one
   orientation for cycloaddition, only one [389]regioisomer, but multiple
   [390]stereoisomers can be obtained.^[391][53] On the contrary,
   [392]unsymmetric dipolarophiles can have multiple regioisomers and
   stereoisomers. These regioisomers and stereoisomers may be predicted
   based on [393]frontier molecular orbital (FMO) theory, [394]steric
   interactions, and [395]stereoelectronic
   interactions.^[396][54]^[397][55]
   [398]Scheme 9. Products of the 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reaction
   between carbonyl ylide dipoles and alkenyl or alkynyl dipolarophiles.
   Modified from M. Hodgson, D.; H. Labande, A.; Muthusamy, S. In Organic
   Reactions; John Wiley & Sons, Inc.: 2004. Scheme 9. Products of the
   1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reaction between carbonyl ylide dipoles and
   alkenyl or alkynyl dipolarophiles. Modified from M. Hodgson, D.; H.
   Labande, A.; Muthusamy, S. In Organic Reactions; John Wiley & Sons,
   Inc.: 2004.

Regioselectivity of the 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reaction mediated by metal
catalysis of diazocarbonyl compounds

   [[399]edit]

   Regioselectivity of 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reactions between
   carbonyl ylide dipoles and alkynyl or alkenyl dipolarophiles is
   essential for generating molecules with defined regiochemistry. FMO
   theory and analysis of the [400]HOMO-LUMO energy gaps between the
   dipole and dipolarophile can rationalize and predict the
   regioselectivity of experimental outcomes.^[401][56]^[402][57] The
   HOMOs and LUMOs can belong to either the dipole or dipolarophile, for
   which HOMO[dipole]-LUMO[dipolarophile] or
   HOMO[dipolarophile]-LUMO[dipole] interactions can exist. Overlap of the
   [403]orbitals with the largest coefficients can ultimately rationalize
   and predict results.
   [404]Scheme 10. diagram of the molecular orbital interactions of
   HOMOdipole-LUMOdipolarophile or HOMOdipolarophile-LUMOdipole between a
   carbonyl ylide dipole and alkenyl dipolarophile. Scheme 10. diagram of
   the molecular orbital interactions of HOMOdipole-LUMOdipolarophile or
   HOMOdipolarophile-LUMOdipole between a carbonyl ylide dipole and
   alkenyl dipolarophile.

   The archetypal regioselectivity of the 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition
   reaction mediated by carbonyl ylide dipoles has been examined by Padwa
   and coworkers.^[405][55]^[406][58] Using a Rh[2](OAc)[4] catalyst in
   benzene, diazodione underwent a 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reaction with
   [407]methyl propiolate and methyl [408]propargyl [409]ether. The
   reaction with [410]methyl propiolate affords two regioisomers with the
   major resulting from the HOMO[dipole]-LUMO[dipolarophile] interaction,
   which has the largest coefficients on the carbon proximal to the
   carbonyl group of the carbonyl ylide and on the methyl propiolate
   terminal alkyne carbon. The reaction with methyl propargyl ether
   affords one regioisomer resulting from the
   HOMO[dipolarophile]-LUMO[dipole] interaction, which has largest
   coefficients on the carbon distal to the carbonyl group of the carbonyl
   ylide and on the methyl propargyl ether terminal alkyne carbon.
   [411]Scheme 11. Regioselectivity and molecular orbital interactions of
   the 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reaction between a diazodione and methyl
   propiolate or methyl propargyl ether. Modified from Padwa, A.;
   Weingarten, M. D. Chem Rev 1996, 96, 223. Scheme 11. Regioselectivity
   and molecular orbital interactions of the 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition
   reaction between a diazodione and methyl propiolate or methyl propargyl
   ether. Modified from Padwa, A.; Weingarten, M. D. Chem Rev 1996, 96,
   223.

   Regioselectivities of 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reactions mediated by
   metal catalysis of diazocarbonyl compounds may also be influenced by
   the metal through formation of stable
   metallocarbenes.^[412][49]^[413][59] Stabilization of the
   metallocarbene, via metal enolate-type interactions, will prevent the
   formation of carbonyl ylides, resulting in a direct reaction between
   the metallocarbene dipole and an alkynyl or alkenyl dipolarophile (see
   image of The dirhodium(II)tetracarboxylate metallocarbene stabilized by
   π[C-Rh]→π[C=O] hyperconjugation.). In this situation, the metal ligands
   will influence the regioselectivity and stereoselectivity of the
   1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reaction.

Stereoselectivity and asymmetric induction of the 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition
reaction mediated by metal catalysis of diazocarbonyl compounds

   [[414]edit]

   The [415]stereoselectivity of 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reactions
   between carbonyl ylide dipoles and alkenyl dipolarophiles has also been
   closely examined. For alkynyl dipolarophiles, stereoselectivity is not
   an issue as relatively planar sp^2 carbons are formed, while
   regioselectivity must be considered (see image of the Products of the
   1,3-Dipolar Cycloaddition Reaction Between Carbonyl Ylide Dipoles and
   Alkenyl or Alkynyl Dipolarophiles). However, for alkenyl
   dipolarophiles, both regioselectivity and stereoselectivity must be
   considered as sp^3 carbons are generated in the product species.

   1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reactions between carbonyl ylide dipoles and
   alkenyl dipolarophiles can generate [416]diastereomeric
   products.^[417][53] The [418]exo product is characterized with
   dipolarophile substituents being [419]cis to the ether bridge of the
   oxacycle. The [420]endo product is characterized with the dipolarophile
   substituents being [421]trans to the ether bridge of the oxacycle. Both
   products can be generated through [422]pericyclic transitions states
   involving [423]concerted synchronous or concerted asynchronous
   processes.

   One early example conferred stereoselectivity in terms of endo and exo
   products with metal catalysts and Lewis acids.^[424][60] Reactions with
   just the metal catalyst Rh[2](OAc)[4] prefer the exo product while
   reactions with the additional Lewis acid Yb(OTf)[3] prefer the endo
   product. The endo selectivity observed for Lewis acid cycloaddition
   reactions is attributed to the optimized orbital overlap of the
   carbonyl π systems between the dipolarophile coordinated by Yb(Otf)[3]
   (LUMO) and the dipole (HOMO). After many investigations, two primary
   approaches for influencing the stereoselectivity of carbonyl ylide
   cycloadditions have been developed that exploit the chirality of metal
   catalysts and Lewis acids.^[425][53]
   [426]Facial Selectivity of the 1,3-Dipolar Cycloaddition Reaction using
   a Metal Catalyst and Lewis Acid Facial Selectivity of the 1,3-Dipolar
   Cycloaddition Reaction using a Metal Catalyst and Lewis
   Acid[427]Rationale for the Endo Selectivity of the 1,3-Dipolar
   Cycloaddition Reaction with a Lewis Acid Rationale for the Endo
   Selectivity of the 1,3-Dipolar Cycloaddition Reaction with a Lewis Acid

   The first approach employs chiral metal catalysts to modulate the endo
   and exo stereoselectivity. The chiral catalysts, in particular
   Rh[2][(S)-DOSP][4] and Rh[2][(S)-BPTV][4] can induce modest asymmetric
   induction and was used to synthesize the [428]antifungal agent
   pseudolaric acid A.^[429][61] This is a result of the [430]chiral metal
   catalyst remaining associated with the carbonyl ylide during the
   cycloaddition, which confers facial selectivity. However, the exact
   mechanisms are not yet fully understood.
   [431]Asymmetric induction of the 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reaction
   with chiral metal catalysts Asymmetric induction of the 1,3-dipolar
   cycloaddition reaction with chiral metal catalysts

   The second approach employs a chiral Lewis acid catalyst to induce
   facial stereoselectivity after the generation of the carbonyl ylide
   using an achiral metal catalyst.^[432][62] The chiral Lewis acid
   catalyst is believed to coordinate to the dipolarophile, which lowers
   the LUMO of the dipolarophile while also leading to
   [433]enantioselectivity.
   [434]Asymmetric induction of the 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reaction
   with chiral Lewis acid catalysts Asymmetric induction of the
   1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reaction with chiral Lewis acid catalysts

Ozone

   [[440]edit]

   [441]Ozonolysis is a very important organic reaction. Alkenes and
   alkynes can be cleaved by ozonolysis to give [442]aldehyde, [443]ketone
   or [444]carboxylic acid products.

Biological applications

   [[445]edit]

   The 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition between organic azides and terminal
   alkynes (i.e., the [446]Huisgen cycloaddition) has been widely utilized
   for [447]bioconjugation.

Copper catalysis

   [[448]edit]

   The Huisgen reaction generally does not proceed readily under mild
   conditions. Meldal et al. and Sharpless et al. independently developed
   a [449]copper(I)-catalyzed version of the Huisgen reaction, CuAAC (for
   Copper-catalyzed Azide-Alkyne Cycloaddition), which proceeds very
   readily in mild, including [450]physiological, conditions (neutral
   [451]pH, room [452]temperature and [453]water
   solution).^[454][64]^[455][65] This reaction is also
   [456]bioorthogonal: azides and alkynes are both generally absent from
   biological systems and therefore these functionalities can be
   [457]chemoselectively reacted even in the [458]cellular context. They
   also do not react with other functional groups found in nature, so they
   do not perturb biological systems. The reaction is so versatile that it
   is termed the [459]"Click" chemistry. Although copper(I) is [460]toxic,
   many protective [461]ligands have been developed to both reduce
   cytotoxicity and improve rate of CuAAC, allowing it to be used in
   [462]in vivo studies.^[463][66]
   [464][lossy-page1-350px-Copper_catalyzed_AAC.tif.jpg]

   For example, Bertozzi et al. reported the [465]metabolic incorporation
   of azide-functionalized [466]saccharides into the [467]glycan of the
   [468]cell membrane, and subsequent labeling with
   [469]fluorophore-alkyne conjugate. The result is that the cell membrane
   is [470]fluorescently labeled, and can therefore be [471]imaged using a
   [472]fluorescence microscope.^[473][67]
   [474][lossy-page1-800px-Metabolic_labeling_with_GlcNAz_and_click_chemis
   try.tif.jpg]

Strain-promoted cycloaddition

   [[475]edit]

   To avoid toxicity of copper(I), Bertozzi et al. developed the
   strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC) between organic
   azide and strained [476]cyclooctyne. The angle distortion of the
   cyclooctyne helps to speed up the reaction by both reducing the
   activation strain and enhancing the interactions, thereby enabling it
   to be used in physiological conditions without the need for the
   catalyst.^[477][68]
   [478][lossy-page1-350px-Strained_promoted_AAC.tif.jpg]

   For instance, Ting et al. introduced an azido functionality onto
   specific [479]proteins on the [480]cell surface using a [481]ligase
   enzyme. The azide-tagged protein is then labeled with
   cyclooctyne-fluorophore conjugate to yield a fluorescently labeled
   protein.^[482][69]
   [483][lossy-page1-650px-Enzyme-mediated_labeling_with_azidooctanoic_aci
   d_and_SPAAC.tif.jpg]
