   From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
   (Redirected from [103]1-tuple)
   Finite ordered list of elements
   For the musical term, see [104]Tuplet. "Octuple" redirects here. For
   the boat, see [105]Octuple scull. "Duodecuple" redirects here. For the
   musical technique, see [106]Twelve-tone technique. "Sextuple" redirects
   here. For the sporting achievement of association football, see
   [107]Sextuple (association football).

   In [108]mathematics, a tuple is a finite [109]sequence or ordered list
   of [110]numbers or, more generally, [111]mathematical objects, which
   are called the elements of the tuple. An n-tuple is a tuple of n
   elements, where n is a non-negative [112]integer. There is only one
   0-tuple, called the empty tuple. A 1-tuple and a 2-tuple are commonly
   called a [113]singleton and an [114]ordered pair, respectively. The
   term "infinite tuple" is occasionally used for "infinite sequences".

   Tuples are usually written by listing the elements within parentheses
   "( )" and separated by commas; for example, (2, 7, 4, 1, 7) denotes a
   5-tuple. Other types of brackets are sometimes used, although they may
   have a different meaning.^[115][a]

   An n-tuple can be formally defined as the [116]image of a [117]function
   that has the set of the n first [118]natural numbers as its
   [119]domain. Tuples may be also defined from ordered pairs by a
   [120]recurrence starting from [121]ordered pairs; indeed, an n-tuple
   can be identified with the ordered pair of its (n − 1) first elements
   and its nth element.

   In [122]computer science, tuples come in many forms. Most typed
   [123]functional programming languages implement tuples directly as
   [124]product types,^[125][1] tightly associated with [126]algebraic
   data types, [127]pattern matching, and [128]destructuring
   assignment.^[129][2] Many programming languages offer an alternative to
   tuples, known as [130]record types, featuring unordered elements
   accessed by label.^[131][3] A few programming languages combine ordered
   tuple product types and unordered record types into a single construct,
   as in [132]C structs and Haskell records. [133]Relational databases may
   formally identify their [134]rows (records) as tuples.

   Tuples also occur in [135]relational algebra; when programming the
   [136]semantic web with the [137]Resource Description Framework (RDF);
   in [138]linguistics;^[139][4] and in [140]philosophy.^[141][5]

Etymology

   [[142]edit]

   The term originated as an abstraction of the sequence: single,
   couple/double, triple, quadruple, quintuple, sextuple, septuple,
   octuple, ..., n‑tuple, ..., where the prefixes are taken from the
   [143]Latin names of the numerals. The unique 0-tuple is called the null
   tuple or empty tuple. A 1‑tuple is called a single (or singleton), a
   2‑tuple is called an ordered pair or couple, and a 3‑tuple is called a
   triple (or triplet). The number n can be any nonnegative [144]integer.
   For example, a [145]complex number can be represented as a 2‑tuple of
   reals, a [146]quaternion can be represented as a 4‑tuple, an
   [147]octonion can be represented as an 8‑tuple, and a [148]sedenion can
   be represented as a 16‑tuple.

   Although these uses treat ‑uple as the suffix, the original suffix was
   ‑ple as in "triple" (three-fold) or "decuple" (ten‑fold). This
   originates from [149]medieval Latin plus (meaning "more") related to
   [150]Greek ‑πλοῦς, which replaced the classical and late antique ‑plex
   (meaning "folded"), as in "duplex".^[151][6]^[152][b]

n-tuples of m-sets

   [[170]edit]

   In [171]discrete mathematics, especially [172]combinatorics and finite
   [173]probability theory, n-tuples arise in the context of various
   counting problems and are treated more informally as ordered lists of
   length n.^[174][7] n-tuples whose entries come from a set of m elements
   are also called arrangements with repetition, [175]permutations of a
   multiset and, in some non-English literature, variations with
   repetition. The number of n-tuples of an m-set is m^n. This follows
   from the combinatorial [176]rule of product.^[177][8] If S is a finite
   set of [178]cardinality m, this number is the cardinality of the n-fold
   [179]Cartesian power S × S × ⋯ × S. Tuples are elements of this product
   set.
