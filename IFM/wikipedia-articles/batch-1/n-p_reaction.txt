   The (n-p) reaction, or (n,p) reaction, is an example of a [64]nuclear
   reaction. It is the reaction which occurs when a [65]neutron enters a
   [66]nucleus and a [67]proton leaves the nucleus simultaneously.^[68][1]

   For example, [69]sulfur-32 (^32S) undergoes an (n,p) nuclear reaction
   when [70]bombarded with neutrons, thus forming [71]phosphorus-32
   (^32P).

   The nuclide [72]nitrogen-14 (^14N) can also undergo an (n,p) nuclear
   reaction to produce [73]carbon-14 (^14C). This nuclear reaction ^14N
   (n,p) ^14C continually happens in the Earth's atmosphere, forming
   equilibrium amounts of the radionuclide ^14C.

   Most (n,p) reactions have threshold neutron energies below which the
   reaction cannot take place as a result of the charged particle in the
   exit channel requiring energy (usually more than a MeV) to overcome the
   [74]Coulomb barrier experienced by the emitted proton. The (n,p)
   nuclear reaction ^14N (n,p) ^14C is an exception to this rule, and is
   exothermic – it can take place at all incident neutron
   energies.^[[75]citation needed] The ^14N (n,p) ^14C nuclear reaction is
   responsible for most of the radiation dose delivered to the human body
   by thermal neutrons – these thermal neutrons are absorbed by the
   nitrogen ^14N in proteins, causing a proton to be emitted; the emitted
   proton deposits its kinetic energy over a very short distance in the
   body tissue, thereby depositing radiation dose.
