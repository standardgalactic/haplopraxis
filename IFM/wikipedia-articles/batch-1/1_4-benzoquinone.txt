   Chemical compound

   1,4-Benzoquinone, commonly known as para-quinone, is a [160]chemical
   compound with the [161]formula C[6]H[4]O[2]. In a pure state, it forms
   bright-yellow crystals with a characteristic irritating odor,
   resembling that of [162]chlorine, [163]bleach, and hot plastic or
   formaldehyde. This six-membered ring compound is the oxidized
   derivative of 1,4-[164]hydroquinone.^[165][4] The molecule is
   multifunctional: it exhibits properties of a [166]ketone, being able to
   form [167]oximes; an oxidant, forming the dihydroxy derivative; and an
   alkene, undergoing addition reactions, especially those typical for
   [168]α,β-unsaturated ketones. 1,4-Benzoquinone is sensitive toward both
   strong mineral acids and alkali, which cause condensation and
   decomposition of the compound.^[169][5]^[170][6]

Preparation

   [[171]edit]

   1,4-Benzoquinone is prepared industrially by oxidation of
   [172]hydroquinone, which can be obtained by several routes. One route
   involves oxidation of [173]diisopropylbenzene and the [174]Hock
   rearrangement. The net reaction can be represented as follows:

          C[6]H[4](CHMe[2])[2] + 3 O[2] → C[6]H[4]O[2] + 2 OCMe[2] + H[2]O

   The reaction proceeds via the bis([175]hydroperoxide) and the
   hydroquinone. Acetone is a coproduct.^[176][7]

   Another major process involves the direct hydroxylation of [177]phenol
   by acidic [178]hydrogen peroxide: C[6]H[5]OH + H[2]O[2] →
   C[6]H[4](OH)[2] + H[2]O Both hydroquinone and [179]catechol are
   produced. Subsequent oxidation of the hydroquinone gives the
   quinone.^[180][8]

   Quinone was originally prepared industrially by oxidation of
   [181]aniline, for example by [182]manganese dioxide.^[183][9] This
   method is mainly practiced in PRC where environmental regulations are
   more relaxed.

   Oxidation of hydroquinone is facile.^[184][4]^[185][10] One such method
   makes use of [186]hydrogen peroxide as the [187]oxidizer and
   [188]iodine or an iodine salt as a catalyst for the oxidation occurring
   in a [189]polar solvent; e.g. [190]isopropyl alcohol.^[191][11]

   When heated to near its melting point, 1,4-benzoquinone [192]sublimes,
   even at atmospheric pressure, allowing for an effective purification.
   Impure samples are often dark-colored due to the presence of
   quinhydrone, a dark green 1:1 [193]charge-transfer complex of quinone
   with [194]hydroquinone.^[195][12]

Structure and redox

   [[196]edit]

   [197][220px-DistancesQuinone.png] C–C and C–O bond distances in
   benzoquinone (Q), its 1e reduced derivative (Q^−), and hydroquinone
   (H[2]Q).^[198][13]

   Benzoquinone is a planar molecule with localized, alternating C=C, C=O,
   and C–C bonds. Reduction gives the semiquinone anion C[6]H[4]O[2]^−},
   which adopts a more delocalized structure. Further reduction coupled to
   protonation gives the hydroquinone, wherein the C6 ring is fully
   delocalized.^[199][13]

Reactions and applications

   [[200]edit]

   Quinone is mainly used as a precursor to hydroquinone, which is used in
   [201]photography and rubber manufacture as a reducing agent and
   antioxidant.^[202][8] Benzoquinonium is a skeletal muscle relaxant,
   ganglion blocking agent that is made from benzoquinone.^[203][14]

Organic synthesis

   [[204]edit]

   It is used as a hydrogen acceptor and oxidant in [205]organic
   synthesis.^[206][15] 1,4-Benzoquinone serves as a [207]dehydrogenation
   reagent. It is also used as a dienophile in [208]Diels Alder
   reactions.^[209][16]

   Benzoquinone reacts with [210]acetic anhydride and [211]sulfuric acid
   to give the triacetate of [212]hydroxyquinol.^[213][17]^[214][18] This
   reaction is called the Thiele reaction or Thiele–Winter
   reaction^[215][19]^[216][20] after [217]Johannes Thiele, who first
   described it in 1898, and after Ernst Winter, who further described its
   [218]reaction mechanism in 1900. An application is found in this step
   of the total synthesis of Metachromin A:^[219][21]

          [220]An application of the Thiele reaction, involving a
          benzoquinone derivative. An application of the Thiele reaction,
          involving a benzoquinone derivative.

   Benzoquinone is also used to suppress double-bond migration during
   [221]olefin metathesis reactions.

   An acidic [222]potassium iodide solution reduces a solution of
   benzoquinone to hydroquinone, which can be reoxidized back to the
   quinone with a solution of [223]silver nitrate.

   Due to its ability to function as an oxidizer, 1,4-benzoquinone can be
   found in methods using the [224]Wacker-Tsuji oxidation, wherein a
   palladium salt catalyzes the conversion of an alkene to a ketone. This
   reaction is typically carried out using pressurized oxygen as the
   oxidizer, but benzoquinone can sometimes preferred. It is also used as
   a reagent in some variants on [225]Wacker oxidations.

   1,4-Benzoquinone is used in the synthesis of [226]Bromadol and related
   analogs.
   [227][220px-FAMCOM.png] Structure of Cp*Rh(para-quinone).^[228][22]

Related 1,4-benzoquinones

   [[229]edit]

   Further information: [230]Quinone

   [231]2,3-Dichloro-5,6-dicyano-1,4-benzoquinone (DDQ) is a stronger
   oxidant and dehydrogenation agent than 1,4-benzoquinone.^[232][23]
   [233]Chloranil 1,4-C[6]Cl[4]O[2] is another potent oxidant and
   dehydrogenation agent. Monochloro-p-benzoquinone is yet another but
   milder oxidant.^[234][24]

Metabolism

   [[235]edit]

   1,4-Benzoquinone is a toxic metabolite found in human blood and can be
   used to track exposure to [236]benzene or mixtures containing benzene
   and benzene compounds, such as petrol.^[237][25] The compound can
   interfere with cellular respiration, and kidney damage has been found
   in animals receiving severe exposure. It is excreted in its original
   form and also as variations of its own metabolite,
   hydroquinone.^[238][9]

Safety

   [[239]edit]

   [240][220px-Brachinus_spPCCA20060328-2821B.jpg] The [241]bombardier
   beetle sprays 1,4-Benzoquinone to deter predators

   1,4-Benzoquinone is able to stain skin dark brown, cause [242]erythema
   (redness, rashes on skin) and lead on to localized tissue
   [243]necrosis. It is particularly irritating to the eyes and
   respiratory system. Its ability to sublime at commonly encountered
   temperatures allows for a greater airborne exposure risk than might be
   expected for a room-temperature solid. [244]IARC has found insufficient
   evidence to comment on the compound's carcinogenicity, but has noted
   that it can easily pass into the bloodstream and that it showed
   activity in depressing bone marrow production in mice and can inhibit
   [245]protease [246]enzymes involved in cellular
   [247]apoptosis.^[248][9]
