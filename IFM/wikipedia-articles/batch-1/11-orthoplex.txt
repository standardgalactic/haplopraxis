   In [81]geometry, a cross-polytope,^[82][1] hyperoctahedron,
   orthoplex,^[83][2] staurotope,^[84][3] or cocube is a [85]regular,
   [86]convex polytope that exists in n-[87]dimensional Euclidean space. A
   2-dimensional cross-polytope is a square, a 3-dimensional
   cross-polytope is a regular [88]octahedron, and a 4-dimensional
   cross-polytope is a [89]16-cell. Its facets are [90]simplexes of the
   previous dimension, while the cross-polytope's [91]vertex figure is
   another cross-polytope from the previous dimension.

   The vertices of a cross-polytope can be chosen as the unit vectors
   pointing along each co-ordinate axis – i.e. all the permutations of
   (±1, 0, 0, ..., 0). The cross-polytope is the [92]convex hull of its
   vertices. The n-dimensional cross-polytope can also be defined as the
   closed [93]unit ball (or, according to some authors, its boundary) in
   the [94]ℓ[1]-norm on R^n:

   In 1 dimension the cross-polytope is simply the [95]line segment [−1,
   +1], in 2 dimensions it is a [96]square (or diamond) with vertices
   {(±1, 0), (0, ±1)}. In 3 dimensions it is an [97]octahedron—one of the
   five convex regular [98]polyhedra known as the [99]Platonic solids.
   This can be generalised to higher dimensions with an n-orthoplex being
   constructed as a [100]bipyramid with an (n−1)-orthoplex base.

   The cross-polytope is the [101]dual polytope of the [102]hypercube. The
   1-[103]skeleton of an n-dimensional cross-polytope is the [104]Turán
   graph T(2n, n) (also known as a cocktail party graph ^[105][4]).

4 dimensions

   [[106]edit]

   The 4-dimensional cross-polytope also goes by the name hexadecachoron
   or [107]16-cell. It is one of the six [108]convex regular 4-polytopes.
   These [109]4-polytopes were first described by the Swiss mathematician
   [110]Ludwig Schläfli in the mid-19th century.

Higher dimensions

   [[111]edit]

   The cross-polytope family is one of three [112]regular polytope
   families, labeled by [113]Coxeter as β[n], the other two being the
   [114]hypercube family, labeled as γ[n], and the [115]simplex family,
   labeled as α[n]. A fourth family, the [116]infinite tessellations of
   hypercubes, he labeled as δ[n].^[117][5]

   The n-dimensional cross-polytope has 2n vertices, and 2^n facets
   ((n − 1)-dimensional components) all of which are
   (n − 1)-[118]simplices. The [119]vertex figures are all
   (n − 1)-cross-polytopes. The [120]Schläfli symbol of the cross-polytope
   is {3,3,...,3,4}.

   The extended [124]f-vector for an n-orthoplex can be computed by
   (1,2)^n, like the coefficients of [125]polynomial products. For example
   a 16-cell is (1,2)^4 = (1,4,4)^2 = (1,8,24,32,16).

   There are many possible [126]orthographic projections that can show the
   cross-polytopes as 2-dimensional graphs. [127]Petrie polygon
   projections map the points into a regular 2n-gon or lower order regular
   polygons. A second projection takes the 2(n−1)-gon petrie polygon of
   the lower dimension, seen as a [128]bipyramid, projected down the axis,
   with 2 vertices mapped into the center.

   The vertices of an axis-aligned cross polytope are all at equal
   distance from each other in the [169]Manhattan distance ([170]L^1
   norm). [171]Kusner's conjecture states that this set of 2d points is
   the largest possible [172]equidistant set for this distance.^[173][7]

Generalized orthoplex

   [[174]edit]

   Regular [175]complex polytopes can be defined in [176]complex
   [177]Hilbert space called generalized orthoplexes (or cross polytopes),
   β^p
   [n] = [2]{3}[2]{3}...[2]{4}[p], or [CDel_node_1.png] [CDel_3.png]
   [CDel_node.png] [CDel_3.png] .. [CDel_3.png] [CDel_node.png]

Related polytope families

   [[231]edit]

   Cross-polytopes can be combined with their dual cubes to form compound
   polytopes:
     * In two dimensions, we obtain the [232]octagrammic star figure
       {⁠8/2⁠},
     * In three dimensions we obtain the [233]compound of cube and
       octahedron,
     * In four dimensions we obtain the compound of tesseract and 16-cell.
