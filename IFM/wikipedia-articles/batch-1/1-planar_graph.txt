   From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
   [63][220px-3-crossing_Heawood_graph.svg.png] A 1-planar drawing of the
   [64]Heawood graph: six of the edges have a single crossing, and the
   remaining 15 edges are not crossed.

   In [65]topological graph theory, a 1-planar graph is a graph that can
   be [66]drawn in the [67]Euclidean plane in such a way that each edge
   has at most one crossing point, where it crosses a single additional
   edge. If a 1-planar graph, one of the most natural generalizations of
   [68]planar graphs, is drawn that way, the drawing is called a 1-plane
   graph or 1-planar embedding of the graph.

Coloring

   [[69]edit]

   1-planar graphs were first studied by [70]Ringel (1965), who showed
   that they can be [71]colored with at most seven colors.^[72][1] Later,
   the precise number of colors needed to color these graphs, in the worst
   case, was shown to be six.^[73][2] The example of the [74]complete
   graph K[6], which is 1-planar, shows that 1-planar graphs may sometimes
   require six colors. However, the proof that six colors are always
   enough is more complicated.
   [75][220px-Prism-vf-color.svg.png] Coloring the vertices and faces of
   the triangular prism graph requires six colors

   Ringel's motivation was in trying to solve a variation of [76]total
   coloring for [77]planar graphs, in which one simultaneously colors the
   vertices and faces of a planar graph in such a way that no two adjacent
   vertices have the same color, no two adjacent faces have the same
   color, and no vertex and face that are adjacent to each other have the
   same color. This can obviously be done using eight colors by applying
   the [78]four color theorem to the given graph and its [79]dual graph
   separately, using two disjoint sets of four colors. However, fewer
   colors may be obtained by forming an auxiliary graph that has a vertex
   for each vertex or face of the given planar graph, and in which two
   auxiliary graph vertices are adjacent whenever they correspond to
   adjacent features of the given planar graph. A [80]vertex coloring of
   the auxiliary graph corresponds to a vertex-face coloring of the
   original planar graph. This auxiliary graph is 1-planar, from which it
   follows that Ringel's vertex-face coloring problem may also be solved
   with six colors.^[81][2] The graph K[6] cannot be formed as an
   auxiliary graph in this way, but nevertheless the vertex-face coloring
   problem also sometimes requires six colors; for instance, if the planar
   graph to be colored is a [82]triangular prism, then its eleven vertices
   and faces require six colors, because no three of them may be given a
   single color.^[83][3]

Edge density

   [[84]edit]

   Every 1-planar graph with n vertices has at most 4n − 8 edges.^[85][4]
   More strongly, each 1-planar drawing has at most n − 2 [86]crossings;
   removing one edge from each crossing pair of edges leaves a planar
   graph, which can have at most 3n − 6 edges, from which the 4n − 8 bound
   on the number of edges in the original 1-planar graph immediately
   follows.^[87][5] However, unlike planar graphs (for which all maximal
   planar graphs on a given vertex set have the same number of edges as
   each other), there exist [88]maximal 1-planar graphs (graphs to which
   no additional edges can be added while preserving 1-planarity) that
   have significantly fewer than 4n − 8 edges.^[89][6] The bound of 4n − 8
   on the maximum possible number of edges in a 1-planar graph can be used
   to show that the [90]complete graph K[7] on seven vertices is not
   1-planar, because this graph has 21 edges and in this case
   4n − 8 = 20 < 21.^[91][7]

   A 1-planar graph is said to be an optimal 1-planar graph if it has
   exactly 4n − 8 edges, the maximum possible. In a 1-planar embedding of
   an optimal 1-planar graph, the uncrossed edges necessarily form a
   quadrangulation (a [92]polyhedral graph in which every face is a
   [93]quadrilateral). Every quadrangulation gives rise to an optimal
   1-planar graph in this way, by adding the two diagonals to each of its
   quadrilateral faces. It follows that every optimal 1-planar graph is
   [94]Eulerian (all of its vertices have even [95]degree), that the
   minimum degree in such a graph is six, and that every optimal 1-planar
   graph has at least eight vertices of degree exactly six. Additionally,
   every optimal 1-planar graph is [96]4-vertex-connected, and every
   4-vertex cut in such a graph is a separating cycle in the underlying
   quadrangulation.^[97][8]

   The graphs that have straight 1-planar drawings (that is, drawings in
   which each edge is represented by a line segment, and in which each
   line segment is crossed by at most one other edge) have a slightly
   tighter bound of 4n − 9 on the maximum number of edges, achieved by
   infinitely many graphs.^[98][9]

Complete multipartite graphs

   [[99]edit]
   [100][300px-1planar-K2222.svg.png] 1-planar drawing of the
   [101]cocktail party graph K[2,2,2,2]

   A complete classification of the 1-planar [102]complete graphs,
   [103]complete bipartite graphs, and more generally [104]complete
   multipartite graphs is known. Every complete bipartite graph of the
   form K[2,n] is 1-planar (even planar), as is every complete tripartite
   graph of the form K[1,1,n]. Other than these infinite sets of examples,
   the only complete multipartite 1-planar graphs are K[6], K[1,1,1,6],
   K[1,1,2,3], K[2,2,2,2], K[1,1,1,2,2], and their subgraphs. The minimal
   non-1-planar complete multipartite graphs are K[3,7], K[4,5], K[1,3,4],
   K[2,3,3], and K[1,1,1,1,3]. For instance, the complete bipartite graph
   K[3,6] is 1-planar because it is a subgraph of K[1,1,1,6], but K[3,7]
   is not 1-planar.^[105][7]

Computational complexity

   [[106]edit]

   It is [107]NP-complete to test whether a given graph is
   1-planar,^[108][10]^[109][11] and it remains NP-complete even for the
   graphs formed from [110]planar graphs by adding a single edge^[111][12]
   and for graphs of bounded [112]bandwidth.^[113][13] The problem is
   [114]fixed-parameter tractable when parameterized by [115]cyclomatic
   number or by [116]tree-depth, so it may be solved in [117]polynomial
   time when those parameters are bounded.^[118][13]

   In contrast to [119]Fáry's theorem for planar graphs, not every
   1-planar graph may be drawn 1-planarly with straight [120]line segments
   for its edges.^[121][14]^[122][15] However, testing whether a 1-planar
   drawing may be straightened in this way can be done in [123]polynomial
   time.^[124][16] Additionally, every [125]3-vertex-connected 1-planar
   graph has a 1-planar drawing in which at most one edge, on the outer
   face of the drawing, has a [126]bend in it. This drawing can be
   constructed in [127]linear time from a 1-planar embedding of the
   graph.^[128][17] The 1-planar graphs have bounded [129]book
   thickness,^[130][18] but some 1-planar graphs including K[2,2,2,2] have
   book thickness at least four.^[131][19]

   1-planar graphs have [132]bounded local treewidth, meaning that there
   is a (linear) function f such that the 1-planar graphs of diameter d
   have [133]treewidth at most f(d); the same property holds more
   generally for the graphs that can be embedded onto a surface of bounded
   genus with a bounded number of crossings per edge. They also have
   [134]separators, small sets of vertices the removal of which decomposes
   the graph into connected components whose size is a constant fraction
   of the size of the whole graph. Based on these properties, numerous
   algorithms for planar graphs, such as [135]Baker's technique for
   designing [136]approximation algorithms, can be extended to 1-planar
   graphs. For instance, this method leads to a [137]polynomial-time
   approximation scheme for the [138]maximum independent set of a 1-planar
   graph.^[139][20]

Generalizations and related concepts

   [[140]edit]

   The class of graphs analogous to [141]outerplanar graphs for
   1-planarity are called the outer-1-planar graphs. These are graphs that
   can be drawn in a disk, with the vertices on the boundary of the disk,
   and with at most one crossing per edge. These graphs can always be
   drawn (in an outer-1-planar way) with straight edges and [142]right
   angle crossings.^[143][21] By using [144]dynamic programming on the
   [145]SPQR tree of a given graph, it is possible to test whether it is
   outer-1-planar in [146]linear time.^[147][22]^[148][23] The
   triconnected components of the graph (nodes of the SPQR tree) can
   consist only of [149]cycle graphs, [150]bond graphs, and four-vertex
   [151]complete graphs, from which it also follows that outer-1-planar
   graphs are planar and have [152]treewidth at most three.

   The 1-planar graphs include the 4-[153]map graphs, graphs formed from
   the adjacencies of regions in the plane with at most four regions
   meeting in any point. Conversely, every optimal 1-planar graph is a
   4-map graph. However, 1-planar graphs that are not optimal 1-planar may
   not be map graphs.^[154][24]

   1-planar graphs have been generalized to k-planar graphs, graphs for
   which each edge is crossed at most k times (0-planar graphs are exactly
   the planar graphs). Ringel defined the local crossing number of G to be
   the least non-negative integer k such that G has a k-planar drawing.
   Because the local crossing number is the maximum [155]degree of the
   [156]intersection graph of the edges of an optimal drawing, and the
   [157]thickness (minimum number of planar graphs into which the edges
   can be partitioned) can be seen as the [158]chromatic number of an
   intersection graph of an appropriate drawing, it follows from
   [159]Brooks' theorem that the thickness is at most one plus the local
   crossing number.^[160][25] The k-planar graphs with n vertices have at
   most O(k^1/2n) edges,^[161][26] and treewidth O((kn)^1/2).^[162][27] A
   [163]shallow minor of a k-planar graph, with depth d, is itself a
   (2d + 1)k-planar graph, so the shallow minors of 1-planar graphs and of
   k-planar graphs are also [164]sparse graphs, implying that the 1-planar
   and k-planar graphs have [165]bounded expansion.^[166][28]

   Nonplanar graphs may also be parameterized by their [167]crossing
   number, the minimum number of pairs of edges that cross in any drawing
   of the graph. A graph with crossing number k is necessarily k-planar,
   but not necessarily vice versa. For instance, the [168]Heawood graph
   has crossing number 3, but it is not necessary for its three crossings
   to all occur on the same edge of the graph, so it is 1-planar, and can
   in fact be drawn in a way that simultaneously optimizes the total
   number of crossings and the crossings per edge.

   Another related concept for nonplanar graphs is [169]graph skewness,
   the minimal number of edges that must be removed to make a graph
   planar.
