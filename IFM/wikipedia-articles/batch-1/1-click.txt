   Online purchasing with only a single mouse click

   [58][220px-Amazon_1-Click_option.png] [59]Amazon.com offering the
   option to either add an item to the user's cart, or purchase it
   immediately using 1-Click

   1-Click, also called one-click or one-click buying, is the technique of
   allowing customers to make purchases with the payment information
   needed to complete the purchase having been entered by the user
   previously.^[60][1] More particularly, it allows an [61]online shopper
   using an [62]Internet marketplace to purchase an item without having to
   use [63]shopping cart software. Instead of manually inputting billing
   and shipping information for a purchase, a user can use one-click
   buying to use a predefined address and [64]credit card number to
   purchase one or more items. Since the expiration of Amazon's patent,
   there has been an advent of checkout experience platforms, such as
   ShopPay, Simpler, PeachPay, Zplit, and Bolt which offer similar
   one-click checkout flows.^[65][2]

Patent

   [[66]edit]

   The [67]United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) issued a
   patent^[68][3] for this technique to [69]Amazon.com in September 1999.
   Amazon.com also owns the "1-Click" trademark.^[70][4]

   On May 12, 2006, the USPTO ordered a [71]reexamination^[72][5] of the
   "One-Click" patent, based on a request filed by Peter Calveley.^[73][6]
   Calveley cited as [74]prior art an earlier e-commerce patent and the
   Digicash [75]electronic cash system.

   On October 9, 2007, the USPTO issued an [76]office action in the
   reexamination which confirmed the patentability of claims 6 to 10 of
   the patent.^[77][7] The [78]patent examiner, however, rejected claims 1
   to 5 and 11 to 26. In November 2007, Amazon responded by amending the
   broadest claims (1 and 11) to restrict them to a shopping cart model of
   commerce. They have also submitted several hundred references for the
   examiner to consider.^[79][8] In March 2010, the reexamined and amended
   patent was allowed.^[80][9]^[81][10]

   Amazon's U.S. patent expired on September 11, 2017.^[82][11]

   In Europe, a patent application^[83][12] on 1-Click ordering was filed
   with the [84]European Patent Office (EPO) but was rejected by the EPO
   in 2007 due to [85]obviousness; the decision was upheld in
   2011.^[86][13]

   A related gift-ordering patent was granted in 2003, but revoked in 2007
   following an [87]opposition.^[88][14]

   In Canada, the Federal Court of Canada held that the One click patent
   could not be rejected as a pure business method since it had a physical
   effect. The Court remanded the application to the Canadian patent
   office for a reexamination.^[89][15]

Licensing

   [[90]edit]

Apple Inc.

   [[91]edit]

   Amazon.com in 2000 licensed 1-Click ordering to Apple Computer (now
   [92]Apple Inc.) for use on its online store.^[93][16]^[94][17] Apple
   subsequently added 1-Click ordering to the [95]iTunes Store^[96][18]
   and [97]iPhoto.^[98][19] Apple paid $1 million to license the patent.

Barnes & Noble

   [[99]edit]

   Amazon filed a patent infringement lawsuit in October 1999 in response
   to [100]Barnes & Noble's offering a 1-Click ordering option called
   "Express Lane". After reviewing the evidence, a judge issued a
   [101]preliminary injunction ordering Barnes & Noble to stop offering
   Express Lane until the case was settled.^[102][20] Barnes & Noble had
   developed a way to [103]design around the patent by requiring shoppers
   to make a second click to confirm their purchase.^[104][21]^[105][22]
   The lawsuit was settled in 2002. The terms of the settlement, including
   whether or not Barnes & Noble took a license to the patent or paid any
   money to Amazon, were not disclosed.^[106][23]

   In response to the lawsuit, the [107]Free Software Foundation urged a
   boycott of Amazon.com.^[108][24] The boycott was lifted by [109]GNU in
   September 2002.^[110][25] ^[111][26]
