   Chemical compound

   The [187]chemical compound 1,2-dichloroethane, commonly known as
   ethylene dichloride (EDC), is a [188]chlorinated hydrocarbon. It is a
   colourless [189]liquid with a [190]chloroform-like [191]odour. The most
   common use of 1,2-dichloroethane is in the production of [192]vinyl
   chloride, which is used to make [193]polyvinyl chloride (PVC) pipes,
   furniture and automobile upholstery, wall coverings, housewares, and
   automobile parts.^[194][4] 1,2-Dichloroethane is also used generally as
   an intermediate for other [195]organic chemical compounds, and as a
   [196]solvent. It forms [197]azeotropes with many other [198]solvents,
   including water (at a boiling point of 70.5 °C or 158.9 °F or 343.6 K)
   and other [199]chlorocarbons.^[200][5]

History

   [[201]edit]

   In 1794, physician [202]Jan Rudolph Deiman, merchant [203]Adriaan Paets
   van Troostwijk, chemist Anthoni Lauwerenburg, and botanist Nicolaas
   Bondt, under the name of Society of Dutch Chemists ([204]Dutch:
   Gezelschap der Hollandsche Scheikundigen), were the first to produce
   1,2-dichloroethane from olefiant gas (oil-making gas, [205]ethylene)
   and [206]chlorine gas.^[207][6] Although the Gezelschap in practice did
   not do much in-depth scientific research, they and their publications
   were highly regarded. Part of that acknowledgement is that
   1,2-dichloroethane was called "Dutch oil" in old chemistry. This is
   also the origin of the archaic term "olefiant gas" (oil-making gas) for
   ethylene, for in this reaction it is ethylene that makes the Dutch oil.
   And "olefiant gas" is the etymological origin of the modern term
   "olefins", the family of hydrocarbons of which ethylene is the first
   member.

Production

   [[208]edit]

   Nearly 20 million tons of 1,2-dichloroethane are produced annually in
   the [209]United States, [210]Western Europe, and [211]Japan.^[212][7]
   Production is primarily achieved through the [213]iron(III)
   chloride-[214]catalysed reaction of ethylene and chlorine:

          H[2]C=CH[2] (g) + Cl[2] (g) → ClC[2]H[4]Cl (l) ([215]ΔH^⊖[r] =
          −218 kJ/mol)

   1,2-dichloroethane is also generated by the [216]copper(II)
   chloride-catalysed [217]oxychlorination of ethylene:

   H[2]C=CH[2] + 2 HCl + ⁠1/2⁠ O[2] → ClC[2]H[4]Cl + H[2]O

Uses

   [[218]edit]

Vinyl chloride production

   [[219]edit]

   Approximately 95% of the world's production of 1,2-dichloroethane is
   used in the production of [220]vinyl chloride [221]monomer (VCM) with
   [222]hydrogen chloride as a byproduct. VCM is the precursor to
   [223]polyvinyl chloride.

   ClC[2]H[4]Cl → H[2]C=CHCl + HCl

   The hydrogen chloride can be re-used in the production of more
   1,2-dichloroethane via the [224]oxychlorination route described
   above.^[225][8]

Other uses

   [[226]edit]

   1,2-Dichloroethane has been used as degreaser and paint remover but
   this use has phased out due to its [227]toxicity. As a useful 'building
   block' reagent, it is used as an intermediate in the production of
   diverse organic compounds such as [228]ethylenediamine and higher
   [229]ethyleneamines.^[230][9] In the laboratory it is occasionally used
   as a source of [231]chlorine, with elimination of ethene and chloride.

   Via several steps, 1,2-dichloroethane is a precursor to
   [232]1,1,1-trichloroethane. Historically, before leaded petrol was
   phased out, chloroethanes were used as an additive in petrol to prevent
   lead buildup in engines.^[233][10]

Safety

   [[234]edit]

   1,2-Dichloroethane is highly [235]flammable^[236][11] and releases
   [237]hydrochloric acid when combusted:

   ClC[2]H[4]Cl + ⁠5/2⁠ O[2] → 2 CO[2] + H[2]O + 2 HCl

   It is also [238]toxic (especially by inhalation due to its high
   [239]vapour pressure) and possibly [240]carcinogenic. Its high
   [241]solubility and 50-year [242]half-life in [243]anoxic [244]aquifers
   make it a perennial pollutant and health risk that is very expensive to
   treat conventionally, requiring a method of
   [245]bioremediation.^[246][12] While the chemical is not used in
   consumer products manufactured in the U.S., a case was reported in 2009
   of molded plastic consumer products (toys and holiday decorations) from
   [247]China that released 1,2-dichloroethane into homes at levels high
   enough to produce cancer risk.^[248][13]^[249][14]

   Substitutes are recommended and will vary according to application.
   [250]Dioxolane and [251]toluene are possible substitutes as solvents.
   [252]Dichloroethane is unstable in the presence of [253]aluminium and,
   when moist, with [254]zinc and [255]iron.^[[256]citation needed]
