   Chemical compound

   1,4-Dioxane ([192]/daɪˈɒkseɪn/) is a [193]heterocyclic [194]organic
   compound, classified as an [195]ether. It is a colorless liquid with a
   faint sweet [196]odor similar to that of [197]diethyl ether. The
   compound is often called simply dioxane because the other dioxane
   [198]isomers ([199]1,2- and [200]1,3-) are rarely encountered.

   Dioxane is used as a solvent for a variety of practical applications as
   well as in the laboratory, and also as a stabilizer for the transport
   of [201]chlorinated hydrocarbons in aluminium containers.^[202][3]

Synthesis

   [[203]edit]

   Dioxane is produced by the acid-catalysed [204]dehydration of
   [205]diethylene glycol, which in turn is obtained from the
   [206]hydrolysis of [207]ethylene oxide.

   In 1985, the global production capacity for dioxane was between 11,000
   and 14,000 tons.^[208][4] In 1990, the total U.S. production volume of
   dioxane was between 5,250 and 9,150 tons.^[209][5]

Structure

   [[210]edit]

   The dioxane molecule is centrosymmetric, meaning that it adopts a
   [211]chair conformation, typical of relatives of [212]cyclohexane.
   However, the molecule is conformationally flexible, and the [213]boat
   conformation is easily adopted, e.g. in the [214]chelation of metal
   cations. Dioxane resembles a smaller [215]crown ether with only two
   ethyleneoxyl units.

Uses

   [[216]edit]

Trichloroethane transport

   [[217]edit]

   In the 1980s, most of the dioxane produced was used as a stabilizer for
   [218]1,1,1-trichloroethane for storage and transport in [219]aluminium
   containers. Normally aluminium is protected by a passivating oxide
   layer, but when these layers are disturbed, the metallic aluminium
   reacts with trichloroethane to give [220]aluminium trichloride, which
   in turn catalyses the [221]dehydrohalogenation of the remaining
   trichloroethane to [222]vinylidene chloride and [223]hydrogen chloride.
   Dioxane "poisons" this catalysis reaction by forming an [224]adduct
   with aluminium trichloride.^[225][4]

As a solvent

   [[226]edit]

   [227][200px-Binary_phase_diagram_dioxane-water.svg.png] Binary
   [228]phase diagram for the system 1,4-dioxane/water

   Dioxane is used in a variety of applications as a versatile
   [229]aprotic solvent, e.g. for inks, adhesives, and cellulose esters.
   It is substituted for [230]tetrahydrofuran (THF) in some processes,
   because of its lower toxicity and higher boiling point (101 °C, versus
   66 °C for THF).^[231][6]

   While diethyl ether is rather insoluble in water, dioxane is
   [232]miscible and in fact is [233]hygroscopic. At standard pressure,
   the mixture of water and dioxane in the ratio 17.9:82.1 by mass is a
   positive [234]azeotrope that boils at 87.6 °C.^[235][7]

   The oxygen atoms are weakly [236]Lewis-basic. It forms adducts with a
   variety of Lewis acids. It is classified as a [237]hard base and its
   base parameters in the [238]ECW model are E[B] = 1.86 and C[B] = 1.29.

   Dioxane produces [239]coordination polymers by linking metal
   centers.^[240][8] In this way, it is used to drive the [241]Schlenk
   equilibrium, allowing the synthesis of dialkyl magnesium
   compounds.^[242][4] [243]Dimethylmagnesium is prepared in this
   manner:^[244][9]^[245][10]

          2 CH[3]MgBr + (C[2]H[4]O)[2] → MgBr[2](C[2]H[4]O)[2] +
          (CH[3])[2]Mg

Spectroscopy

   [[246]edit]

   Dioxane is used as an internal standard for [247]nuclear magnetic
   resonance spectroscopy in [248]deuterium oxide.^[249][11]

Toxicology

   [[250]edit]

Safety

   [[251]edit]

   Dioxane has an [252]LD[50] of 5170 mg/kg in rats.^[253][4] It is
   irritating to the eyes and respiratory tract. Exposure may cause damage
   to the central nervous system, liver and kidneys.^[254][12] In a 1978
   mortality study conducted on workers exposed to 1,4-dioxane, the
   observed number of deaths from cancer was not significantly different
   from the expected number.^[255][13] Dioxane is classified by the
   National Toxicology Program as "reasonably anticipated to be a human
   carcinogen".^[256][14] It is also classified by the [257]IARC as a
   [258]Group 2B carcinogen: possibly carcinogenic to humans because it is
   a known carcinogen in other animals.^[259][15] The [260]United States
   Environmental Protection Agency classifies dioxane as a probable human
   [261]carcinogen (having observed an increased incidence of cancer in
   controlled animal studies, but not in epidemiological studies of
   workers using the compound), and a known irritant (with a
   no-observed-adverse-effects level of 400 milligrams per cubic meter) at
   concentrations significantly higher than those found in commercial
   products.^[262][16] Animal studies in rats suggest that the greatest
   health risk is associated with inhalation of vapors in the pure
   form.^[263][17]^[264][18]^[265][19] The State of New York has adopted a
   first-in-the-nation drinking water standard for 1,4-Dioxane and set the
   maximum contaminant level of 1 part per billion.^[266][20]

Explosion hazard

   [[267]edit]

   Like some other ethers, dioxane combines with atmospheric oxygen upon
   prolonged exposure to air to form potentially explosive [268]peroxides.
   [269]Distillation of these mixtures is dangerous. Storage over metallic
   sodium could limit the risk of peroxide accumulation.

Environment

   [[270]edit]

   Dioxane tends to concentrate in the water and has little affinity for
   soil. It is resistant to abiotic degradation in the environment, and
   was formerly thought to also resist [271]biodegradation. However, more
   recent studies since the 2000s have found that it can be biodegraded
   through a number of pathways, suggesting that [272]bioremediation can
   be used to treat 1,4-dioxane contaminated water.^[273][21]^[274][22]

   Dioxane has affected groundwater supplies in several areas. Dioxane at
   the level of 1 μg/L (~1 ppb) has been detected in many locations in the
   US.^[275][5] In the U.S. state of New Hampshire, it had been found at
   67 sites in 2010, ranging in concentration from 2 ppb to over 11,000
   ppb. Thirty of these sites are solid waste landfills, most of which
   have been closed for years. In 2019, the Southern Environmental Law
   Center successfully sued Greensboro, North Carolina's Wastewater
   treatment after 1,4-Dioxane was found at 20 times above EPA safe levels
   in the Haw River.^[276][23]

Cosmetics

   [[277]edit]

   As a byproduct of the [278]ethoxylation process, a route to some
   ingredients found in cleansing and moisturizing products, dioxane can
   contaminate cosmetics and personal care products such as deodorants,
   perfumes, shampoos, toothpastes and mouthwashes.^[279][24]^[280][25]
   The ethoxylation process makes the cleansing agents, such as
   [281]sodium laureth sulfate and ammonium laureth sulfate, less abrasive
   and offers enhanced foaming characteristics. 1,4-Dioxane is found in
   small amounts in some cosmetics, a yet unregulated substance used in
   cosmetics in both China and the U.S.^[282][26] Research has found the
   chemical in ethoxylated raw ingredients and in off-the-shelf cosmetic
   products. The [283]Environmental Working Group (EWG) found that 97% of
   hair relaxers, 57% of baby soaps and 22 percent of all products in Skin
   Deep, their database for cosmetic products, are contaminated with
   1,4-dioxane.^[284][27]

   Since 1979 the [285]U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) have
   conducted tests on cosmetic raw materials and finished products for the
   levels of 1,4-dioxane.^[286][28] 1,4-Dioxane was present in ethoxylated
   raw ingredients at levels up to 1410 ppm (~0.14%wt), and at levels up
   to 279 ppm (~0.03%wt) in off the shelf cosmetic products.^[287][28]
   Levels of 1,4-dioxane exceeding 85 ppm (~0.01%wt) in children's
   shampoos indicate that close monitoring of raw materials and finished
   products is warranted.^[288][28] While the FDA encourages manufacturers
   to remove 1,4-dioxane, it is not required by federal law.^[289][29]

   On 9 December 2019, New York passed a bill to ban the sale of cosmetics
   with more than 10 ppm of 1,4-dioxane as of the end of 2022. The law
   will also prevent the sale of household cleaning and personal care
   products containing more than 2 ppm of 1,4-dioxane at the end of
   2022.^[290][30]
