   From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
   (Redirected from [58]1.96)
   Number useful in statistics for analyzing a normal curve

   [59][250px-NormalDist1.96.png] 95% of the area under the [60]normal
   distribution lies within 1.96 standard deviations away from the mean.

   In [61]probability and [62]statistics, the 97.5th percentile point of
   the standard [63]normal distribution is a number commonly used for
   statistical calculations. The approximate value of this number is 1.96,
   meaning that 95% of the area under a [64]normal curve lies within
   approximately 1.96 [65]standard deviations of the [66]mean. Because of
   the [67]central limit theorem, this number is used in the construction
   of approximate 95% [68]confidence intervals. Its ubiquity is due to the
   arbitrary but common convention of using confidence intervals with 95%
   probability in science and frequentist statistics, though other
   probabilities (90%, 99%, etc.) are sometimes
   used.^[69][1]^[70][2]^[71][3]^[72][4] This convention seems
   particularly common in medical statistics,^[73][5]^[74][6]^[75][7] but
   is also common in other areas of application, such as earth
   sciences,^[76][8] social sciences and business research.^[77][9]

   There is no single accepted name for this number; it is also commonly
   referred to as the "standard normal [78]deviate", "[79]normal score" or
   "[80]Z score" for the 97.5 percentile point, the .975 point, or just
   its approximate value, 1.96.

   testing 2×2 [84]contingency tables.

History

   [[85]edit]
   [86][200px-Youngronaldfisher2.JPG] Ronald Fisher

   The use of this number in applied statistics can be traced to the
   influence of [87]Ronald Fisher's classic textbook, [88]Statistical
   Methods for Research Workers, first published in 1925:

     "The value for which P = .05, or 1 in 20, is 1.96 or nearly 2; it is
     convenient to take this point as a limit in judging whether a
     deviation is to be considered significant or not."^[89][11]

   In Table 1 of the same work, he gave the more precise value
   1.959964.^[90][12] In 1970, the value truncated to 20 [91]decimal
   places was calculated to be

          1.95996 39845 40054 23552...^[92][13]^[93][14]

   The commonly used approximate value of 1.96 is therefore accurate to
   better than one part in 50,000, which is more than adequate for applied
   work.

   Some people even use the value of 2 in the place of 1.96, reporting a
   95.4% confidence interval as a 95% confidence interval. This is not
   recommended but is occasionally seen.^[94][15]

Software functions

   [[95]edit]

   The inverse of the standard normal [96]CDF can be used to compute the
   value. The following is a table of function calls that return 1.96 in
   some commonly used applications:
   Application Function call
   [97]Excel [98]NORM.S.INV(0.975)
   [99]MATLAB [100]norminv(0.975)
   [101]R [102]qnorm(0.975)
   [103]Python ([104]SciPy) [105]scipy.stats.norm.ppf(0.975)
   [106]SAS [107]probit(0.025);
   [108]SPSS x = COMPUTE [109]IDF.NORMAL(0.975,0,1).
   [110]Stata [111]invnormal(0.975)
   [112]Wolfram Language ([113]Mathematica)
   InverseCDF[NormalDistribution[0, 1], 0.975]^[114][16]^[115][17]
