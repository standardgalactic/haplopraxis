   Chemical compound

   1,4-Butanediol, also called Butane-1,4-diol (other names include 1,4-B,
   BD, BDO and 1,4-BD),^[152][5] is a [153]primary alcohol and an
   [154]organic compound with the [155]formula HOCH[2]CH[2]CH[2]CH[2]OH.
   It is a colorless [156]viscous [157]liquid first synthesized in 1890
   via acidic hydrolysis of N,N'-dinitro-1,4-butanediamine by Dutch
   chemist Pieter Johannes Dekkers, who called it "tetramethylene
   glycol".^[158][6]^[159][7]

Synthesis

   [[160]edit]

   [161][380px-ButaneToDiol.svg.png] Conversion of butane to butanediol or
   butyrolactone via oxidation to maleic anhydride followed by
   hydrogenation over [162]copper chromite.^[163][8]

   In one industrial [164]chemical synthesis, [165]acetylene reacts with
   two equivalents of [166]formaldehyde to form [167]butyne-1,4-diol.
   [168]Hydrogenation of butyne-1,4-diol gives butane-1,4-diol.^[169][9]
   It is also made on an industrial scale from [170]maleic anhydride in
   the Davy process, which is first converted to the methyl maleate ester,
   then hydrogenated. Other routes are from [171]butadiene, [172]allyl
   acetate and [173]succinic acid.

   A biological route to BD has been commercialized that uses a
   [174]genetically modified organism.^[175][10] The [176]biosynthesis
   proceeds via [177]4-hydroxybutyrate.

Industrial use

   [[178]edit]

   Butane-1,4-diol is used industrially as a [179]solvent^[[180]additional
   citation(s) needed] and in the manufacture of some types of
   [181]plastics, elastic [182]fibers and [183]polyurethanes. In
   [184]organic chemistry, 1,4-butanediol is used for the synthesis of
   [185]γ-butyrolactone (GBL). In the presence of [186]phosphoric acid and
   high temperature, it dehydrates to the important solvent
   [187]tetrahydrofuran.^[188][11] At about 200 °C in the presence of
   soluble [189]ruthenium [190]catalysts, the diol undergoes
   [191]dehydrogenation to form [192]butyrolactone.^[193][12] It is used
   to synthesize [194]1,4-Butanediol diglycidyl ether which is then used
   as a [195]reactive diluent for [196]epoxy resins.^[197][13]

   1,4-Butanediol is used in the production of [198]polybutylene
   terephthalate (PBT) plastic.^[199][14]

   World production of butane-1,4-diol was claimed to be about one million
   metric tons per year and market price is about [200]US$ 2,000 (€1,600)
   per ton (2005). In 2013, worldwide production was claimed to be
   billions of pounds (consistent with approximately one million metric
   tons).^[201][15]

   Almost half of it is dehydrated to [202]tetrahydrofuran to make fibers
   such as [203]Spandex.^[204][16] The largest producer is
   [205]BASF.^[206][17]

Use as a recreational drug

   [[207]edit]

   [208][265px-Dangerous_dietary_supplements.gif] FDA warning against
   products containing GHB and its prodrugs, such as butane-1,4-diol.

   Butane-1,4-diol is also used as a [209]recreational drug known by some
   users as "Bute",^[210][18] "One Comma Four", "Liquid Fantasy", "One
   Four Bee" or "One Four B-D-O".

   Some [211]federal courts in the USA have stated that 1,4-butanediol
   exerts effects similar to its metabolite, [212]GABA analogue
   [213]gamma-hydroxybutyrate (GHB), but several other federal courts have
   ruled that it does not.

   1,4-butenediol (CAS 110-64-5) may be incorrectly sold as 1,4-butanediol
   but should not be confused with it.

Pharmacokinetics

   [[214]edit]

   Butane-1,4-diol is rapidly converted into GHB acid by the enzymes
   [215]alcohol dehydrogenase and [216]aldehyde dehydrogenase, and
   differing levels of these enzymes may account for differences in
   effects and [217]side effects between users.^[218][19] While
   co-administration of [219]ethanol and GHB already poses serious risks,
   co-administration of [220]ethanol with 1,4-butanediol will interact
   considerably and has many other potential risks. This is because the
   same enzymes that are responsible for metabolizing alcohol also
   metabolize 1,4-butanediol so there is a strong chance of a dangerous
   [221]drug interaction.^[222][19]^[223][20] Emergency room patients who
   overdose on both [224]ethanol and 1,4-butanediol often present with
   symptoms of [225]alcohol intoxication initially and as the ethanol is
   metabolized the 1,4-butanediol is then able to better compete for the
   enzyme and a second period of intoxication ensues as the 1,4-butanediol
   is converted into GHB.^[226][19]
   [227][600px-GHB_metabolic_pathway.svg.png] Metabolic pathway of
   butane-1,4-diol, [228]γ-butyrolactone and γ-hydroxybutyric acid (GHB).

Pharmacodynamics

   [[229]edit]

   Butane-1,4-diol seems to have two types of pharmacological actions. The
   major psychoactive effects of 1,4-butanediol are because it is
   metabolized into GHB; however there is a study suggesting that
   1,4-butanediol may have potential alcohol-like pharmacological effects
   on its own.^[230][20] The study arrived at this conclusion based on the
   finding that butane-1,4-diol coadministered with ethanol led to
   potentiation of some of the behavioral effects of ethanol. However,
   potentiation of ethanol's effects may simply be caused by competition
   for the [231]alcohol dehydrogenase and [232]aldehyde dehydrogenase
   enzymes with co-administered 1,4-butanediol. The shared metabolic
   rate-limiting steps thus leads to slowed metabolism and clearance for
   both compounds including ethanol's known toxic metabolite
   [233]acetaldehyde.

   Another study found no effect following intracerebroventricular
   injection of butane-1,4-diol in rats.^[234][21] This contradicts the
   hypothesis of butane-1,4-diol having inherent alcohol-like
   pharmacological effects.

   Like gamma-hydroxybutyric acid, butane-1,4-diol is safe only in small
   amounts. Adverse effects in higher doses include nausea, vomiting,
   dizziness, sedation, vertigo, and potentially death if ingested in
   large amounts. Anxiolytic effects are diminished and side effects
   increased when used in combination with alcohol.

Legality

   [[235]edit]

   While butane-1,4-diol is not currently [236]scheduled federally in the
   United States,^[237][22] a number of states have classified
   1,4-butanediol as a controlled substance. Individuals have been
   prosecuted for possession of 1,4-butanediol under the [238]Federal
   Analog Act as substantially similar to GHB.^[239][23] A federal case in
   New York in 2002 ruled that 1,4-butanediol could not be considered an
   [240]analog of GHB under federal law,^[241][24] but that decision was
   later overturned by the Second Circuit.^[242][25] A jury in Federal
   District Court in Chicago found that 1,4-butanediol was not an analog
   of GHB under federal law, which was not disputed on the case's appeal
   to the Seventh Circuit Court of Appeals, however this finding did not
   affect the outcome of the case.^[243][26] In the [244]United Kingdom,
   1,4-butanediol was scheduled in December 2009 (along with another GHB
   precursor, [245]gamma-butyrolactone) as a [246]Class C controlled
   substance. In Germany, the drug is not explicitly illegal, but might
   also be treated as illegal if used as a drug. It is controlled as a
   Schedule VI [247]precursor in [248]Canada.

2007 contamination of Bindeez toy

   [[249]edit]

   See also: [250]Bindeez

   A toy called "[251]Bindeez" ("Aqua Dots" in North America) was recalled
   by the distributor in November 2007 because of the presence of
   butane-1,4-diol. The toy consists of small beads that stick to each
   other by sprinkling water. Butane-1,4-diol was detected by
   [252]GC-MS.^[253][27] The production plant seems to have intended to
   cut costs by replacing less toxic [254]pentane-1,5-diol with
   butane-1,4-diol. ChemNet China listed the price of butane-1,4-diol at
   between about US$1,350–2,800 per metric ton, while the price for
   1,5-pentanediol is about US$9,700 per metric ton.^[255][28]

2021 poisoning at Darmstadt Technical University

   [[256]edit]

   In August 2021, several people fell severely ill after consuming drinks
   at building L2.01 at the Lichtwiese Campus of [257]Darmstadt Technical
   University, Germany. Seven showed severe symptoms, two were transported
   to a hospital in [258]Frankfurt am Main, and a 30-year-old person was,
   for a time, in a critical state. Butane-1,4-diol had been detected in
   milk packages, as well as in water filters. At the location, detectives
   also found [259]bromophenols and [260]dicyclohexylamine.^[261][29]
