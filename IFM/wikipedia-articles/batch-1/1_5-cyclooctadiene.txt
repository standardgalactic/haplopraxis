   Chemical compound

   1,5-Cyclooctadiene (also known as cycloocta-1,5-diene) is a [132]cyclic
   [133]hydrocarbon with the chemical formula
   C[8]H[12], specifically [−(CH[2])[2]−CH=CH−][2].

   There are three [134]configurational isomers with this structure, that
   differ by the arrangement of the four C–C single bonds adjacent to the
   double bonds. Each pair of single bonds can be on the same side (cis,Z)
   or on opposite sides (trans,E) of the double bond's plane; the three
   possibilities are denoted cis,cis, trans,trans, and cis,trans; or
   (Z,Z), (E,E), and (Z,E). (Because of overall symmetry, trans,cis is the
   same configuration as cis,trans.)

   Generally abbreviated COD, the cis,cis isomer of this [135]diene is a
   useful precursor to other organic compounds and serves as a [136]ligand
   in [137]organometallic chemistry. It is a colorless liquid with a
   strong odor. ^[138][2]^[139][3] 1,5-Cyclooctadiene can be prepared by
   dimerization of [140]butadiene in the presence of a nickel catalyst, a
   coproduct being [141]vinylcyclohexene. Approximately 10,000 tons were
   produced in 2005.^[142][4]^[143][5]

Organic reactions

   [[144]edit]

   COD reacts with borane to give
   [145]9-borabicyclo[3.3.1]nonane,^[146][6] commonly known as 9-BBN, a
   [147]reagent in organic chemistry used in [148]hydroborations:

          [149][450px-Synthesis_of_9-BBN_dimer.png]

   COD adds
   SCl[2] (or similar reagents) to give
   2,6-dichloro-9-thiabicyclo[3.3.1]nonane:^[150][7]^[151][8]

          [152]2,6-Dichloro-9-thiabicyclo[3.3.1]nonane, synthesis and
          reactions

   The resulting dichloride can be further modified as the di[153]azide or
   di[154]cyano derivative in a [155]nucleophilic substitution aided by
   [156]anchimeric assistance.

   COD is used as an intermediate in one of the syntheses of
   [157]disparlure, a [158]gypsy moth [159]pheromone.^[160][9]

   1,5-COD binds to low-valent metals via both alkene groups. Metal-COD
   complexes are attractive because they are sufficiently stable to be
   isolated, often being more robust than related ethylene complexes. The
   stability of COD complexes is attributable to the [174]chelate effect.
   The COD ligands are easily displaced by other ligands, such as
   phosphines.

   Ni(COD)[2] is prepared by reduction of [175]anhydrous nickel
   [176]acetylacetonate in the presence of the ligand, using
   [177]triethylaluminium ^[178][10]

          1⁄3

   [Ni(C[5]H[7]O[2])[2]][3] + 2COD + 2Al(C[2]H[5])[3] → Ni(COD)[2] +
   2Al(C[2]H[5])[2](C[5]H[7]O[2]) + C[2]H[4] + C[2]H[6]

   The related
   Pt(COD)[2] is prepared by a more circuitous route involving the
   dilithium [179]cyclooctatetraene:^[180][11]

   Li[2]C[8]H[8] + PtCl[2](COD) + 3C[7]H[10] → [Pt(C[7]H[10])[3]] + 2LiCl
   + C[8]H[8] + C[8]H[12]
   Pt(C[7]H[10])[3] + 2COD → Pt(COD)[2] + 3C[7]H[10]

   Extensive work has been reported on complexes of COD, much of which has
   been described in volumes 25, 26, and 28 of [181]Inorganic Syntheses.
   The platinum complex is a precursor to a 16-electron complex of
   ethylene:

   Pt(COD)[2] + 3C[2]H[4] → Pt(C[2]H[4])[3] + 2COD

   COD complexes are useful as starting materials; one noteworthy example
   is the reaction:

   Ni(COD)[2] + 4CO → Ni(CO)[4] + 2COD

   The product
   Ni(CO)[4] is highly toxic, thus it is advantageous to generate it in
   the reaction vessel upon demand. Other low-valent metal complexes of
   COD include [182]cyclooctadiene rhodium chloride dimer,
   [183]cyclooctadiene iridium chloride dimer, and Fe(COD)(CO)[3], and
   [184]Crabtree's catalyst.

   The
   M(COD)[2] complexes with nickel, palladium, and platinum have
   [185]tetrahedral geometry, whereas [M(COD)[2]]^+ complexes of rhodium
   and iridium are [186]square planar.

(E,E)-COD

   [[187]edit]

          [188][600px-EE-COD_synthesis.svg.png] E,E-COD synthesis
          (Stöckmann et al. 2011)

   The highly strained [189]trans,trans isomer of 1,5-cyclooctadiene is a
   known compound. (E,E)-COD was first synthesized by [190]George M.
   Whitesides and [191]Arthur C. Cope in 1969 by [192]photoisomerization
   of the cis,cis compound.^[193][12] Another synthesis (double
   elimination reaction from a cyclooctane ring) was reported by [194]Rolf
   Huisgen in 1987.^[195][13] The [196]molecular conformation of (E,E)-COD
   is twisted rather than chair-like. The compound has been investigated
   as a [197]click chemistry mediator.^[198][14]
