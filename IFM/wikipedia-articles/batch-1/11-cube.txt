   From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
   (Redirected from [102]11-cube)
   Convex polytope, the n-dimensional analogue of a square and a cube
   For other uses, see [103]Hypercube (disambiguation).
   In the following [104]perspective projections, [105]cube is 3-cube and
   [106]tesseract is 4-cube.

   In [107]geometry, a hypercube is an [108]n-dimensional analogue of a
   [109]square ([110]n = 2) and a [111]cube ([112]n = 3); the special case
   for [113]n = 4 is known as a [114]tesseract. It is a [115]closed,
   [116]compact, [117]convex figure whose 1-[118]skeleton consists of
   groups of opposite [119]parallel [120]line segments aligned in each of
   the space's [121]dimensions, [122]perpendicular to each other and of
   the same length. A unit hypercube's longest diagonal in n dimensions is
   equal to
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
   displaystyle="true" scriptlevel="0"> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD">
   <msqrt> <mi>n</mi> </msqrt> </mrow> </mstyle> </mrow> <annotation
   encoding="application/x-tex">{\displaystyle {\sqrt {n}}}</annotation>
   </semantics> :MATH]
   {\displaystyle {\sqrt {n}}} .

   An n-dimensional hypercube is more commonly referred to as an n-cube or
   sometimes as an n-dimensional cube.^[123][1]^[124][2] The term measure
   polytope (originally from Elte, 1912)^[125][3] is also used, notably in
   the work of [126]H. S. M. Coxeter who also labels the hypercubes the γn
   polytopes.^[127][4]

   The hypercube is the special case of a [128]hyperrectangle (also called
   an n-orthotope).

   A unit hypercube is a hypercube whose side has length one [129]unit.
   Often, the hypercube whose corners (or vertices) are the 2^n points in
   R^n with each coordinate equal to 0 or 1 is called the unit hypercube.

Construction

   [[130]edit]

By the number of dimensions

   [[131]edit]
   [132][220px-From_Point_to_Tesseract_%28Looped_Version%29.gif] An
   animation showing how to create a tesseract from a point.

   A hypercube can be defined by increasing the numbers of dimensions of a
   shape:

          0 – A point is a hypercube of dimension zero.
          1 – If one moves this point one unit length, it will sweep out a
          line segment, which is a unit hypercube of dimension one.
          2 – If one moves this line segment its length in a
          [133]perpendicular direction from itself; it sweeps out a
          2-dimensional square.
          3 – If one moves the square one unit length in the direction
          perpendicular to the plane it lies on, it will generate a
          3-dimensional cube.
          4 – If one moves the cube one unit length into the fourth
          dimension, it generates a 4-dimensional unit hypercube (a unit
          [134]tesseract).

   This can be generalized to any number of dimensions. This process of
   sweeping out volumes can be formalized mathematically as a
   [135]Minkowski sum: the d-dimensional hypercube is the Minkowski sum of
   d mutually perpendicular unit-length line segments, and is therefore an
   example of a [136]zonotope.

   The 1-[137]skeleton of a hypercube is a [138]hypercube graph.

Related families of polytopes

   [[221]edit]

   The hypercubes are one of the few families of [222]regular polytopes
   that are represented in any number of dimensions.^[223][8]

   The hypercube (offset) family is one of three [224]regular polytope
   families, labeled by [225]Coxeter as γ[n]. The other two are the
   hypercube dual family, the [226]cross-polytopes, labeled as β[n,] and
   the [227]simplices, labeled as α[n]. A fourth family, the [228]infinite
   tessellations of hypercubes, is labeled as δ[n].

   Another related family of semiregular and [229]uniform polytopes is the
   [230]demihypercubes, which are constructed from hypercubes with
   alternate vertices deleted and [231]simplex facets added in the gaps,
   labeled as hγ[n].

   n-cubes can be combined with their duals (the [232]cross-polytopes) to
   form compound polytopes:
     * In two dimensions, we obtain the [233]octagrammic star figure
       {8/2},
     * In three dimensions we obtain the [234]compound of cube and
       octahedron,
     * In four dimensions we obtain the compound of tesseract and 16-cell.

Relation to (n−1)-simplices

   [[235]edit]

   The graph of the n-hypercube's edges is [236]isomorphic to the
   [237]Hasse diagram of the (n−1)-[238]simplex's [239]face lattice. This
   can be seen by orienting the n-hypercube so that two opposite vertices
   lie vertically, corresponding to the (n−1)-simplex itself and the null
   polytope, respectively. Each vertex connected to the top vertex then
   uniquely maps to one of the (n−1)-simplex's facets (n−2 faces), and
   each vertex connected to those vertices maps to one of the simplex's
   n−3 faces, and so forth, and the vertices connected to the bottom
   vertex map to the simplex's vertices.

   This relation may be used to generate the face lattice of an
   (n−1)-simplex efficiently, since face lattice enumeration algorithms
   applicable to general polytopes are more computationally expensive.

Generalized hypercubes

   [[240]edit]

   Regular [241]complex polytopes can be defined in [242]complex
   [243]Hilbert space called generalized hypercubes, γ^p
   [n] = [p]{4}[2]{3}...[2]{3}[2], or [CDel_pnode_1.png] [CDel_4.png]
   [CDel_node.png] [CDel_3.png] .. [CDel_3.png] [CDel_node.png]
   [CDel_3.png] [CDel_node.png] . Real solutions exist with p = 2, i.e.
   γ^2
   [n] = γ[n] = [2]{4}[2]{3}...[2]{3}[2] = {4,3,..,3}. For p > 2, they
   exist in
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle
   displaystyle="true" scriptlevel="0"> <msup> <mrow
   class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mi mathvariant="double-struck">C</mi> </mrow>
   <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mi>n</mi> </mrow> </msup> </mstyle>
   </mrow> <annotation encoding="application/x-tex">{\displaystyle \mathbb
   {C} ^{n}}</annotation> </semantics> :MATH]
   {\displaystyle \mathbb {C} ^{n}} . The facets are generalized
   (n−1)-cube and the [244]vertex figure are regular [245]simplexes.

   The [246]regular polygon perimeter seen in these orthogonal projections
   is called a [247]Petrie polygon. The generalized squares (n = 2) are
   shown with edges outlined as red and blue alternating color p-edges,
   while the higher n-cubes are drawn with black outlined p-edges.

   The number of m-face elements in a p-generalized n-cube are:
   [MATH: <semantics> <mrow class="MJX-TeXAtom-ORD"> <mstyle

Relation to exponentiation

   [[290]edit]

   Any positive integer raised to another positive integer power will
   yield a third integer, with this third integer being a specific type of
   [291]figurate number corresponding to an n-cube with a number of
   dimensions corresponding to the exponential. For example, the exponent
   2 will yield a [292]square number or "perfect square", which can be
   arranged into a square shape with a side length corresponding to that
   of the base. Similarly, the exponent 3 will yield [293]a perfect cube,
   an integer which can be arranged into a cube shape with a side length
   of the base. As a result, the act of raising a number to 2 or 3 is more
   commonly referred to as "[294]squaring" and "cubing", respectively.
   However, the names of higher-order hypercubes do not appear to be in
   common use for higher powers.
