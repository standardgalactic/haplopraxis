   Chemical compound

   1,4-Dichlorobenzene (1,4-DCB, p-DCB, or para-dichlorobenzene, sometimes
   abbreviated as PDCB or para) is an [163]aryl chloride and isomer of
   [164]dichlorobenzene with the [165]formula C[6]H[4]Cl[2]. This
   colorless solid has a strong [166]odor. The molecule consists of a
   [167]benzene ring with two [168]chlorine atoms (replacing hydrogen
   atoms) on opposing sites of the ring.

   It is used as a [169]disinfectant, [170]pesticide, and [171]deodorant,
   most familiarly in [172]mothballs in which it is a replacement for the
   more traditional [173]naphthalene because of naphthalene's greater
   [174]flammability (though both chemicals have the same [175]NFPA 704
   rating). It is also used as a [176]precursor in the production of the
   chemically and thermally resistant polymer [177]poly(p-phenylene
   sulfide).^[178][3]

Production

   [[179]edit]

   p-DCB is produced by [180]chlorination of benzene using [181]ferric
   chloride as a catalyst:

          C[6]H[6] + 2 Cl[2] → C[6]H[4]Cl[2] + 2 [182]HCl

   The chief impurity is the 1,2 [183]isomer. The compound can be purified
   by [184]fractional crystallization, taking advantage of its relatively
   high [185]melting point of 53.5 °C; the isomeric [186]dichlorobenzenes
   and [187]chlorobenzene melt well below room temperature.^[188][3]

Uses

   [[189]edit]

Disinfectant, deodorant, and pesticide

   [[190]edit]

   [191][220px-1%2C4_dichlorobenzene_balls.jpg] 1,4-dichlorobenzene balls
   sold as [192]urinal disinfectant

   p-DCB is used to control [193]moths, [194]molds, and
   [195]mildew.^[196][4] It also finds use as a disinfectant^[197][3] in
   waste containers and restrooms and is the characteristic smell
   associated with [198]urinal cakes. Its usefulness for these
   applications arises from p-DCB's low [199]solubility in water and its
   relatively high [200]volatility: it [201]sublimes readily near room
   temperature.^[202][3]

Precursor to other chemicals

   [[203]edit]

   [204]Nitration gives [205]1,4-dichloronitrobenzene, a precursor to
   commercial [206]dyes and pigments.^[207][5] The chloride sites on p-DCB
   can be [208]substituted with hydroxylamine and sulfide groups. In a
   growing application, p-DCB is the precursor to the high performance
   polymer poly(p-phenylene sulfide):^[209][6]

          [210]Synthesis of polyphenylensulfide

Environmental and health effects

   [[211]edit]

   p-DCB is poorly soluble in water and is not easily broken down by
   [212]soil organisms. Like many [213]hydrocarbons, p-DCB is
   [214]lipophilic and will accumulate in fatty tissues if consumed by a
   person or animal.

   The United States [215]Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS)
   and the [216]International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) have
   determined that p-DCB may reasonably be anticipated to be a
   [217]carcinogen.^[218][7] This has been indicated by animal studies,
   although a full-scale human study has not been done.^[219][8]

   The [220]United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has set a
   target maximum [221]contaminant level of 75 [222]micrograms of p-DCB
   per liter of [223]drinking water (75 μg/L),^[224][9] but publishes no
   information on the cancer risk.^[225][10] p-DCB is also an
   [226]EPA-registered [227]pesticide.^[228][11] The United States
   [229]Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) has set a
   maximum level of 75 parts of p-DCB per million parts air in the
   workplace (75 ppm) for an 8-hour day, 40-hour
   [230]workweek.^[231][12]^[232][13]

   A mechanism for the carcinogenic effects of mothballs and some types of
   air fresheners containing p-DCB has been identified in
   roundworms.^[233][14]

   Due to its carcinogenic nature, use of paradichlorobenzene in the
   European Union is forbidden as an air freshener (since 2005) and in
   mothballs (since 2008).

Biodegradation

   [[234]edit]

   [235]Rhodococcus phenolicus is a [236]bacterium species able to degrade
   dichlorobenzene as its sole [237]carbon source.^[238][15]
