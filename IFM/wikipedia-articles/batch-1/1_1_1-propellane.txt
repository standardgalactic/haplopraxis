   Chemical compound

   [1.1.1]Propellane is an [85]organic compound, the simplest member of
   the [86]propellane family. It is a [87]hydrocarbon with formula
   C[5]H[6] or C[2](CH[2])[3]. The molecular structure consists of three
   rings of three [88]carbon atoms each, sharing one [89]C–C bond.

   [1.1.1]Propellane is a [90]highly strained molecule. The bonds of the
   two central carbon atoms have an [91]inverted tetrahedral geometry, and
   the [92]length of the central bond is 160 pm. The [93]strength of that
   bond is disputed; estimates vary from 59–65 [94]kcal/[95]mol to no
   strength at all. The energy of the [96]biradical state (with no central
   bond at all) is calculated to be 80 kcal/mol higher. At 114 °C it will
   spontaneously [97]isomerize to [98]3-methylidenecyclobutene (5 below)
   with a [99]half-life of 5 minutes. Its [100]strain energy is estimated
   to be 102 kcal/mol (427 [101]kJ/mol). Surprisingly, [1.1.1]propellane
   is persistent at room temperature and is somewhat less susceptible to
   thermal decomposition than the less strained (90 kcal/mol)
   [2.2.2]propellane system, which has an estimated half-life of only
   about 1 h at 25 °C.^[102][1] This unusual stability is attributed to
   delocalisation of electron density from the bond between the central
   carbon atoms onto the bridging carbon atoms.^[103][2]

   The type of bonding in this molecule has been explained in terms of
   [104]charge-shift bonding.^[105][3]

Synthesis

   [[106]edit]

   [1.1.1]Propellane was first reported by [107]Kenneth B. Wiberg and F.
   Walker in 1982. The synthesis commences with [108]cyclopropanation of
   [109]1,1-bis(chloromethyl)ethylene,^[110][4] according to the following
   scheme:

          [111][400px-111propellaneSynthesis.png] Scheme 1. Synthesis of
          [1.1.1]propellane

   Synthesis begins with conversion of the 1,3-di-[112]carboxylic acid of
   [113]bicyclo[1.1.1]pentane 1 in a [114]Hunsdiecker reaction to the
   corresponding di[115]bromide 2 followed by a [116]coupling reaction
   with [117]n-butyllithium. The final product 3 was isolated by
   [118]column chromatography at −30 °C.

   However, a much simplified synthesis was published by
   Szeimies.^[119][5] It starts with [120]dibromocarbene addition to the
   [121]alkene bond of 3-chloro-2-(chloromethyl)propene 6 followed by
   [122]deprotonation by [123]methyllithium and [124]nucleophilic
   displacements in 7.^[125][6] The product was not isolated but kept in
   solution at −196 °C.

Reactions

   [[126]edit]

Acetic acid addition

   [[127]edit]

   [1.1.1]Propellane spontaneously reacts with [128]acetic acid to yield a
   [129]methylidenecyclobutane [130]ester (4 above).

Polymerization

   [[131]edit]

   [1.1.1]Propellane undergoes a [132]polymerization reaction where the
   central C–C bond is split and connected to adjacent [133]monomer units,
   resulting in [134]staffanes.^[135][7]

          [136][400px-Staffanes.png] Scheme 2. Synthesis of [n]staffane

   A [137]radical polymerization initiated by [138]methyl formate and
   [139]benzoyl peroxide results in a distribution of [140]oligomers. An
   [141]anionic addition polymerization with [142]n-butyllithium results
   in a fully polymerized product. [143]X-ray diffraction of the polymer
   shows that the connecting C–C bonds have [144]bond lengths of only
   1.48 Å, significantly shorter than the normal 1.54 Å.

   The compound [145]1,3-dehydroadamantane, which can be viewed as a
   bridged [1.3.3]propellane, also polymerizes in a similar way.
