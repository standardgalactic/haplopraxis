   100% renewable energy is the goal of the use [133]renewable resources
   for all energy. 100% [134]renewable energy for electricity, heating,
   cooling and transport is motivated by [135]climate change, pollution
   and other environmental issues, as well as economic and [136]energy
   security concerns. Shifting the total global [137]primary energy supply
   to renewable sources requires [138]a transition of the energy system,
   since most of today's energy is derived from non-renewable [139]fossil
   fuels.

   Research into this topic is fairly new, with few studies published
   before 2009, but has gained increasing attention in recent years. The
   majority of studies show that a global transition to 100% renewable
   energy across all sectors – power, heat, transport and industry – is
   feasible and economically
   viable.^[140][5]^[141][6]^[142][7]^[143][8]^[[144]need quotation to
   verify] A cross-sectoral, holistic approach is seen as an important
   feature of 100% renewable energy systems and is based on the assumption
   "that the best solutions can be found only if one focuses on the
   synergies between the sectors" of the energy system such as
   electricity, heat, transport or industry.^[145][9]

   The main barriers to the widespread implementation of large-scale
   renewable energy and low-carbon energy strategies are seen to be
   primarily social and political rather than technological or
   economic.^[146][10] The key roadblocks are: [147]climate change denial,
   the [148]fossil fuels lobby, political inaction, [149]unsustainable
   energy consumption, outdated [150]energy infrastructure, and financial
   constraints.^[151][11]

Feasibility

   [[152]edit]

   No uniform definition for 100% renewable energy systems has been
   adopted across the published literature.^[153][9]

   Recent studies show that a global [154]transition to 100% renewable
   energy across all sectors – power, heat, transport and desalination
   well before 2050 is feasible.^[155][5]^[156][6]^[157][7]^[158][8]
   According to a review of the 181 [159]peer-reviewed papers on 100%
   renewable energy that were published until 2018, "[t]he great majority
   of all publications highlights the technical feasibility and economic
   viability of 100% RE systems."^[160][9] A review of 97 papers published
   since 2004 and focusing on islands concluded that across the studies
   100% renewable energy was found to be "technically feasible and
   economically viable."^[161][12] A 2022 [162]review found that the main
   conclusion of most of the literature in the field is that 100%
   renewables is feasible worldwide at low cost.^[163][13]

   Existing technologies, including storage, are capable of generating a
   secure energy supply at every hour throughout the year. The sustainable
   energy system is more efficient and cost effective than the existing
   system.^[164][14] The [165]United Nations [166]Intergovernmental Panel
   on Climate Change (IPCC) stated in their [167]2011 report that there is
   little that limits integrating [168]renewable technologies for
   satisfying the total global energy demand.

   [169]Mark Z. Jacobson, professor of civil and environmental engineering
   at [170]Stanford University and director of its Atmosphere and Energy
   program, says that producing all new energy with [171]wind power,
   [172]solar power, and [173]hydropower by 2030 is feasible, and that
   existing energy supply arrangements could be replaced by
   2050.^[174][15] Barriers to implementing the renewable energy plan are
   seen to be "primarily social and political, not technological or
   economic".^[175][16] Jacobson says that [176]energy costs today with a
   wind, solar, and water system should be similar to today's energy costs
   from other optimally cost-effective strategies.^[177][17] The main
   obstacle against this scenario is the lack of political will.^[178][18]
   His conclusions have been disputed by other researchers.^[179][19]
   Jacobson published a response that disputed the piece point by
   point^[180][20] and claimed that the authors were motivated by
   allegiance to energy technologies that the 2015 paper
   excluded.^[181][19]

   Jacobson says that [182]energy costs today with a wind, solar, and
   water system should be similar to today's energy costs from other
   optimally cost-effective strategies and he has rebutted their
   criticisms.^[183][21]^[184][22]^[185][20] A followup paper was
   published by Jacobson and others in 2022, in which paths to 100%
   renewable energy by 2035 and 2050 were developed for 145
   countries.^[186][23] The study concluded that a wind-water-solar (WWS)
   based system "requires less energy, costs less, and creates more jobs
   than business as usual". The cost reduction was primarily due to the
   substantial (-56.4%) decrease in overall energy demand thanks to the
   increased efficiency of relying on renewable electricity for all energy
   needs.

   In 2014, renewable sources such as [187]wind, [188]geothermal,
   [189]solar, [190]biomass, and burnt waste provided 19% of the total
   energy consumed worldwide, with roughly half of that coming from
   traditional use of biomass.^[191][24] The largest sector in terms of
   energy consumption is electricity with a renewable share of 22.8%, most
   of it coming from hydropower with a share of 16.6%, followed by wind
   with 3.1%.^[192][24] As of 2018^[193][update], according to [194]REN21,
   transformation is picking up speed in the power sector, but urgent
   action is required in heating, cooling and transport.^[195][25]

   There are many places around the world with grids that are run almost
   exclusively on renewable energy (
   see [196]below). At the national level, at least 30 nations already
   have renewable energy contributing more than 20% of the energy
   supply.^[197][26] [198]Renewable energy use has grown more quickly than
   even advocates anticipated.^[199][27] As of 2019^[200][update],
   however, it needs to grow six times faster to limit global warming to
   2 °C (3.6 °F).^[201][28]

Energy transition

   [[202]edit]

   100% renewable energy is an energy system where all energy use is
   sourced from [203]renewable energy sources. The endeavor to use 100%
   renewable energy for electricity, heating/cooling and transport is
   motivated by [204]global warming, pollution and other environmental
   issues, as well as economic and [205]energy security concerns. Shifting
   the total global [206]primary energy supply to renewable sources
   requires a transition of the energy system, since most of today's
   energy is derived from non-renewable [207]fossil fuels.

   According to the [208]Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change there
   are few fundamental technological limits to integrating a portfolio of
   [209]renewable energy technologies to meet most of total global energy
   demand. [210]Renewable energy use has grown more quickly than even
   advocates anticipated.^[211][29] As of 2019^[212][update], however, it
   needs to grow six times faster to limit global warming to 2 °C
   (3.6 °F).^[213][30]

   100% renewable energy in a country is typically a more challenging goal
   than [214]carbon neutrality. The latter is a [215]climate mitigation
   target, politically decided by many countries, and may also be achieved
   by balancing the total [216]carbon footprint of the country (not only
   emissions from energy and fuel) with [217]carbon dioxide removal and
   [218]carbon projects abroad.

   As of 2018^[219][update] according to [220]REN21 transformation is
   picking up speed in the power sector, but urgent action is required in
   heating, cooling and transport.^[221][31] There are many places around
   the world with grids that are run almost exclusively on renewable
   energy. At the national level, at least 30 nations already have
   renewable energy contributing more than 20% of the energy
   supply.^[[222]citation needed]

   According to a review of the 181 [223]peer-reviewed papers on 100%
   renewable energy published until 2018, "[t]he great majority of all
   publications highlights the technical feasibility and economic
   viability of 100% RE systems." While there are still many publications
   that focus on electricity only, there is a growing number of papers
   that cover different energy sectors and [224]sector-coupled, integrated
   energy systems. This cross-sectoral, holistic approach is seen as an
   important feature of 100% renewable energy systems and is based on the
   assumption "that the best solutions can be found only if one focuses on
   the synergies between the sectors" of the energy system such as
   electricity, heat, transport or industry.^[225][32]

   [226]Stephen W. Pacala and [227]Robert H. Socolow of Princeton
   University have developed a series of "[228]climate stabilization
   wedges" that can allow us to maintain our quality of life while
   avoiding catastrophic [229]climate change, and "renewable energy
   sources", in aggregate, constitute the largest number of their
   "wedges".^[230][33]

   Similarly, in the United States, the independent [231]National Research
   Council has noted that "sufficient domestic renewable resources exist
   to allow renewable electricity to play a significant role in future
   [232]electricity generation and thus help confront issues related to
   climate change, [233]energy security, and the escalation of energy
   costs ... Renewable energy is an attractive option because renewable
   resources available in the United States, taken collectively, can
   supply significantly greater amounts of electricity than the total
   current or projected domestic demand."^[234][34]

   The main barriers to the widespread implementation of large-scale
   renewable energy and low-carbon energy strategies are political rather
   than technological. According to the 2013 Post Carbon Pathways report,
   which reviewed many international studies, the key roadblocks are:
   [235]climate change denial, the [236]fossil fuels lobby, political
   inaction, [237]unsustainable energy consumption, outdated [238]energy
   infrastructure, and financial constraints.^[239][35]

   Studies have shown that Southeast Asia countries could achieve almost
   100% renewable elecitricity based on solar, wind, and off-river pumped
   hydro energy storage at a competitive LCOE of around
   US$55–115/MWh.^[240][36]

History

   [[241]edit]

   Using 100% renewable energy was first suggested in a paper in
   [242]Science^[243][37] published in 1975 by Danish physicist [244]Bent
   Sørensen, which was followed by several other proposals.^[245][38] In
   1976, [246]energy policy analyst [247]Amory Lovins coined the term
   "[248]soft energy path" to describe an alternative future where
   [249]energy efficiency and appropriate [250]renewable energy sources
   steadily replace a centralized energy system based on fossil and
   nuclear fuels.^[251][39]
   [252][220px-Timeline_of_selected_key_milestones_of_100%25_renewable_ene
   rgy_systems_research.gif] Timeline of selected key milestones of 100%
   renewable energy systems research^[253][13]

   In 1998, the first detailed analysis of scenarios with high shares of
   renewables were published. These were followed by the first detailed
   100% scenarios. In 2006, a PhD thesis was published by Czisch in which
   it was shown that in a 100% renewable scenario energy supply could
   match demand in every hour of the year in Europe and North Africa. In
   the same year, Danish Energy professor [254]Henrik Lund published a
   first paper^[255][40] in which he addresses the optimal combination of
   renewables, which was followed by several other papers on the
   [256]transition to 100% renewable energy in Denmark. Since then, Lund
   has been publishing several papers on 100% renewable energy. After
   2009, publications began to rise steeply, covering 100% scenarios for
   countries in Europe, America, Australia and other parts of the
   world.^[257][38]
   [258][220px-Development_of_peer-reviewed_journal_articles_based_on_100%
   25_RE_system_analyses_for_concrete_geographic_entities.gif] Development
   of peer-reviewed journal articles based on 100% RE system analyses for
   concrete geographic entities^[259][13]

   Even in the early 21st century, it was extraordinary for scientists and
   decision-makers to consider the concept of 100% renewable electricity.
   However, renewable energy progress has been so rapid that things have
   totally changed since then:^[260][41]

     [261]Solar photovoltaic modules have dropped about 75 percent in
     price. Current scientific and technological advances in the
     laboratory suggest that they may become less expensive than the cost
     of installation of a photovoltaic system on residential or
     commercial buildings. On-shore [262]wind power is spreading over all
     continents and is economically competitive with fossil and nuclear
     power in several regions. [263]Concentrated solar thermal power
     (CST) with [264]thermal storage has moved from the demonstration
     stage of maturity to the limited commercial stage and still has the
     potential for further cost reductions of about 50 percent.^[265][41]

   Renewable energy use has grown much faster than even advocates had
   anticipated.^[266][27] [267]Wind turbines generate 39^[268][42] percent
   of Danish electricity, and Denmark has many [269]biogas digesters and
   waste-to-energy plants as well. Together, wind and biomass provide 44%
   of the electricity consumed by the country's six million inhabitants.
   In 2010, Portugal's 10 million people produced more than half their
   electricity from indigenous renewable energy resources. Spain's 40
   million inhabitants meet one-third of their electrical needs from
   renewables.^[270][27]

   Renewable energy has a history of strong public support. In America,
   for example, a 2013 [271]Gallup survey showed that two in three
   Americans want the U.S. to increase domestic energy production using
   solar power (76%), wind power (71%), and natural gas (65%). Far fewer
   want more petroleum production (46%) and more nuclear power (37%).
   Least favored is coal, with about one in three Americans favouring
   it.^[272][43]

   [273]REN21 says renewable energy already plays a significant role and
   there are many policy targets that aim to increase this:

     At the national level, at least 30 nations around the world already
     have renewable energy contributing more than 20% of energy supply.
     National renewable energy markets are projected to continue to grow
     strongly in the coming decade and beyond, and some 120 countries
     have various policy targets for longer-term shares of renewable
     energy, including a binding 20% by 2020 target for the European
     Union. Some countries have much higher long-term policy targets of
     up to 100% renewables. Outside Europe, a diverse group of 20 or more
     other countries target renewable energy shares in the 2020–2030 time
     frame that range from 10% to 50%.^[274][44]

   Supporters of 100% renewable energy do not consider [275]nuclear power
   as renewable or [276]sustainable due to [277]perceived risks of
   disasters and [278]high-level waste management, and consider
   [279]carbon capture and storage to have limited safe storage
   potential.^[280][38] These constraints have also led to an interest in
   100% renewable energy. A well established body of academic literature
   has been written over the past decade^[[281]when?], evaluating
   scenarios for 100% renewable energy for various geographical areas. In
   recent years^[[282]when?], more detailed analyses have emerged from
   government and industry sources.^[283][45] The incentive to use 100%
   renewable energy is created by [284]global warming and ecological as
   well as economic concerns, post [285]peak oil.
   [286][350px-Renewable_energy.png] Share of electricity production from
   renewables, 2023^[287][46]

   The first country to propose 100% renewable energy was [288]Iceland, in
   1998.^[289][47] Proposals have been made for Japan in 2003,^[290][48]
   and for Australia in 2011.^[291][49] [292]Albania, Iceland, and
   Paraguay obtain essentially all of their electricity from renewable
   sources (Albania and Paraguay 100% from [293]hydroelectricity, Iceland
   72% hydro and 28% geothermal).^[294][50] Norway obtains nearly all of
   its electricity from renewable sources (97 percent from
   hydropower).^[295][51] Iceland proposed using hydrogen for
   transportation and its fishing fleet. Australia proposed biofuel for
   those elements of transportation not easily converted to electricity.
   The road map for the United States,^[296][52]^[297][53] commitment by
   Denmark,^[298][54] and Vision 2050 for Europe set a 2050 timeline for
   converting to 100% renewable energy,^[299][55] later reduced to 2040 in
   2011.^[300][56] Zero Carbon Britain 2030 proposes eliminating carbon
   emissions in Britain by 2030 by transitioning to renewable
   energy.^[301][57] In 2015, Hawaii enacted a law that the Renewable
   Portfolio Standard shall be 100 percent by 2045. This is often confused
   with renewable energy. If electricity produced on the grid is 65 GWh
   from fossil fuel and 35 GWh from renewable energy and rooftop off grid
   solar produces 80 GWh of renewable energy, then the total renewable
   energy is 115 GWh and the total electricity on the grid is 100 GWh.
   Then the RPS is 115 percent.^[302][58]

   Cities like [303]Paris and [304]Strasbourg in France, planned to use
   100% renewable energy by 2050.^[305][59]^[306][60]

   Similarly, in the United States, the independent [307]National Research
   Council has noted that "sufficient domestic renewable resources exist
   to allow renewable electricity to play a significant role in future
   electricity generation and thus help confront issues related to climate
   change, [308]energy security, and the escalation of energy costs ...
   Renewable energy is an attractive option because renewable resources
   available in the United States, taken collectively, can supply
   significantly greater amounts of electricity than the total current or
   projected domestic demand."^[309][61]

   It is estimated that the world will spend an extra $8 trillion over the
   next 25 years to prolong the use of non-renewable resources, a cost
   that would be eliminated by transitioning instead to 100% renewable
   energy.^[310][62] Research that has been published in [311]Energy
   Policy suggests that converting the entire world to 100% renewable
   energy by 2050 is both possible and affordable, but requires political
   support.^[312][63]^[313][64] It would require building many more
   [314]wind turbines and solar power systems but wouldn't utilize
   [315]bioenergy. Other changes involve use of [316]electric cars and the
   development of enhanced transmission grids and
   storage.^[317][65]^[318][66] As part of the [319]Paris Agreement,
   countries periodically update their climate change targets for the
   future, by 2018 no [320]G20 country had committed to a 100% renewable
   target.^[321][67]

   Until 2018, there were 181 peer-reviewed papers on 100% renewable
   energy. In the same year, 100% renewable energy was also mentioned in
   the [322]Special Report on Global Warming of 1.5 °C as a potential
   means to "expand the range of 1.5 °C pathways", if the findings can be
   corroborated.^[323][9]

   World total primary energy consumption by type in 2020^[324][68]
     Oil (31.2%)
     Coal (27.2%)
     Natural Gas (24.7%)
     Hydro ([325]renewables) (6.9%)
     Nuclear (4.3%)

     Others ([326]renewables) (5.7%)

   As of 2021, wind and solar were consistently increasing their share
   worldwide, but still represented just 5% of global primary energy
   consumption, albeit far more of [327]useful energy consumption. A
   report by [328]J.P. Morgan Asset Management (the biggest lender to
   fossil fuels in the world) analyzed renewable energy forecasts made by
   eight scientists and research bodies (including [329]Bent Sorensen,
   [330]Mark Z. Jacobson, [331]Amory Lovins) between 1970 and 2020 and
   claimed that all of them were unrealistically optimistic as they
   ignored "energy density, intermittency and the complex realities of
   incumbent energy systems".^[332][69]^[333][70]

   another example is [485]renewable heat such as in the case of Denmark,

   buildings to provide multiple kilowatts of heat per kilowatt of
   electricity.

100% clean electricity

   [[486]edit]

   See also: [487]Renewable portfolio standard

   Other electricity generating sources are considered clean, though not
   necessarily renewable, as they also do not emit carbon dioxide or other
   greenhouse gases and air pollutants. The largest of these is nuclear
   energy, which produces no emissions. Some argue that transitioning to
   100% renewable energy would be too slow to [488]limit climate change,
   and that closing down [489]nuclear power stations is a
   mistake.^[490][119]^[491][120] [492]Carbon capture and storage projects
   may still use coal or natural gas but capture carbon dioxide for
   storage or alternative uses. Pathways to eliminate greenhouse gases may
   include these in addition to renewable energy to save money,^[493][121]
   or to avoid shutting down existing plants and allow for flexibility in
   designing a carbon-free electric grid.

   In 2018, California passed SB 100, which mandates 100% clean,
   carbon-free by 2045, including a 60% renewable electricity goal by
   2030.^[494][122]^[495][123] 2019 legislation in [496]Washington also
   requires 100% clean electricity by 2045, eliminating coal by
   2025.^[497][124] Further states and territories to require 100%
   carbon-free electricity are Hawaii, Maine, Minnesota, Nevada, New
   Mexico, New York, Virginia, Puerto Rico, and Washington, DC.^[498][125]
   According to a study by Global Energy Monitor, China is expected to
   generate 1,200 gigawatts of renewable energy (wind and solar) by
   2025.^[499][126]

Obstacles

   [[500]edit]

   Further information: [501]Politics of global warming

   According to [502]Mark Z. Jacobson, the most significant barriers to
   the widespread implementation of large-scale renewable energy and low
   carbon energy strategies, at the pace required to prevent [503]runaway
   climate change, are primarily political and not
   technological.^[504][10]^[[505]dubious – [506]discuss] According to the
   2013 Post Carbon Pathways report, which reviewed many international
   studies, the key roadblocks are:^[507][11]

     * [508]Climate change denial
     * [509]Efforts to impede renewable energy by the fossil fuel industry
     * [510]Political paralysis
     * Unsustainable consumption of energy and resources
     * Path dependencies and outdated infrastructure
     * Financial and governance constraints

   In 2011, the [511]Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, some of
   the world's leading climate researchers selected by the United Nations,
   said "as infrastructure and energy systems develop, in spite of the
   complexities, there are few, if any, fundamental technological limits
   to integrating a portfolio of renewable energy technologies to meet a
   majority share of total energy demand in locations where suitable
   renewable resources exist or can be supplied".^[512][127] IPCC
   scenarios "generally indicate that growth in renewable energy will be
   widespread around the world".^[513][128] The IPCC said that if
   governments were supportive, and the full complement of renewable
   energy technologies were deployed, renewable energy supply could
   account for almost 80% of the world's energy use within forty
   years.^[514][129] [515]Rajendra Pachauri, chairman of the IPCC, said
   the necessary investment in renewables would cost only about 1% of
   global GDP annually. This approach could contain greenhouse gas levels
   to less than 450 parts per million, the safe level beyond which climate
   change becomes catastrophic and irreversible.^[516][129]

   [517]Stephen W. Pacala and [518]Robert H. Socolow have developed a
   series of "[519]climate stabilization wedges" that can allow societies
   to maintain their quality of life while avoiding catastrophic
   [520]climate change, and "renewable energy sources", in aggregate,
   constitute the largest number of their "wedges".^[521][130]

Lack of urgency and coordination

   [[522]edit]

   See also: [523]Climate change mitigation § Enaction of a state of
   emergency, and [524]Policy

   [525]Lester R. Brown founder and president of the [526]Earth Policy
   Institute, a nonprofit research organization based in Washington, D.C.,
   says a rapid transition to 100% renewable energy is both possible and
   necessary. Brown compares with the U.S. entry into World War II and the
   subsequent rapid mobilization and transformation of the US industry and
   economy. A quick transition to 100% renewable energy and saving of our
   civilization is proposed by Brown to follow an approach with similar
   urgency.^[527][131]

Required minerals

   [[528]edit]

   See also: [529]Environmental aspects of the electric car

   According to [530]World Bank the "below 2°C" climate scenario requires
   3 billions of tonnes of metals and minerals by 2050. Supply of mined
   resources such as zinc, molybdenum, silver, nickel, copper must
   increase by up to 500%.^[531][132] A 2018 study analysed the metal
   requirements to transition the global energy system up to 2060.
   Currently used battery technologies and known reserves are not
   compatible with the transition scenario as a result of insufficient
   [532]cobalt and [533]lithium reserves. Batteries containing less or no
   cobalt are feasible. Lithium is much more difficult to replace with
   maintained performance and cost.^[534][133]

Institutional inertia

   [[535]edit]

   A review suggests large institutions are prone to resisting "the
   challenge of 100% RE scenarios based on the dogma that the world cannot
   do without fossil fuels and nuclear energy". Institutions that have
   received extensive criticism include the [536]International Energy
   Agency and the [537]Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, with the
   latter also being criticized for not including studies on 100% RE
   systems in their IPCC reports.^[538][13]

Manufacturing concentration in China

   [[539]edit]

   See also: [540]Renewable energy in China

   A report found that China is about to produce "almost 95% of the
   world's polysilicon and the ingots and wafers" of the solar panel
   supply chain, with this level of concentration in any global supply
   chain "would represent a considerable vulnerability".^[541][134]

Intermittency

   [[542]edit]

   One of the main obstacles to 100% renewable energy is the
   [543]intermittency or variability of renewable energy sources – such as
   times when sufficient amounts of energy can be generated neither via
   wind nor via solar power ("[544]Dunkelflauten").

   Proposed notable options to manage this intermittency by the time the
   first transitional period to 100% renewable energy is completed
   include:
     * certain forms of (flexible) [545]dispatchable generation^[546][135]
       such as biomass (including forms of [547]pellet fuel,
       [548]woodchips, [549]algae fuel/[550]bioreactors, and [551]biomass
       grown on land [552]formerly used for meat-production) or
       hydroelectricity
     * diversification of (nonsynchronous)
       renewables^[553][136]^[554][135]
     * [555]super grids^[556][135]^[557][137] (due to foreign unique
       capacities/resources for generation and storage) and strengthening
       interconnections^[558][13] and larger grids in general (due to
       differences in weather or daytime)
     * curtailing excess generation^[559][135] and [560]power-to-X (e.g.
       producing green hydrogen immediately when there is abundant
       energy)^[561][13]
     * [562]Geothermal energy to reduce energy storage needs^[563][138] or
       potentially as dispatchable energy via in-reservoir
       storage^[564][139]^[565][140]
     * oversizing solar and wind capacities^[566][13]
     * flexible energy demand- and supply-regulating [567]smart grids
          + [568]Demand response technologies^[569][135]^[570][13]
          + [571]Vehicle-to-grid uses a vehicle's battery to supply the
            grid when needed^[572][141]^[573][142]^[574][13]
          + Smart scheduling^[575][143]^[576][144]
          + Monitoring and/or controlling energy use that is noncritical
            during periods of peak power consumption, and returning their
            function during nonpeak hours, such as for residential devices
            (like washing machines)^[577][145]
          + Optimizing the interaction between electricity, heat,
            transport, and industry^[578][13]
     * technologies and options for [579]energy
       storage^[580][146]^[581][147]^[582][148]^[583][135] For example:
          + Batteries^[584][149]
          + [585]Thermal energy storage^[586][150]^[587][151]^[588][152]
          + Ammonia^[589][153]
          + [590]Green hydrogen^[591][154]^[592][13]
          +
       See also: [593]List of energy storage projects

   In 2013, Smil analyzed proposals to depend on wind and solar-generated
   electricity including the proposals of Jacobson and colleagues, and
   writing in an issue of Spectrum prepared by the [594]Institute of
   Electrical and Electronics Engineers, he identified numerous points of
   concern, such as cost, intermittent power supply, growing
   [595]NIMBYism, and a lack of infrastructure as negative factors and
   said that "History and a consideration of the technical requirements
   show that the problem is much greater than these advocates have
   supposed."^[596][155]^[597][156] Smil and Hansen are concerned about
   the [598]variable output of solar and wind power. According to
   [599]Amory Lovins the [600]electricity grid alone can compensate for
   variability, just as it routinely backs up nonworking coal-fired and
   nuclear plants with working ones.^[601][157]

   In November 2014 the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change came out
   with their [602]fifth report, saying that in the absence of any one
   technology (such as bioenergy, [603]carbon dioxide capture and storage,
   nuclear, wind and solar), climate change mitigation costs can increase
   substantially depending on which technology is absent. For example, it
   may cost 40% more to reduce carbon emissions without carbon dioxide
   capture. (Table 3.2)^[604][158] According to a 2018 study, "in the
   absence of firm low-carbon [605][dispatchable] resources, the cost of
   decarbonizing power generation rises rapidly as the emissions limit
   approaches zero" and a renewable-only generation (with batteries)
   results in energy prices 42-163% higher in regions with lower VRE
   availability, and 11-105% higher in regions with higher VRE
   availability. The study introduced the term "firm low-carbon energy
   source" (e.g. [606]nuclear, [607]geothermal), which is intended to
   operate along "fast-burst" sources (e.g. batteries) and "fuel saving"
   (VRE).^[608][159]

   The [609]International Energy Agency says that there has been too much
   attention on issue of the variability of renewable electricity
   production.^[610][160] The issue of [611]intermittent supply applies to
   popular renewable technologies, mainly [612]wind power and [613]solar
   photovoltaics, and its significance depends on a range of factors that
   include the market penetration of the renewables concerned, the balance
   of plant and the wider connectivity of the system, as well as the
   demand side flexibility. Variability is rarely a barrier to increased
   renewable energy deployment when [614]dispatchable generation such as
   hydroelectricity or solar thermal storage is also available. But at
   high levels of market penetration it requires careful analysis and
   management, and additional costs may be required for back-up or system
   modification.^[615][160] Renewable electricity supply in the 20-50+%
   penetration range has already been implemented in several European
   systems, albeit in the context of an integrated European grid
   system:^[616][161]

Seasonal energy storage

   [[617]edit]

   Hydropower is currently the only large scale low-carbon seasonal energy
   storage. In countries with high variation in energy demand by season
   (for example the UK uses far more gas for heating in the winter than it
   uses electricity) but lacking hydropower [618]electrical
   interconnectors to countries with lots of hydropower (e.g. UK -
   Norway), electricity from hydropower is likely to be insufficient and
   development of a [619]hydrogen economy would likely be needed: this is
   being trialled in the UK and 8 TWh of inter-seasonal hydrogen energy
   storage has been proposed.^[620][162]

   In Australia, as well as storing renewable energy as hydrogen, it is
   also proposed to be exported in the form of [621]ammonia.^[622][163]

     * Various questions for design particulars of intermittency

Recent developments

   [[655]edit]

   See also: [656]Renewable energy by country

   [657]The Fourth Revolution: Energy is a German [658]documentary film
   released in 2010. It shows the vision of a global society, which lives
   in a world where the energy is produced 100% with renewable energies,
   showing a complete reconstruction of the economy, to reach this goal.
   In 2011, [659]Hermann Scheer wrote the book The Energy Imperative: 100
   Percent Renewable Now, published by Routledge.

   [660]Reinventing Fire is a book by Amory Lovins released in October
   2011. Lovins claims that combining reduced energy use with [661]energy
   efficiency gains would result in a $5 trillion saving and a
   faster-growing economy. This can all be done with the profitable
   commercialization of existing energy-saving technologies, through
   market forces, led by business.^[662][176] Former US president
   [663]Bill Clinton says the book is a "wise, detailed and comprehensive
   blueprint".^[664][177] The first paragraph of the preface says:

     Imagine fuel without fear. No climate change. No oil spills, dead
     coal miners, dirty air, devastated lands, lost wildlife. No
     [665]energy poverty. No oil-fed wars, tyrannies, or terrorists.
     Nothing to run out. Nothing to cut off. Nothing to worry about. Just
     energy abundance, benign and affordable, for all, for
     ever.^[666][178]

   The [667]Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change has said that there
   are few fundamental technological limits to integrating a portfolio of
   renewable energy technologies to meet most of total global energy
   demand. In a 2011 review of 164 recent scenarios of future renewable
   energy growth, the report noted that the majority expected renewable
   sources to supply more than 17% of total energy by 2030, and 27% by
   2050; the highest forecast projected 43% supplied by renewables by 2030
   and 77% by 2050.^[668][127]

   In 2011, the [669]International Energy Agency has said that [670]solar
   energy technologies, in its many forms, can make considerable
   contributions to solving some of the most urgent problems the world now
   faces:^[671][179]

     The development of affordable, inexhaustible and clean solar energy
     technologies will have huge longer-term benefits. It will increase
     countries' energy security through reliance on an indigenous,
     inexhaustible and mostly import-independent resource, enhance
     sustainability, reduce pollution, lower the costs of mitigating
     climate change, and keep fossil fuel prices lower than otherwise.
     These advantages are global. Hence the additional costs of the
     incentives for early deployment should be considered learning
     investments; they must be wisely spent and need to be widely
     shared.^[672][179]

   In 2011, the refereed journal [673]Energy Policy published two articles
   by [674]Mark Z. Jacobson, a professor of engineering at [675]Stanford
   University, and research scientist Mark A. Delucchi, about changing our
   energy supply mix and "Providing all global energy with wind, water,
   and solar power". The articles analyze the feasibility of providing
   worldwide energy for electric power, transportation, and
   heating/cooling from wind, water, and sunlight (WWS), which are safe
   clean options. In Part I, Jacobson and Delucchi discuss WWS energy
   system characteristics, aspects of energy demand, WWS resource
   availability, WWS devices needed, and material requirements.^[676][180]
   They estimate that 3,800,000 5 MW [677]wind turbines, 5350 100 MW
   [678]geothermal power plants, and 270 new 1300 MW [679]hydroelectric
   power plants would be required. In terms of [680]solar power, an
   additional 49,000 300 MW [681]concentrating solar plants, 40,000 300 MW
   [682]solar photovoltaic power plants, and 1.7 billion 3 kW rooftop
   photovoltaic systems would also be needed. Such an extensive WWS
   infrastructure could decrease world power demand by 30%.^[683][180] In
   Part II, Jacobson and Delucchi address variability of supply, system
   economics, and energy policy initiatives associated with a WWS system.
   The authors advocate producing all new energy with WWS by 2030 and
   replacing existing energy supply arrangements by 2050. Barriers to
   implementing the renewable energy plan are seen to be "primarily social
   and political, not technological or economic". Energy costs with a WWS
   system should be similar to today's energy costs.^[684][21]

   In general, Jacobson has said wind, water and solar technologies can
   provide 100 percent of the world's energy, eliminating all [685]fossil
   fuels.^[686][181] He advocates a "smart mix" of renewable energy
   sources to reliably meet electricity demand:

     Because the wind blows during stormy conditions when the sun does
     not shine and the sun often shines on calm days with little wind,
     combining wind and solar can go a long way toward meeting demand,
     especially when geothermal provides a steady base and hydroelectric
     can be called on to fill in the gaps.^[687][182]

   A 2012 study by the [688]University of Delaware for a 72 GW system
   considered 28 billion combinations of renewable energy and storage and
   found the most cost-effective, for the [689]PJM Interconnection, would
   use 17 GW of solar, 68 GW of [690]offshore wind, and 115 GW of onshore
   wind, although at times as much as three times the demand would be
   provided. 0.1% of the time would require generation from other
   sources.^[691][183]

   In March 2012, Denmark's parliament agreed on a comprehensive new set
   promotional programs for energy efficiency and renewable energy aimed
   at reaching 100 percent of electricity, heat and fuels from renewables
   by 2050.^[692][184] IRENEC is an annual conference on 100% renewable
   energy started in 2011 by [693]Eurosolar Turkey. The 2013 conference
   was in Istanbul.^[694][185]^[695][186]

   More recently, Jacobson and his colleagues have developed detailed
   proposals for switching to 100% renewable energy produced by wind,
   water and sunlight, for New York,^[696][187] California^[697][188] and
   Washington^[698][189] states, by 2050. As of 2014^[699][update], a more
   expansive new plan for the 50 states has been drawn up, which includes
   an online interactive map showing the renewable resource potential of
   each of the 50 states. The 50-state plan is part of [700]The Solutions
   Project, an independent outreach effort led by Jacobson, actor
   [701]Mark Ruffalo, and film director [702]Josh Fox.^[703][190]

   As of 2014^[704][update], many detailed assessments show that the
   energy service needs of a world enjoying radically higher levels of
   wellbeing, can be economically met entirely through the diverse
   currently available technological and organizational innovations around
   wind, solar, biomass, biofuel, hydro, ocean and geothermal energy.
   Debate over detailed plans remain, but transformations in global energy
   services based entirely around renewable energy are in principle
   technically practicable, economically feasible, socially viable, and so
   realisable. This prospect underpins the ambitious commitment by
   Germany, one of the world's most successful industrial economies, to
   undertake a major [705]energy transition, [706]Energiewende.^[707][191]

   In 2015 a study was published in [708]Energy and Environmental Science
   that describes a pathway to 100% renewable energy in the United States
   by 2050 without using biomass. Implementation of this roadmap is
   regarded as both environmentally and economically feasible and
   reasonable, as by 2050 it would save about $600 Billion Dollars
   [709]health costs a year due to reduced [710]air pollution and $3.3
   Trillion global warming costs. This would translate in yearly cost
   savings per head of around $8300 compared to a business as usual
   pathway. According to that study, barriers that could hamper
   implementation are neither technical nor economic but social and
   political, as most people didn't know that benefits from such a
   transformation far exceeded the costs.^[711][192]

   In June 2017, twenty-one researchers published an article in the
   [712]Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United
   States of America rejecting Jacobson's earlier PNAS article, accusing
   him of modeling errors and of using invalid modeling
   tools.^[713][193]^[714][194] They further asserted he made implausible
   assumptions through his reliance upon increasing national [715]energy
   storage from 43 minutes to 7 weeks, increasing [716]hydrogen production
   by 100,000%, and increasing [717]hydropower by the equivalent of 600
   [718]Hoover Dams.^[719][193] Article authors [720]David G. Victor
   called Jacobson's work "dangerous" and [721]Ken Caldeira emphasized
   that increasing hydropower output by 1,300 gigawatts, a 25% increase,
   is the equivalent flow of 100 [722]Mississippi Rivers.^[723][193]
   Jacobson published a response in the same issue of the PNAS and also
   authored a blog post where he asserted the researchers were advocates
   of the fossil fuel industry.^[724][193]^[725][195]^[726][196] Another
   study published in 2017 confirmed the earlier results for a 100%
   renewable power system for North America, without changes in hydropower
   assumptions, but with more realistic emphasis on a balanced storage
   portfolio, in particular seasonal storage, and for competitive
   economics.^[727][197]

Grid integration simulation

   [[728]edit]

   In 2015, Jacobson and Delucchi, together with Mary Cameron and Bethany
   Frew, examined with computer simulation (Loadmatch), in more detail how
   a wind-water-solar (WWS) system can track the energy demand from minute
   to minute. This turned out to be possible in the United States for 6
   years, including WWS variability by extreme weather events.^[729][198]
   In 2017, the plan was further developed for 139 countries by a team of
   27 researchers^[730][199] and in 2018, Jacobson and Delucchi with Mary
   Cameron and Brian Mathiesen published the Loadmatch results for 20
   regions in which the 139 countries in the world are divided. According
   to this research, a WWS system can follow the demand in all
   regions.^[731][200]

   The program Loadmatch receives as input estimated series, per half
   minute during 2050–2055, of
     * the energy demand
     * the intermittent wind and solar energy supply predicted with a 3D
       global climate / weather model GATOR-GCMOM^[732][201]
     * the hydropower, geothermal, tidal and wave energy

   and specifications of
     * the capacities and maximum loading / unloading speeds of the
       different types of storage
     * losses due to storage, transport, distribution and maintenance
     * a demand-supply management system ([733]smart grid).

   The program has been carried out for each region 10-20 times with
   adapted input for the storage capacities, until a solution was found in
   which the energy demand was followed, per half minute for 5 years, with
   low costs.

   The WWS system is assumed to connect in the electric network
     * geographically dispersed variable energy sources, [734]concentrated
       solar power (CSP) and hydro power
     * storage facilities: [735]pumped hydro, as heat in CSP plants, in
       batteries, as hydrogen by electrolysis of water, or as
       [736]compressed air underground.

   In 2020, Jacobson clarified in a textbook^[738][203] computer
   simulation results of a WWS energy system. To match demand with supply
   every minute more solar and wind farms and high-voltage lines must be
   installed than to match year-averaged demand and supply. Oversizing
   (also in a conventional energy system) ensures that the demand can be
   followed during peak hours, but causes unused supply during off-peak
   hours. In a WWS system, more energy exchange between areas leads to
   more transmission loss. The table shows WWS supply, unused supply,
   losses and end-use, in GW average power to reliably supply the world
   and four major regions with energy by 2050. See textbook Table 8.10;
   energy in TWh is divided by 26.3 kh (1000 hours) to get power in GW.
   The bottom row is the storage capacity of pumped hydro plants (Table
   8.7).
