   Chemical compound

   1,1,1,2-Tetrafluoroethane (also known as norflurane ([146]INN), R-134a,
   Klea 134a, Freon 134a, Forane 134a, Genetron 134a, Green Gas, Florasol
   134a, Suva 134a, HFA-134a, or HFC-134a) is a [147]hydrofluorocarbon
   (HFC) and [148]haloalkane [149]refrigerant with thermodynamic
   properties similar to [150]R-12 (dichlorodifluoromethane) but with
   insignificant [151]ozone depletion potential and a lower 100-year
   [152]global warming potential (1,430, compared to R-12's GWP of
   10,900).^[153][1] It has the formula CF[3]CH[2]F and a [154]boiling
   point of −26.3 °C (−15.34 °F) at atmospheric pressure. R-134a cylinders
   are colored [155]light blue.^[156][2] A phaseout and transition to
   [157]HFO-1234yf and other refrigerants, with GWPs similar to CO[2],
   began in 2012 within the automotive market.^[158][3]

Uses

   [[159]edit]

   1,1,1,2-Tetrafluoroethane is a [160]non-flammable gas used primarily as
   a "high-temperature" refrigerant for domestic [161]refrigeration and
   [162]automobile air conditioners. These devices began using
   1,1,1,2-tetrafluoroethane in the early 1990s as a replacement for the
   more environmentally harmful [163]R-12. Retrofit kits are available to
   convert units that were originally R-12-equipped.
   [164][200px-Tetrafluoroethane_liquid.jpg] Boiling of Tetrafluoroethane
   liquid when exposed to normal atmospheric pressure and temperature.

   Other common uses include plastic foam blowing, as a cleaning solvent,
   a propellant for the delivery of pharmaceuticals (e.g., inhaler
   canisters such as for [165]bronchodilators), wine cork removers,
   [166]gas dusters ("canned air"), and in air driers for removing the
   moisture from [167]compressed air. 1,1,1,2-Tetrafluoroethane has also
   been used to cool computers in some [168]overclocking attempts. It is
   the refrigerant used in plumbing pipe freeze kits. It is also commonly
   used as a propellant for [169]airsoft airguns. The gas is often mixed
   with a silicone-based lubricant.

Aspirational and niche applications

   [[170]edit]

   1,1,1,2-Tetrafluoroethane is also being considered as an [171]organic
   solvent, both as a liquid and a [172]supercritical
   fluid.^[173][4]^[174][5] ^[175][6]

   It is used in the [176]resistive plate chamber [177]particle detectors
   in the [178]Large Hadron Collider.^[179][7]^[180][8] It is also used
   for other types of particle detectors, e.g. some [181]cryogenic
   particle detectors.^[182][9] It can be used as an alternative to
   [183]sulfur hexafluoride in [184]magnesium [185]smelting as a
   [186]shielding gas.^[187][10]

History and environmental impacts

   [[188]edit]

   1,1,1,2-Tetrafluoroethane was introduced in the early 1990s as a
   replacement for [189]dichlorodifluoromethane (R-12), which has massive
   ozone depleting properties.^[190][11] Even though
   1,1,1,2-Tetrafluoroethane has insignificant ozone depletion potential
   ([191]ozone layer) and negligible acidification potential ([192]acid
   rain), it has a 100-year [193]global warming potential (GWP) of 1430
   and an approximate [194]atmospheric lifetime of 14 years.^[195][1] Its
   concentration in the atmosphere and contribution to [196]radiative
   forcing have been growing since its introduction. Thus it was included
   in the [197]IPCC list of greenhouse gases.^[198][12]
   [199][260px-HFC-134a_mm.png] HFC-134a measured by the Advanced Global
   Atmospheric Gases Experiment ([200]AGAGE) in the lower atmosphere
   ([201]troposphere) at stations around the world. Abundances are given
   as pollution free monthly mean mole fractions in
   [202]parts-per-trillion.[203][200px-Mauna_Loa_HFC-134a_%28CH2FCF3%29_co
   ncentration.png] HFC-134a atmospheric concentration since year 1995.

   R-134a began being phased out from use in the [204]European Union,
   starting in the mid 2010s, by a directive of 2006, recommending the
   replacement of gases in air conditioning systems with a GWP above
   100.^[205][13]

   1,1,1,2-tetrafluoroethane is subject to use restrictions in the US and
   other countries as well. The Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE) has
   proposed that it be best replaced by a new fluorochemical refrigerant
   [206]HFO-1234yf (CF[3]CF=CH[2]) in automobile air-conditioning
   systems.^[207][14] As of model year 2021, newly manufactured light-duty
   vehicles in the United States no longer use R-134a.^[208][3]

   California may also prohibit the sale of canned R-134a to individuals
   to avoid non-professional recharge of air conditioners.^[209][15] A ban
   had been in place in Wisconsin since October 1994 under ATCP 136
   prohibiting sales of container sizes holding less than 15 lbs of
   1,1,1,2-tetrafluoroethane, but this restriction applied only when the
   chemical was intended to be a refrigerant. However, the ban was lifted
   in Wisconsin in 2012.^[210][16] During the time that it was active,
   this Wisconsin-specific ban contained loopholes. For example, it was
   legal for a person to purchase [211]gas duster containers with any
   amount of the chemical because in that instance the chemical is neither
   intended to be a refrigerant ^[212][16] nor is HFC-134a included in the
   § 7671a listing of class I and class II substances.^[213][17]

Production and reactions

   [[214]edit]

   Tetrafluoroethane is typically made by reacting [215]trichloroethylene
   with [216]hydrogen fluoride:^[217][18]

          CHCl=CCl[2] + 4 HF → CF[3]CH[2]F + 3 HCl

   It reacts with butyllithium to give trifluorovinyl lithium:^[218][19]

          CF[3]CH[2]F + 2 BuLi → CF[2]=CFLi + LiF + 2 BuH

Safety

   [[219]edit]

   [220][220px-R134a_container.png] R-134a cylinder

   Mixtures with air of the gas 1,1,1,2-tetrafluoroethane are not
   [221]flammable at atmospheric pressure and temperatures up to 100 °C
   (212 °F). However, mixtures with high concentrations of air at elevated
   pressure and/or temperature can be [222]ignited.^[223][20] Contact of
   1,1,1,2-tetrafluoroethane with flames or hot surfaces in excess of
   250 °C (482 °F) may cause vapor [224]decomposition and the emission of
   [225]toxic gases including [226]hydrogen fluoride and [227]carbonyl
   fluoride,^[228][21] however the decomposition temperature has been
   reported as above 370 °C.^[229][22] 1,1,1,2-Tetrafluoroethane itself
   has an [230]LD[50] of 1,500 g/m^3 in rats, making it relatively
   non-toxic, apart from the dangers inherent to [231]inhalant abuse. Its
   gaseous form is denser than air and will displace air in the lungs.
   This can result in [232]asphyxiation if excessively
   inhaled.^[233][23]^[234][24] This contributes to most deaths by
   [235]inhalant abuse.

   Aerosol cans containing 1,1,1,2-tetrafluoroethane, when inverted,
   become effective freeze sprays. Under pressure,
   1,1,1,2-tetrafluoroethane is compressed into a liquid, which upon
   vaporization absorbs a significant amount of [236]thermal energy. As a
   result, it will greatly lower the temperature of any object it contacts
   as it evaporates.
   [237][220px-Freon_134a_refrigerant_for_car_AC_001_%28cropped%29.jpg]
   Freon 134a refrigerant for car AC

Medical use

   [[238]edit]

   For its medical uses, 1,1,1,2-tetrafluoroethane has the [239]generic
   name norflurane. It is used as propellant for some metered dose
   [240]inhalers.^[241][25] It is considered safe for this
   use.^[242][26]^[243][27]^[244][28] In combination with
   [245]pentafluoropropane, it is used as a topical [246]vapocoolant spray
   for numbing [247]boils before [248]curettage.^[249][29]^[250][30] It
   has also been studied as a potential [251]inhalational
   anesthetic,^[252][31] but it is nonanaesthetic at doses used in
   inhalers.^[253][26]
