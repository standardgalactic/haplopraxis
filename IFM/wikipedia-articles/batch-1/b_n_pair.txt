   From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

   In [64]mathematics, a (B, N) pair is a structure on [65]groups of Lie
   type that allows one to give uniform [66]proofs of many results,
   instead of giving a large number of case-by-case proofs. Roughly
   speaking, it shows that all such [67]groups are similar to the
   [68]general linear group over a [69]field. They were introduced by the
   mathematician [70]Jacques Tits, and are also sometimes known as Tits
   systems.

Definition

   [[71]edit]

   A (B, N) pair is a pair of [72]subgroups B and N of a group G such that
   the following axioms hold:
     * G is generated by B and N.
     * The [73]intersection, T, of B and N is a [74]normal subgroup of N.
     * The group W = N/T is generated by a set S of elements of [75]order
       2 such that
          + If s is an element of S and w is an element of W then sBw is
            contained in the [76]union of BswB and BwB.
          + No element of S normalizes B.

   The set S is uniquely determined by B and N and the pair (W,S) is a
   [77]Coxeter system.^[78][1]

Terminology

   [[79]edit]

   BN pairs are closely related to [80]reductive groups and the
   terminology in both subjects overlaps. The size of S is called the
   rank. We call
     * B the (standard) [81]Borel subgroup,
     * T the (standard) [82]Cartan subgroup, and
     * W the [83]Weyl group.

   A subgroup of G is called
     * parabolic if it contains a conjugate of B,
     * standard parabolic if, in fact, it contains B itself, and
     * a Borel (or minimal parabolic) if it is a conjugate of B.

Examples

   [[84]edit]

   Abstract examples of (B, N) pairs arise from certain group actions.
     * Suppose that G is any [85]doubly transitive permutation group on a
       set E with more than 2 elements. We let B be the subgroup of G
       fixing a point x, and we let N be the subgroup fixing or exchanging
       2 points x and y. The subgroup T is then the set of elements fixing
       both x and y, and W has order 2 and its nontrivial element is
       represented by anything exchanging x and y.
     * Conversely, if G has a (B, N) pair of rank 1, then the action of G
       on the cosets of B is [86]doubly transitive. So (B, N) pairs of
       rank 1 are more or less the same as doubly transitive actions on
       sets with more than 2 elements.

   More concrete examples of (B, N) pairs can be found in reductive
   groups.
     * Suppose that G is the general linear group GL[n] K over a field K.
       We take B to be the [87]upper triangular [88]matrices, T to be the
       [89]diagonal matrices, and N to be the [90]monomial matrices, i.e.
       matrices with exactly one non-zero element in each row and column.
       There are n − 1 generators, represented by the matrices obtained by
       swapping two adjacent rows of a diagonal matrix. The Weyl group is
       the [91]symmetric group on n letters.
     * More generally, if G is a reductive group over a field K then the
       group G = G(K) has a (B, N) pair in which
          + B = P(K), where P is a minimal parabolic subgroup of G, and
          + N = N(K), where N is the normalizer of a split maximal torus
            contained in P.^[92][2]
     * In particular, any [93]finite [94]group of Lie type has the
       structure of a (B, N) pair.
          + Over the [95]field of two elements, the Cartan subgroup is
            trivial in this example.
     * A semisimple simply-connected [96]algebraic group over a [97]local
       field has a (B, N) pair where B is an [98]Iwahori subgroup.

Properties

   [[99]edit]

Bruhat decomposition

   [[100]edit]

   The [101]Bruhat decomposition states that G = BWB. More precisely, the
   [102]double cosets B\G/B are represented by a [103]set of lifts of W to
   N.^[104][3]

Parabolic subgroups

   [[105]edit]

   Every parabolic subgroup equals its [106]normalizer in G.^[107][4]

   Every standard parabolic is of the form BW(X)B for some subset X of S,
   where W(X) denotes the Coxeter subgroup generated by X. Moreover, two
   standard parabolics are conjugate [108]if and only if their sets X are
   the same. Hence there is a [109]bijection between subsets of S and
   standard parabolics.^[110][5] More generally, this bijection extends to
   conjugacy classes of parabolic subgroups.^[111][6]

Tits's simplicity theorem

   [[112]edit]

   BN-pairs can be used to prove that many groups of Lie type are
   [113]simple modulo their [114]centers. More precisely, if G has a
   BN-pair such that B is a [115]solvable group, the intersection of all
   conjugates of B is trivial, and the set of generators of W cannot be
   decomposed into two [116]non-empty commuting sets, then G is simple
   whenever it is a [117]perfect group. In practice all of these
   conditions except for G being perfect are easy to check. Checking that
   G is perfect needs some slightly messy calculations (and in fact there
   are a few small groups of Lie type which are not perfect). But showing
   that a group is perfect is usually far easier than showing it is
   simple.
