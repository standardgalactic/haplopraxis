   From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
   English-language suffix
   Look up [83]-ism in Wiktionary, the free dictionary.

   -ism ([84]/-ˌɪzəm/) is a [85]suffix in many [86]English words,
   originally derived from the [87]Ancient Greek suffix [88]-ισμός
   (-ismós), and reached [89]English through the [90]Latin -ismus, and the
   [91]French -isme.^[92][1] It is used to create abstract nouns of
   action, state, condition, or doctrine, and is often used to describe
   [93]philosophies, [94]theories, [95]religions, [96]social movements,
   [97]artistic movements, [98]lifestyles,^[99][2] [100]behaviors,
   [101]scientific phenomena,^[102][3] or [103]medical
   conditions.^[104][4]^[105][5]

   The concept of an -ism may resemble that of a [106]grand
   narrative.^[107][6]

   Skeptics of any given -isms can quote the dictum attributed to
   [108]Eisenhower: "All -isms are wasms".^[109][7]

History

   [[110]edit]

   The first recorded usage of the suffix ism as a separate word in its
   own right was in 1680. By the nineteenth century it was being used by
   [111]Thomas Carlyle to signify a pre-packaged [112]ideology. It was
   later used in this sense by such writers as [113]Julian Huxley and
   [114]George Bernard Shaw. In the United States of the mid-nineteenth
   century, the phrase "the isms" was used as a collective derogatory term
   to lump together the radical social reform movements of the day (such
   as [115]slavery abolitionism, [116]feminism, [117]alcohol
   prohibitionism, [118]Fourierism, [119]pacifism, Technoism, early
   [120]socialism, etc.) and various spiritual or religious movements
   considered non-mainstream by the standards of the time (such as
   [121]transcendentalism, [122]spiritualism, [123]Mormonism etc.).
   Southerners often prided themselves on the American South being free
   from all of these pernicious "Isms" (except for alcohol temperance
   campaigning, which was compatible with a traditional Protestant focus
   on individual morality). So on September 5 and 9, 1856, the Examiner
   newspaper of [124]Richmond, Virginia, ran editorials on "Our Enemies,
   the Isms and their Purposes", while in 1858 [125]Parson Brownlow called
   for a "Missionary Society of the South, for the Conversion of the
   Freedom Shriekers, Spiritualists, Free-lovers, Fourierites, and
   [126]Infidel Reformers of the North" (see The Freedom-of-thought
   Struggle in the Old South by [127]Clement Eaton). In the present day,
   it appears in the title of a standard survey of political thought,
   Today's Isms by William Ebenstein, first published in the 1950s, and
   now in its 11th edition.

   In 2004, the [128]Oxford English Dictionary added two new draft
   definitions of -isms to reference their relationship to words that
   convey injustice:^[129][8]
     * "Forming nouns with the sense 'belief in the superiority of
       one—over another'; as [130]racism, [131]sexism, [132]speciesism,
       etc."
     * "Forming nouns with the sense '[133]discrimination or
       [134]prejudice against on the basis of—'; as [135]ageism, bodyism,
       [136]heightism, faceism, [137]lookism, [138]sizeism,
       [139]weightism, etc."

   In December 2015, [140]Merriam-Webster Dictionary declared -ism to be
   the Word of the Year.^[141][9]
