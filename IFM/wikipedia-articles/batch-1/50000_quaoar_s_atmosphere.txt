   Quaoar ([247]minor-planet designation: 50000 Quaoar) is a large,
   [248]ringed [249]dwarf planet in the [250]Kuiper belt, a region of icy
   [251]planetesimals beyond [252]Neptune. It has an elongated
   [253]ellipsoidal shape with an average diameter of 1,090 km (680 mi),
   about half the size of the dwarf planet [254]Pluto. The object was
   discovered by American astronomers [255]Chad Trujillo and [256]Michael
   Brown at the [257]Palomar Observatory on 4 June 2002. Quaoar's surface
   contains crystalline water ice and [258]ammonia hydrate, which suggests
   that it might have experienced [259]cryovolcanism. A small amount of
   [260]methane is present on its surface, which can only be retained by
   the largest Kuiper belt objects.

   Quaoar has one known moon, [261]Weywot, which was discovered by Brown
   in February 2007.^[262][20] Both objects were named after mythological
   figures from the Native American [263]Tongva people in Southern
   California. [264]Quaoar is the Tongva creator deity and [265]Weywot is
   his son. In 2023, astronomers announced the discovery of two thin
   [266]rings orbiting Quaoar outside its [267]Roche limit, which defies
   theoretical expectations that rings outside the Roche limit should not
   be stable.^[268][13]

History

   [[269]edit]

Discovery

   [[270]edit]

   [271][220px-P48_1994_Jean_Large.jpg] Quaoar was discovered using the
   [272]Samuel Oschin telescope at [273]Palomar
   Observatory[274][220px-Quaoar_discovery_animation.gif] Animation of
   three discovery images taken over a period of 4.5 hours, showing the
   slow movement of Quaoar (indicated by the arrow)^[275][21]

   Quaoar was discovered on 4 June 2002 by American astronomers [276]Chad
   Trujillo and [277]Michael Brown at the [278]Palomar Observatory in the
   [279]Palomar Mountain Range in [280]San Diego County,
   California.^[281][1] The discovery formed part of the Caltech Wide Area
   Sky Survey, which was designed to search for the brightest [282]Kuiper
   belt objects using the Palomar Observatory's 1.22-meter [283]Samuel
   Oschin telescope.^[284][22] Quaoar was first identified in images by
   Trujillo on 5 June 2002, when he noticed a dim, 18.6-[285]magnitude
   object slowly moving among the stars of the [286]constellation
   [287]Ophiuchus.^[288][23]^[289][24] Quaoar appeared relatively bright
   for a distant object, suggesting that it could have a size comparable
   to the diameter of [290]Pluto.^[291][25]

   To ascertain Quaoar's orbit, Brown and Trujillo initiated a search for
   archival [292]precovery images. They obtained several precovery images
   taken by the [293]Near-Earth Asteroid Tracking survey from various
   observatories in 1996 and 2000–2002.^[294][21] In particular, they had
   also found two archival [295]photographic plates taken by astronomer
   [296]Charles T. Kowal in May 1983,^[297][24] who at the time was
   searching for the hypothesized [298]Planet X at the Palomar
   Observatory.^[299][26]^[300][27] From these precovery images, Brown and
   Trujillo were able to calculate Quaoar's orbit and distance. Additional
   precovery images of Quaoar have been later identified, with the
   earliest known found by Edward Rhoads on a photographic plate imaged on
   25 May 1954 from the [301]Palomar Observatory Sky
   Survey.^[302][1]^[303][3]

   Before announcing the discovery of Quaoar, Brown had planned to conduct
   follow-up observations using the [304]Hubble Space Telescope to measure
   Quaoar's size.^[305][28] He had also planned to announce the discovery
   as soon as possible and found it necessary to keep the discovery
   information confidential during the follow-up observations.^[306][29]
   Rather than submitting his Hubble proposal under [307]peer review,
   Brown submitted his proposal directly to one of Hubble's operators, who
   promptly allocated time to Brown.^[308][29]^[309][30] While setting up
   the observing algorithm for Hubble, Brown had also planned to use one
   of the [310]Keck telescopes in [311]Mauna Kea, Hawaii, as a part of a
   study on [312]cryovolcanism on the [313]moons of [314]Uranus.^[315][29]
   This provided him additional time for follow-up observations and took
   advantage of the whole observing session in July to analyze Quaoar's
   [316]spectrum and characterize its surface
   composition.^[317][31]^[318][29]

   The discovery of Quaoar was formally announced by the [319]Minor Planet
   Center in a [320]Minor Planet Electronic Circular on 7 October
   2002.^[321][24] It was given the [322]provisional designation 2002
   LM[60], indicating that its discovery took place during the first half
   of June 2002.^[323][24]^[324][32] Quaoar was the 1,512th object
   discovered in the first half of June, as indicated by the preceding
   letter and numbers in its provisional designation.^[325][d] On that
   same day, Trujillo and Brown reported their scientific results from
   observations of Quaoar at the 34th annual meeting of the [326]American
   Astronomical Society's [327]Division for Planetary Sciences in
   [328]Birmingham, Alabama. They announced Quaoar was the largest Kuiper
   belt object found yet, surpassing previous record holders [329]20000
   Varuna and [330]2002 AW197.^[331][22]^[332][28] Quaoar's discovery has
   been cited by Brown as having contributed to the [333]reclassification
   of Pluto as a dwarf planet.^[334][29] Since then, Brown has contributed
   to the discovery of larger [335]trans-Neptunian objects, including
   [336]Haumea, [337]Eris, [338]Makemake and [339]Gonggong.

Name and symbol

   [[340]edit]

   Upon Quaoar's discovery, it was initially given the temporary nickname
   "Object X" as a reference to [341]Planet X, due to its potentially
   large size and unknown nature.^[342][29] At the time, Quaoar's size was
   uncertain, and its high brightness led the discovery team to speculate
   that it may be a possible tenth planet. After measuring Quaoar's size
   with the Hubble Space Telescope in July, the team began considering
   names for the object, particularly those from local [343]Native
   American mythologies.^[344][29] Following the [345]International
   Astronomical Union's (IAU) [346]naming convention for [347]minor
   planets, non-resonant Kuiper belt objects are to be named after
   [348]creation deities.^[349][32] The team settled on the name
   [350]Kwawar, the creator god of the [351]Tongva people indigenous to
   the [352]Los Angeles Basin, where Brown's institute, the
   [353]California Institute of Technology, was located.^[354][26]

   According to Brown, the name "Quaoar" is pronounced with three
   syllables, and Trujillo's website on Quaoar gives a three-syllable
   pronunciation, [355]/ˈkwɑː.oʊ(w)ɑːr/, as an approximation of the Tongva
   pronunciation [356][ˈkʷaʔuwar].^[357][23] The name can be also
   pronounced as two syllables, [358]/ˈkwɑːwɑːr/, reflecting the usual
   English spelling and pronunciation of the deity
   Kwawar.^[359][28]^[360][33]^[361][34]

   In Tongva mythology, Kwawar is the genderless^[362][33] creation force
   of the universe, singing and dancing deities into existence.^[363][2]
   He first sings and dances to create Weywot (Sky Father), then they
   together sing Chehooit (Earth Mother) and Tamit (Grandfather Sun) into
   existence. As they did this, the creation force became more complex as
   each new deity joined the singing and dancing. Eventually, after
   reducing chaos to order, they created the seven great giants that
   upheld the world,^[364][23]^[365][28] then the animals and finally the
   first man and woman, Tobohar and Pahavit.^[366][23]

   Upon their investigation of names from Tongva mythology, Brown and
   Trujillo realized that there were contemporary members of the Tongva
   people, whom they contacted for permission to use the name.^[367][29]
   They consulted tribal historian Marc Acuña, who confirmed that the name
   Kwawar would indeed be an appropriate name for the newly discovered
   object.^[368][23]^[369][33] However, the Tongva preferred the spelling
   Qua-o-ar, which Brown and Trujillo adopted, though with the hyphens
   omitted.^[370][29] The name and discovery of Quaoar were publicly
   announced in October, though Brown had not sought approval of the name
   by the IAU's [371]Committee on Small Body Nomenclature
   (CSBN).^[372][29] Indeed, Quaoar's name was announced before the
   official numbering of the object, which [373]Brian Marsden—the head of
   the Minor Planet Center—remarked in 2004 to be a violation of the
   protocol.^[374][29]^[375][35] Despite this, the name was approved by
   the CSBN, and the naming citation, along with Quaoar's official
   numbering, was published in a [376]Minor Planet Circular on 20 November
   2002.^[377][36]

   Quaoar was given the [378]minor planet number 50000, which was not by
   coincidence but to commemorate its large size, being that it was found
   in the search for a Pluto-sized object in the Kuiper belt.^[379][36]
   The large Kuiper belt object 20000 Varuna was similarly numbered for a
   similar occasion.^[380][37] However, subsequent even larger discoveries
   such as [381]136199 Eris were simply numbered according to the order in
   which their orbits were confirmed.^[382][32]

   The usage of [383]planetary symbols is no longer recommended in
   astronomy, so Quaoar never received a symbol in the astronomical
   literature. A symbol ⟨[384] 🝾 ⟩, used mostly among
   astrologers,^[385][38] is included in [386]Unicode as
   U+1F77E.^[387][39] The symbol was designed by Denis Moskowitz, a
   software engineer in Massachusetts; it combines the letter Q (for
   'Quaoar') with a canoe, and is stylized to recall angular Tongva rock
   art.^[388][40]

Orbit and classification

   [[389]edit]

   [390][330px-TheKuiperBelt_Orbits_Quaoar.svg.png] [391]Ecliptic view of
   Quaoar's orbit (blue) compared to Pluto (red) and Neptune (white). The
   approximate perihelion (q) and aphelion (Q) dates are marked for their
   respective orbits.[392][220px-Cubewanos.png] Polar view of Quaoar's
   orbit (yellow) along with various other large Kuiper belt objects

   Quaoar orbits the [393]Sun at an average distance of 43.7 [394]AU
   (6.54 billion km; 4.06 billion mi), taking 288.8 years to complete one
   full orbit around the Sun. With an [395]orbital eccentricity of 0.04,
   Quaoar follows a nearly circular orbit, only slightly varying in
   distance from 42 AU at [396]perihelion to 45 AU at
   [397]aphelion.^[398][3] At such distances, light from the Sun takes
   more than 5 hours to reach Quaoar.^[399][23] Quaoar has last passed
   aphelion in late 1932 and is currently approaching the Sun at a rate of
   0.035 AU per year, or about 170 meters per second (380 mph).^[400][41]
   Quaoar will reach perihelion around February 2075.^[401][6]

   Because Quaoar has a nearly circular orbit, it does not approach close
   to [402]Neptune such that its orbit can become significantly
   [403]perturbed under the gravitational influence of Neptune.^[404][4]
   Quaoar's [405]minimum orbit intersection distance from Neptune is only
   12.3 AU—it does not approach Neptune within this distance over the
   course of its orbit, as it is not in a [406]mean-motion orbital
   resonance with Neptune.^[407][1]^[408][4] Simulations by the [409]Deep
   Ecliptic Survey show that the perihelion and aphelion distances of
   Quaoar's orbit do not change significantly over the next ten million
   years; Quaoar's orbit appears to be stable over the long term.^[410][4]

   Quaoar is a [411]trans-Neptunian object.^[412][3] It is classified as a
   [413]distant minor planet by the Minor Planet Center.^[414][1] Because
   Quaoar is not in a mean-motion resonance with Neptune, it is also
   classified as a [415]classical Kuiper belt object (cubewano) by the
   Minor Planet Center and Deep Ecliptic Survey.^[416][4]^[417][5]
   Quaoar's orbit is moderately inclined to the [418]ecliptic plane by 8
   degrees, relatively high when compared to the inclinations of Kuiper
   belt objects within the dynamically cold
   population.^[419][29]^[420][42] Because Quaoar's [421]orbital
   inclination is greater than 4 degrees, it is part of the dynamically
   hot population of high-inclination classical Kuiper belt
   objects.^[422][42] The high inclinations of hot classical Kuiper belt
   objects such as Quaoar are thought to have resulted from gravitational
   scattering by Neptune during its outward [423]migration in the early
   Solar System.^[424][43]

Physical characteristics

   [[425]edit]

Size and shape

   [[426]edit]

   CAPTION: History of diameter estimates for Quaoar

   As of 2024^[434][update], measurements of Quaoar's shape from its
   rotational [435]light curve and [436]stellar occultations show that
   Quaoar is a [437]triaxial ellipsoid with an average diameter of
   1,090 km (680 mi).^[438][7] Quaoar's diameter is roughly half that of
   Pluto and is slightly smaller than Pluto's moon [439]Charon.^[440][29]
   At the time of its discovery in 2002, Quaoar was the largest object
   found in the [441]Solar System since the discovery of Pluto.^[442][29]
   Quaoar was also the first [443]trans-Neptunian object to be measured
   directly from [444]Hubble Space Telescope images.^[445][19]

   Quaoar's [446]far-infrared [447]thermal emission and brightness in
   visible light both vary significantly (visible light curve amplitude
   0.12–0.16 [448]magnitudes) as Quaoar rotates every 17.68 hours, which
   most likely indicates Quaoar is elongated along its equator.^[449][7] A
   2024 analysis of Quaoar's visible and far-infrared rotational light
   curve by Csaba Kiss and collaborators determined that the lengths of
   Quaoar's equatorial axes differ by 19% (a/b = 1.19) and the lengths of
   Quaoar's polar and shortest equatorial axis differ by 16% (b/c = 1.16),
   which corresponds to ellipsoid dimensions of 1,286 km × 1,080 km
   × 932 km (799 mi × 671 mi × 579 mi).^[450][a]^[451][7] The ellipsoidal
   shape of Quaoar matches the size and shape measurements from previous
   stellar occultations, and also explains why the size and shape of
   Quaoar appeared to change in these occultations.^[452][7]^: 6 
   [453][220px-Quaoar_ellipsoid_Kiss_et_al._2024.png] Diagram showing
   three mutually orthogonal views of Quaoar's ellipsoidal shape

   Quaoar's elongated shape contradicts theoretical expectations that it
   should be in [454]hydrostatic equilibrium, because of its large size
   and slow rotation.^[455][7]^: 10  According to Michael Brown, rocky
   bodies around 900 km (560 mi) in diameter should relax into
   [456]hydrostatic equilibrium, whereas icy bodies relax into hydrostatic
   equilibrium somewhere between 200 km (120 mi) and 400 km
   (250 mi).^[457][48] Slowly-rotating objects in hydrostatic equilibrium
   are expected to be [458]oblate spheroids ([459]Maclaurin spheroids),
   whereas rapidly-rotating objects in hydrostatic equilibrium, such as
   [460]Haumea which rotates in nearly 4 hours, are expected to be
   flattened and elongated ellipsoids ([461]Jacobi
   ellipsoids).^[462][7]^: 10  To explain Quaoar's non-equilibrium shape,
   Kiss and collaborators hypothesized that Quaoar originally had a rapid
   rotation and was in hydrostatic equilibrium, but its shape became
   "frozen in" and did not change as Quaoar spun down due to [463]tidal
   forces from its moon Weywot.^[464][7]^: 10  This would resemble the
   situation of Saturn's moon [465]Iapetus, which is too oblate for its
   current rotation rate.^[466][49]

Mass and density

   [[467]edit]

   [468][220px-Quaoar%2C_Earth_%26_Moon_size_comparison.png] Quaoar
   compared to the [469]Earth and the [470]Moon

   Quaoar has a mass of 1.2×10^21 kg, which was determined from Weywot's
   orbit using [471]Kepler's third law.^[472][13] Measurements of Quaoar's
   diameter and mass as of 2024^[473][update] indicate it has a density
   between 1.66–1.77 g/cm^3, which suggests its interior is composed of
   roughly 70% rock and 30% ice with low [474]porosity.^[475][7]^: 10–11 
   Quaoar's density was previously thought to be much higher, between
   2–4 g/cm^3, because early measurements inaccurately suggested that
   Quaoar had a smaller diameter and a higher mass.^[476][7]^: 10  These
   early high-density estimates for Quaoar led researchers to hypothesize
   that the object might be a rocky [477]planetary core exposed by a large
   [478]impact event, but these hypotheses have since become obsolete as
   newer estimates indicate a lower density for
   Quaoar.^[479][45]^: 1550 ^[480][7]^: 10 

Surface

   [[481]edit]

   Quaoar has a dark surface that reflects about 12% of the visible light
   it receives from the Sun.^[482][13] This may indicate that fresh ice
   has disappeared from Quaoar's surface.^[483][45] The surface is
   moderately red, meaning that Quaoar reflects longer (redder)
   wavelengths of light more than shorter (bluer) wavelengths.^[484][50]
   Many Kuiper belt objects such as [485]20000 Varuna and [486]28978 Ixion
   share a similar moderately red color.

   Spectroscopic observations by [487]David Jewitt and [488]Jane Luu in
   2004 revealed signs of crystalline water [489]ice and [490]ammonia
   hydrate on Quaoar's surface. These substances are expected to gradually
   break down due to solar and cosmic radiation, and crystalline water ice
   can only form in warm temperatures of at least 110 K (−163 °C), so the
   presence of crystalline water ice on Quaoar's surface indicates that it
   was heated to this temperature sometime in the last ten million
   years.^[491][50]^: 731  For context, Quaoar's present-day surface
   temperature is less than 50 K (−223.2 °C).^[492][50]^: 732  Jewitt and
   Luu proposed two hypotheses for Quaoar's heating, which are [493]impact
   events and [494]radiogenic heating.^[495][50]^: 731  The latter
   hypothesis allows for the possibility of [496]cryovolcanism on Quaoar,
   which is supported by the presence of ammonia hydrate on Quaoar's
   surface.^[497][50]^: 733  Ammonia hydrate is believed to be
   cryovolcanically deposited onto Quaoar's surface.^[498][50]^: 733  A
   2006 study by Hauke Hussmann and collaborators suggested that
   radiogenic heating alone may not be capable of sustaining an
   [499]internal ocean of liquid water at Quaoar's mantle–core
   boundary.^[500][51]

   More precise observations of Quaoar's near [501]infrared spectrum in
   2007 indicated the presence of small quantities (5%) of solid
   [502]methane and [503]ethane. Given its [504]boiling point of 112 K
   (−161 °C), methane is a volatile ice at average surface temperatures of
   Quaoar, unlike water ice or ethane. Both models and observations
   suggest that only a few larger bodies ([505]Pluto, [506]Eris and
   [507]Makemake) can retain the volatile ices whereas the dominant
   population of small [508]trans-Neptunian objects lost them. Quaoar,
   with only small amounts of methane, appears to be in an intermediary
   category.^[509][31]

   In 2022, low-resolution near-infrared (0.7–5 μm) [510]spectroscopic
   observations by the [511]James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) revealed the
   presence of [512]carbon dioxide ice, complex organics, and significant
   amounts of [513]ethane ice on Quaoar's surface. Other possible chemical
   compounds include [514]hydrogen cyanide and [515]carbon
   monoxide.^[516][52]^: 4  JWST also took medium-resolution near-infrared
   spectra of Quaoar and found evidence of small amounts of [517]methane
   on Quaoar's surface. However, both JWST's low- and medium-resolution
   spectra of Quaoar did not show conclusive signs of ammonia
   hydrates.^[518][52]^: 10 

Possible atmosphere

   [[519]edit]

   The presence of methane and other [520]volatiles on Quaoar's surface
   suggest that it may support a tenuous [521]atmosphere produced from the
   [522]sublimation of volatiles.^[523][14] With a measured mean
   temperature of approximately 44 K (−229.2 °C), the upper limit of
   Quaoar's [524]atmospheric pressure is expected to be in the range of a
   few [525]microbars.^[526][14] Due to Quaoar's small size and mass, the
   possibility of Quaoar having an atmosphere of [527]nitrogen and
   [528]carbon monoxide has been ruled out, since the gases would escape
   from Quaoar.^[529][14] The possibility of a methane atmosphere, with
   the upper limit being less than 1 microbar,^[530][47]^[531][14] was
   considered until 2013, when Quaoar [532]occulted a 15.8-magnitude star
   and revealed no sign of a substantial atmosphere, placing an upper
   limit to at least 20 nanobars, under the assumption that Quaoar's mean
   temperature is 42 K (−231.2 °C) and that its atmosphere consists of
   mostly methane.^[533][47]^[534][14] The upper limit of atmosphere
   pressure was tightened to 10 nanobars after another stellar occultation
   in 2019.^[535][53]

Satellite

   [[536]edit]

   [537][330px-Artist_impression_of_Quaoar_and_its_ring_ESA24681885.jpeg]
   Artist's impression of Quaoar with its ring and its moon Weywot

   Main article: [538]Weywot

   Quaoar has one known moon, [539]Weywot (full designation (50000) Quaoar
   I Weywot), discovered in 2006 and named after the sky god [540]Weywot,
   son of Quaoar.^[541][20]^[542][54] It orbits Quaoar at distance of
   about 13,300 km and is thought to be approximately 170 km (110 mi) in
   diameter.^[543][55]

Rings

   [[544]edit]

Discovery

   [[545]edit]

   [546][440px-Quaoar_rings_2022-08-09_occultation_chord.png] [547]Light
   curve graph of a star's brightness as seen by the [548]Gemini North
   Observatory during the 9 August 2022 occultation by Quaoar and its two
   rings. The asymmetry of the outer Q1R ring's opacity is apparent from
   its differing brightness dips before and after the occultation by
   Quaoar at the center.

   Besides accurately determining sizes and shapes, stellar occultation
   campaigns were planned on a long-term basis to search for rings and/or
   atmospheres around small bodies of the outer solar system. These
   campaigns agglomerated efforts of various teams in France, Spain and
   Brazil and were conducted under the umbrella of the [549]European
   Research Council project Lucky Star.^[550][10] The discovery of
   Quaoar's first known ring, Q1R, involved various instruments used
   during stellar occultations observed between 2018 and 2021: the robotic
   ATOM telescope of the [551]High Energy Stereoscopic System (HESS) in
   Namibia, the 10.4-m [552]Gran Telescopio Canarias (La Palma Island,
   Spain); the ESA [553]CHEOPS space telescope, and several stations run
   by citizen astronomers in Australia where a report of a Neptune-like
   ring originated and a dense arc in Q1R was first
   observed.^[554][10]^[555][56]^[556][57] Taken together, these
   observations reveal the presence of a partly dense, mostly tenuous and
   uniquely distant ring around Quaoar, a discovery announced in February
   2023.^[557][10]^[558][56]

   In April 2023, astronomers of the Lucky Star project published the
   discovery of another ring of Quaoar, Q2R.^[559][13] The Q2R ring was
   detected by the highly-sensitive 8.2-m [560]Gemini North and the 4.0-m
   [561]Canada-France-Hawaii Telescope in Mauna Kea, Hawaii, during an
   observing campaign to confirm Quaoar's Q1R ring in a stellar
   occultation on 9 August 2022.^[562][13] Quaoar is the fourth minor
   planet known and confirmed to have a [563]ring system, after [564]10199
   Chariklo, [565]2060 Chiron, and [566]Haumea.^[567][10]^[568][e]

Properties

   [[569]edit]

   Orbit diagrams of the Quaoar–Weywot system
   Viewed from Earth
   Viewed top-down over Quaoar's north pole

   Quaoar possesses two narrow rings, provisionally named Q1R and Q2R by
   order of discovery, which are confined at radial distances where their
   orbital periods are integer ratios of Quaoar's rotational period. That
   is, the rings of Quaoar are in [570]spin-orbit resonances.^[571][13]

   The outer ring, Q1R, orbits Quaoar at a distance of 4,057 ± 6 km
   (2,521 ± 4 mi), over seven times the radius of Quaoar and more than
   double the theoretical maximum distance of the [574]Roche
   limit.^[575][13] The Q1R ring is not uniform and is strongly irregular
   around its circumference, being more opaque (and denser) where it is
   narrow and less opaque where it is broader.^[576][10] The Q1R ring's
   radial width ranges from 5 to 300 km (3 to 200 mi) while its
   [577]optical depth ranges from 0.004 to 0.7.^[578][13] The irregular
   width of the Q1R ring resembles [579]Saturn's frequently-[580]perturbed
   [581]F ring or [582]Neptune's [583]ring arcs, which may imply the
   presence of small, kilometer-sized [584]moonlets embedded within the
   Q1R ring and gravitationally perturbing the material. The Q1R ring
   likely consists of icy particles that [585]elastically collide with
   each other without [586]accreting into a larger mass.^[587][10]

   Q1R is located in between the 6:1 [588]mean-motion orbital resonance
   with Quaoar's moon Weywot at 4,021 ± 57 km (2,499 ± 35 mi) and Quaoar's
   1:3 spin-orbit resonance at 4,197 ± 58 km (2,608 ± 36 mi). The Q1R
   ring's coincidental location at these resonances implies they play a
   key role in maintaining the ring without having it accrete into a
   single moon.^[589][10] In particular, the confinement of rings to the
   1:3 spin-orbit resonance may be common among ringed small Solar System
   bodies, as it has been previously seen in Chariklo and
   Haumea.^[590][10]

   The inner ring, Q2R, orbits Quaoar at a distance of 2,520 ± 20 km
   (1,566 ± 12 mi), about four and a half times Quaoar's radius and also
   outside Quaoar's Roche limit.^[591][13] The Q2R ring's location
   coincides with Quaoar's 5:7 spin-orbit resonance at 2,525 ± 58 km
   (1,569 ± 36 mi). Compared to Q1R, the Q2R ring appears relatively
   uniform with a radial width of 10 km (6.2 mi). With an optical depth of
   0.004, the Q2R ring is very tenuous and its opacity is comparable to
   the least dense part of the Q1R ring.^[592][13]

Exploration

   [[593]edit]

   [594][220px-Quaoar_animation_dark_crsub_circle.gif] Quaoar from
   [595]New Horizons viewed at a distance of 14 [596]AU

   It has been calculated that a flyby mission to Quaoar using a Jupiter
   gravity assist would take 13.6 years, for launch dates of 25 December
   2026, 22 November 2027, 22 December 2028, 22 January 2030 and 20
   December 2040. Quaoar would be 41 to 43 AU from the Sun when the
   spacecraft arrived.^[597][58] In July 2016, the [598]Long Range
   Reconnaissance Imager (LORRI) aboard the [599]New Horizons spacecraft
   took a sequence of four images of Quaoar from a distance of about
   14 AU.^[600][59] [601]Interstellar Probe, a concept by Pontus Brandt
   and his colleagues at Johns Hopkins [602]Applied Physics Laboratory
   would potentially fly by Quaoar in the 2030s before continuing to the
   [603]interstellar medium, and the first of [604]China National Space
   Administration's proposed [605]Shensuo probe designed to explore the
   heliosphere has it considered as a potential flyby
   target.^[606][60]^[607][61]^[608][62] Quaoar has been chosen as a flyby
   target for missions like these particularly for its [609]escaping
   methane atmosphere and possible cryovolcanism, as well as its close
   proximity to the [610]heliospheric nose.^[611][60]
