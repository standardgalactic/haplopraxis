   1 (one, unit, unity) is a [342]number, [343]numeral, and [344]glyph. It
   is the first and smallest [345]positive integer of the infinite
   sequence of [346]natural numbers. This fundamental property has led to
   its unique uses in other fields, ranging from science to sports, where
   it commonly denotes the first, leading, or top thing in a group. 1 is
   the [347]unit of [348]counting or [349]measurement, a determiner for
   singular nouns, and a gender-neutral pronoun. Historically, the
   representation of 1 evolved from ancient Sumerian and Babylonian
   symbols to the modern Arabic numeral.

   In mathematics, 1 is the multiplicative identity, meaning that any
   number multiplied by 1 equals the same number. 1 is by convention not
   considered a [350]prime number. In [351]digital technology, 1
   represents the "on" state in [352]binary code, the foundation of
   [353]computing. Philosophically, 1 symbolizes the ultimate reality or
   source of existence in various traditions.

As a word

   See also: [410]One (pronoun)

   One originates from the [411]Old English word an, derived from the
   [412]Germanic root [413]*ainaz, from the [414]Proto-Indo-European root
   *oi-no- (meaning "one, unique").^[415][16] Linguistically, one is a
   [416]cardinal number used for counting and expressing the number of
   items in a collection of things.^[417][17] One is most commonly a
   [418]determiner used with [419]singular countable [420]nouns, as in one
   day at a time.^[421][18] The determiner has two senses: numerical one
   (I have one apple) and singulative one (one day I'll do it).^[422][19]
   One is also a gender-neutral [423]pronoun used to refer to an
   unspecified [424]person or to people in general as in one should take
   care of oneself.^[425][20]

   Words that derive their meaning from one include alone, which signifies
   all one in the sense of being by oneself, none meaning not one, once
   denoting one time, and atone meaning to become at one with the someone.
   Combining alone with only (implying one-like) leads to lonely,
   conveying a sense of solitude.^[426][21] Other common [427]numeral
   prefixes for the number 1 include uni- (e.g., [428]unicycle, universe,
   unicorn), sol- (e.g., solo dance), derived from Latin, or mono- (e.g.,
   [429]monorail, monogamy, monopoly) derived from
   Greek.^[430][22]^[431][23]

Symbols and representation

History

   See also: [432]History of the Hindu–Arabic numeral system

   Among the earliest known records of a numeral system, is the
   [433]Sumerian decimal-[434]sexagesimal system on [435]clay tablets
   dating from the first half of the third millennium BCE.^[436][24] The
   Archaic Sumerian numerals for 1 and 60 both consisted of horizontal
   semi-circular symbols.^[437][25] By c. 2350 BCE, the older Sumerian
   curviform numerals were replaced with [438]cuneiform symbols, with 1
   and 60 both represented by the same symbol
   [439][20px-Babylonian_1.svg.png] . The Sumerian cuneiform system is a
   direct ancestor to the [440]Eblaite and [441]Assyro-Babylonian
   [442]Semitic cuneiform [443]decimal systems.^[444][26] Surviving
   Babylonian documents date mostly from Old Babylonian (c. 1500 BCE) and
   the Seleucid (c. 300 BCE) eras.^[445][24] The Babylonian cuneiform
   script notation for numbers used the same symbol for 1 and 60 as in the
   Sumerian system.^[446][27]

   The most commonly used glyph in the modern Western world to represent
   the number 1 is the [447]Arabic numeral, a vertical line, often with a
   [448]serif at the top and sometimes a short horizontal line at the
   bottom. It can be traced back to the [449]Brahmic script of ancient
   India, as represented by [450]Ashoka as a simple vertical line in his
   [451]Edicts of Ashoka in c. 250 BCE.^[452][28] This script's numeral
   shapes were transmitted to Europe via the [453]Maghreb and
   [454]Al-Andalus during the Middle Ages ^[455][29] The Arabic numeral,
   and other glyphs used to represent the number one (e.g., Roman numeral
   (
   I ), Chinese numeral (一)) are [456]logograms. These symbols directly
   represent the concept of 'one' without breaking it down into phonetic
   components.^[457][30]

Modern typefaces

   This Woodstock typewriter from the 1940s lacks a separate key for the
   numeral 1.
   [458]Hoefler Text, a typeface designed in 1991, uses [459]text figures
   and represents the numeral 1 as similar to a small-caps I.

   In modern [460]typefaces, the shape of the character for the digit 1 is
   typically typeset as a lining figure with an [461]ascender, such that
   the digit is the same height and width as a [462]capital letter.
   However, in typefaces with [463]text figures (also known as Old style
   numerals or non-lining figures), the glyph usually is of [464]x-height
   and designed to follow the rhythm of the lowercase, as, for example, in
   [465]Horizontal guidelines with a one fitting within lines, a four
   extending below guideline, and an eight poking above guideline
   .^[466][31] In old-style typefaces (e.g., [467]Hoefler Text), the
   typeface for numeral 1 resembles a [468]small caps version of
   I, featuring parallel serifs at the top and bottom, while the capital
   I retains a full-height form. This is a relic from the [469]Roman
   numerals system where
   [470]I represents 1.^[471][32] Many older [472]typewriters do not have
   a dedicated key for the numeral 1, requiring the use of the lowercase
   letter [473]L or uppercase [474]I as

   clockwise on right, and one through twelve again clockwise on left,
   with J shapes where ones' digits would be expected when numbering the
   clock hours. Shadow suggests 3 PM toward the lower left. The 24-hour
   tower clock in [480]Venice, using J as a symbol for 1

   The lower case "

   [481]j" can be considered a [482]swash variant of a lower-case Roman
   numeral "[483]i", often employed for the final i of a "lower-case"
   Roman numeral. It is also possible to find historic examples of the use
   of j or J as a substitute for the Arabic numeral
   1.^[484][37]^[485][38]^[486][39]^[487][40] In German, the serif at the
   top may be extended into a long upstroke as long as the vertical line.
   This variation can lead to confusion with the glyph used for [488]seven
   in other countries and so to provide a visual distinction between the
   two the digit 7 may be written with a horizontal stroke through the
   vertical line.^[489][41]

In other fields

   In digital technology, data is represented by [490]binary code, i.e., a
   [491]base-2 numeral system with numbers represented by a sequence of 1s
   and [492]0s. Digitised data is represented in physical devices, such as
   [493]computers, as pulses of electricity through switching devices such
   as [494]transistors or [495]logic gates where "1" represents the value
   for "on". As such, the numerical value of [496]true is equal to 1 in
   many [497]programming languages.^[498][42]^[499][43] In [500]lambda
   calculus and [501]computability theory, natural numbers are represented
   by [502]Church encoding as functions, where the Church numeral for 1 is
   represented by the function

   In [504]physics, selected [505]physical constants are set to 1 in
   [506]natural unit systems in order to simplify the form of equations;
   for example, in [507]Planck units the [508]speed of light equals
   1.^[509][45] [510]Dimensionless quantities are also known as
   'quantities of dimension one'.^[511][46] In [512]quantum mechanics, the
   normalization condition for [513]wavefunctions requires the integral of
   a wavefunction's squared modulus to be equal to 1.^[514][47] In
   chemistry, [515]hydrogen, the first element of the [516]periodic table
   and the most [517]abundant element in the known [518]universe, has an
   [519]atomic number of 1. Group 1 of the periodic table consists of
   hydrogen and the [520]alkali metals.^[521][48]

   In philosophy, the number 1 is commonly regarded as a symbol of unity,
   often representing God or the universe in [522]monotheistic
   traditions.^[523][49] The Pythagoreans considered the numbers to be
   plural and therefore did not classify 1 itself as a number, but as the
   origin of all numbers. In their number philosophy, where odd numbers
   were considered male and even numbers female, 1 was considered neutral
   capable of transforming even numbers to odd and vice versa by
   addition.^[524][49] The [525]Neopythagorean philosopher [526]Nicomachus
   of Gerasa's number treatise, as recovered by [527]Boethius in the Latin
   translation [528]Introduction to Arithmetic, affirmed that one is not a
   number, but the source of number.^[529][50] In the philosophy of
   [530]Plotinus (and that of other [531]neoplatonists), 'The One' is the
   ultimate reality and source of all existence.^[532][51] [533]Philo of
   Alexandria (20 BC – AD 50) regarded the number one as God's number, and
   the basis for all numbers.^[534][52]
