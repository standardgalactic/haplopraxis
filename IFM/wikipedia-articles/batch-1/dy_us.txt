   Father Sky-god in Proto-Indo-European mythology

   using {{[85]lang}}, {{[86]transliteration}} for transliterated
   languages, and {{[87]IPA}} for phonetic transcriptions, with an
   appropriate [88]ISO 639 code. Wikipedia's [89]multilingual support
   templates may also be used. [90]See why. (January 2024)
   Not to be confused with [91]Deus.
   [92][316px-%D0%A2-%D0%BB%D0%B8%D0%BC%D0%B0%D0%BD10.JPG] The sky over
   the [93]feather grass-covered [94]steppe in Ukraine. *Dyḗus ph₂tḗr has
   been translated as "father daylight-sky-god".

   *Dyḗus (lit. 'daylight-sky-god'), also *Dyḗus ph₂tḗr (lit. 'father
   daylight-sky-god'),^[95][1]^[96][2] is the reconstructed name of the
   [97]daylight-sky god in [98]Proto-Indo-European mythology. *Dyēus was
   conceived as a divine personification of the bright sky of the day and
   the seat of the gods, the *deywṓs. Associated with the vast diurnal sky
   and with the fertile rains, *Dyēus was often paired with [99]*Dʰéǵʰōm,
   the [100]Earth Mother, in a relationship of union and contrast.

   While its existence is not directly attested by archaeological or
   written materials, *Dyēus is considered by scholars the most securely
   reconstructed deity of the Indo-European pantheon, as identical
   formulas referring to him can be found among the subsequent
   [101]Indo-European languages and myths of the [102]Vedic Indo-Aryans,
   [103]Latins, [104]Greeks, [105]Phrygians, [106]Messapians,
   [107]Thracians, [108]Illyrians, [109]Albanians and
   [110]Hittites.^[111][3]^[112][2]

Name

   [[113]edit]

Etymology

   [[114]edit]

   The divine name *Dyēus derives from the stem *dyeu-, denoting the
   "diurnal sky" or the "brightness of the day" (in contrast to the
   darkness of the night), ultimately from the root *di or dei- ("to
   shine, be bright").^[115][1]^[116][4] [117]Cognates in
   [118]Indo-European languages revolving around the concepts of "day",
   "sky" and "deity" and sharing the root *dyeu- as an [119]etymon, such
   as Sanskrit dyumán- 'heavenly, shining, radiant',^[120][5] suggest that
   Dyēus referred to the vast and bright sky of the day conceived as a
   divine entity among Proto-Indo-European speakers.^[121][1]^[122][4]

   A [123]vṛddhi-derivative appears in *deywós ("celestial"), the common
   word for "god" in [124]Proto-Indo-European. In classic Indo-European,
   associated with the late [125]Khvalynsk culture (3900–3500),^[126][6]
   *Dyēus also had the meaning of "Heaven", whereas it denoted "god" in
   general (or the Sun-god in particular) in the [127]Anatolian
   tradition.^[128][7] The [129]suffix-derivative *diwyós ("divine") is
   also attested in Latin, Greek and Sanskrit.^[130][4]^[131][8] The noun
   *deynos ("day"), interpreted as a back-formation of *deywós, has
   descendant cognates in [132]Albanian din ("break of the
   day"),^[133][9]^[134][10] [135]Vedic Sanskrit dína- "day"^[136][9] and
   divé-dive ("day by day"), [137]Lithuanian dienà and [138]Latvian dìena
   ("day"),^[139][9] [140]Slavic dъnъ ("day")^[141][9] or [142]Slavic
   [143]Poludnitsa ("Lady Midday"),^[144][11]^[145][12] Latin [146]Dies,
   goddess of the day and counterpart to [147]Greek [148]Hemera, Hittite
   siwat ("day"), [149]Palaic Tīyat- ("Sun, day"), [150]Ancient Greek
   endios ("midday"), [151]Old Armenian tiw (տիւ, "bright day"), [152]Old
   Irish noenden ("nine-day period"), [153]Welsh heddyw
   ("today").^[154][13]^[155][14]

   While the [156]Greek goddess Pandeia or [157]Pandia ([158]Ancient
   Greek: Πανδία, Πανδεία, "all brightness") may have been another name
   for the [159]Moon Goddess [160]Selene,^[161][15] her name still
   preserves the root *di-/*dei-, meaning "to shine, be bright".^[162][16]

Epithets

   [[163]edit]

   The most constant epithet associated with *Dyēus is "father" (*ph₂tḗr).
   The term "Father Dyēus" was inherited in the Vedic [164]Dyáuṣ Pitṛ́,
   Greek [165]Zeus Patēr, Illyrian [166]Dei-pátrous, Roman [167]Jupiter
   (*Djous patēr), even in the form of "dad" or "papa" in the
   [168]Scythian Papaios for Zeus, or the [169]Palaic expression
   [170]Tiyaz papaz.^[171][17] The epithet *Ph₂tḗr Ǵenh[1]-tōr ("Father
   Procreator") is also attested in the Vedic, Iranian, Greek, and perhaps
   the Roman ritual traditions.^[172][18]

Role

   [[173]edit]

   *Dyēus was the Sky or Day conceived as a divine entity, and thus the
   dwelling of the gods, the Heaven.^[174][7] As the gateway to the
   deities and the father of both the [175]Divine Twins and the goddess of
   the Dawn ([176]*H₂éwsōs), *Dyēus was a prominent deity in the
   [177]Proto-Indo-European pantheon.^[178][19]^[179][20] He was however
   likely not their ruler or the holder of the supreme power like Zeus and
   Jupiter.^[180][7]

   *Dyēus was associated with the bright and vast sky, but also to the
   cloudy weather in the Vedic and Greek formulas *Dyēus' rain.^[181][21]
   Although several [182]reflexes of Dyēus are storm deities, such as Zeus
   and Jupiter, this is thought to be a late development exclusive to
   Mediterranean traditions, probably derived from syncretism with
   [183]Canaanite deities and the Proto-Indo-European god
   [184]*Perkʷūnos.^[185][22]

   Due to his celestial nature, *Dyēus is often described as "all-seeing"
   or "with wide vision" in Indo-European myths. It is unlikely however
   that he was in charge of the supervision of justice and righteousness,
   as it was the case for Zeus or the [186]Indo-Iranian
   [187]Mithra–[188]Varuna duo, but he was suited to serve at least as a
   witness to oaths and treaties.^[189][23] Proto-Indo-Europeans also
   visualized the sun as the "lamp of Dyēus" or the "eye of Dyēus", as
   seen in various reflexes: "the god's lamp" in [190]Euripides' Medea,
   "heaven's candle" in [191]Beowulf, "the land of Hatti's torch" (the
   Sun-goddess of [192]Arinna) in a Hittite prayer,^[193][24] [194]Helios
   as the eye of Zeus,^[195][25]^[196][26] [197]Hvare-khshaeta as the eye
   of [198]Ahura Mazda, and the sun as "God's eye" in [199]Romanian
   folklore.^[200][27]

Consort

   [[201]edit]

   *Dyēus is often paired with [202]*Dʰéǵʰōm, the Earth goddess, and
   described as uniting with her to ensure the growth and sustenance of
   terrestrial life; the earth becomes pregnant as the rain falls from the
   sky.^[203][28]^[204][20] The relationship between Father Sky (*Dyēus
   Ph₂tḗr) and Mother Earth (*Dʰéǵʰōm Méh₂tēr) is also of contrast: the
   latter is portrayed as the vast and dark dwelling of mortals, located
   below the bright seat of the gods.^[205][29] According to Jackson
   however, as the thunder-god is frequently associated with the
   fructifying rains, she may be a more fitting partner of [206]*Perkʷūnos
   than of *Dyēus.^[207][30]

   While Hausos and the Divine Twins are generally considered the
   offsprings of *Dyēus alone,^[208][31] some scholars have proposed a
   spouse-goddess reconstructed as *Diwōnā or
   *Diuōneh₂,^[209][32]^[210][33] with a possible descendant in Zeus's
   consort [211]Dione. A thematic echo occurs in the Vedic tradition as
   Indra's wife [212]Indrānī displays a similar jealous and quarrelsome
   disposition under provocation. A second descendant may be found in Dia,
   a mortal said to unite with Zeus in a Greek myth. After the mating of
   Dia's husband [213]Ixion with the phantom of [214]Hera, the spouse of
   Zeus, the story leads ultimately to the birth of the [215]Centaurs (who
   may be seen as reminiscent of the [216]Divine Twins, sons of
   *Dyēus).^[217][31] Another reflex may be found in the [218]Mycenaean
   Greek Diwia, possibly a feminine counterpart of Zeus attested in the
   second part of the 2nd millennium BC and which may have survived in the
   [219]Pamphylian dialect of Asia Minor.^[220][34]^[221][35]^[222][36]
   The reconstruction is however only based upon the Greek—and to a lesser
   extent the Vedic—tradition, and it remains therefore not
   secured.^[223][31]

   If the female goddesses [224]Hera, [225]Juno, [226]Frigg and
   [227]Shakti share a common association with marriage and fertility,
   [228]Mallory and [229]Adams note however that "these functions are much
   too generic to support the supposition of a distinct PIE 'consort
   goddess' and many of the 'consorts' probably represent assimilations of
   earlier goddesses who may have had nothing to do with
   marriage."^[230][37]

Evidence

   [[231]edit]
   [232][220px-Stater_Zeus_Lampsacus_CdM.jpg] [233]Laurel-wreathed head of
   [234]Zeus, c 360–340 BC.

   Cognates deriving either from the stem *dyeu- ("daylight, bright sky"),
   the epithet *Dyēus Ph[2]ter ("Father Sky"), the [235]vṛddhi-derivative
   *deiwós ("celestial", a "god"), the derivative *diwyós ("divine"), or
   the back-formation *deynos (a "day") are among the most widely attested
   in [236]Indo-European languages.^[237][2]^[238][3]

"Sky-Father" epithet

   [[343]edit]
   [344][220px-J%C3%BApiter_y_Tetis%2C_por_Dominique_IngresFXD.jpg] The
   Roman god [345]Jupiter (Iovis-pater), 1811.

   Ritual and formulaic expressions stemming from the form *Dyēus Ph[2]ter
   ("Father Dyēus") were inherited in the following liturgic and poetic
   traditions:
     * [346]PIE: *dyēus ph[2]tḗr, 'Father Sky' ([347]voc. *dyeu ph[2]ter,
       "O Father Sky"),^[348][2]^[349][3]^[350][72]
          + [351]Greek: Zeus Patēr (Ζεῦς πατήρ; voc. Ζεῦ
            πάτερ),^[352][4]^[353][72]
          + [354]Indo-Iranian: *Dyauš-pHtar,^[355][39]
               o [356]Vedic: Dyáuṣ-pitā́ (voc. Dyáuṣ-pitṛ́,
                 द्यौष्पितृ),^[357][4]^[358][72]
          + [359]Italic: *Djous-patēr > *Dijēs-patēr (voc.
            *Djow-patēr),^[360][38]
               o [361]Old Latin: Dies Pater,^[362][72]
                    # [363]Latin: Diespiter (from *Dijēs-patēr);
                      [364]Iūpiter (from *Djow-patēr), archaic Iovispater,
                      later Iuppiter,^[365][38]^[366][72]^[367][1]
               o [368]Oscan: Dípatír, [369]Umbrian: Iupater (dat. Iuve
                 patre),^[370][38] [371]South Picene: dipater (gen.
                 dipoteres),^[372][73]^[373][72]
          + [374]Illyrian: [375]Deipaturos, recorded by [376]Hesychius as
            Δειπάτυροϛ (Deipáturos), a god worshiped in
            [377]Tymphaea.^[378][1]^[379][4]

   Other reflexes are variants that have retained both linguistic
   descendants of the stem *dyeu- ("sky") alongside the original structure
   "Father God". Some traditions have replaced the epithet *ph[2]ter with
   the nursery word papa ("dad, daddy"):
     * [380]Luwian: Tātis tiwaz, "Daddy [381]Tiwaz", the
       Sun-god,^[382][74]
     * [383]Palaic: Tiyaz papaz, "Papa [384]Tiyaz", the Sun-god,^[385][17]
     * [386]Scythian: Papaios (Papa Zios), "father Zeus", the god of the
       Sky,^[387][17]
     * [388]Old Irish: in Dagdae Oll-athair, "Great Father the [389]Dagda"
       (from the [390]Proto-Celtic formula *sindos dago-dēwos ollo fātir,
       "Great Father the Good God").^[391][75]^[392][76]

   Other variants are less secured:
     * [393]Hittite: attas Isanus, "Father Sun-god"; the name of the
       sky-god was replaced with a [394]Hattic sun-god loan, but the
       original structure of the formula left intact,^[395][19]
     * [396]Latvian: Debess tēvs, "Father of Heaven",^[397][2]
     * [398]Old Norse: Óðinn Alföðr, "[399]Odin, All-Father" or "Odin
       Father of All",^[400][77]^[401][78]
     * [402]Russian: [403]Stribogŭ, "Father God",^[404][2]
     * [405]Albanian: Zot, "lord" or "God", epithet of [406]Zojz, the
       sky-father (generally thought to be derived from
       [407]Proto-Albanian *dźie̅u ̊ a(t)t-, "heavenly father";^[408][79]
       although the etymology *w(i)tš- pati-, "lord of the house", has
       also been proposed),^[409][80]
     * [410]Tokharian B: kauṃ-ñäkte, 'sun, sun-god'.^[411][72]

"Divine" derivations

                 (Δῖα < *Díw-ya), a goddess venerated in classical times
                 at [607]Phlius and [608]Sicyon, and possibly identified

Legacy

   [[639]edit]

   As the pantheons of the individual mythologies related to
   Proto-Indo-European religion evolved, attributes of *Dyēus seem to have
   been redistributed to other deities. In Greek and Roman mythology,
   *Dyēus was the chief god, while the etymological continuant of Dyēus
   became a very abstract god in [640]Vedic mythology, and his original
   prominence over other gods largely diluted.^[641][19]^[642][3]

In Albanian tradition

   [[643]edit]
   [644][220px-Mali_i_Tomorrit_nga_kalaja_e_Beratit.jpg] The cult
   practiced by the Albanians on [645]Mount Tomorr in central [646]Albania
   is considered as a continuation of the ancient sky-god worship.

   After the first access of the ancestors of the [647]Albanians to the
   Christian religion in antiquity, the presumable Albanian term for
   Sky-Father – [648]Zot – has been used for [649]God, [650]the Father and
   [651]the Son ([652]Christ).^[653][129] In [654]Albanian folk beliefs
   the peak of the highest mountains like [655]Tomorr in central
   [656]Albania has been associated with the sky-god
   [657]Zojz.^[658][66]^[659][130] The enduring sanctity of the mountain,
   the annual pilgrimage to its summit, and the solemn sacrifice of a
   white bull by the local people provide abundant evidence that the
   ancient cult of the sky-god on Mount Tomorr continues through the
   generations almost untouched by the course of political events and
   religious changes.^[660][131]

In Slavic tradition

   [[661]edit]

   At one point, [662]early Slavs, like some [663]Iranian peoples after
   the [664]Zoroastrian religious reformation, demonized the Slavic
   successor of *Dyēus (abandoning this word in the sense of "heaven" at
   the same time, keeping the word for day, however, and abandoning many
   of the names of the other Proto-Indo-European gods, replacing them with
   new Slavic or Iranian names), while not replacing it with any other
   specific god, as a result of cultural contacts with Iranian peoples in
   the first millennium BC. Hence, after the process of demonization by
   the Slavs, *Dyēus is considered to have originated two continuations:
   *divo ("strange, odd thing") and *divъ ("demon").^[665][132] The result
   of this demonization may be Pan-Slavic demons, e.g. [666]Polish and
   [667]Czech [668]dziwożona, or Div occurring in [669]The Tale of Igor's
   Campaign.^[670][133]^[671][134]

   According to some researchers, at least some of *Dyēus's traits could
   have been taken over by [672]Svarog ([673]Urbańczyk: Sun-[674]Dažbóg –
   heavenly fire, [675]Svarožič – earthly fire, Svarog – heaven,
   lightning).^[676][135]^[677][136] [678]Helmold recalls that the Slavs
   were also supposed to believe in a god in heaven, who only deals with
   heavenly matters and commands other gods.^[679][137]

In non-Indo-European traditions

   [[680]edit]

   Various loanwords of *deiwós were introduced in non-Indo-European
   languages, such as [681]Estonian taevas or [682]Finnish taivas ("sky"),
   borrowed from [683]Proto-Indo-Iranian into these Uralic
   languages.^[684][1]^[685][138]
