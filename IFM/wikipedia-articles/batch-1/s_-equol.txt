   Chemical compound

   Equol (4',7-isoflavandiol) is an [105]isoflavandiol^[106][1]
   [107]estrogen metabolized from [108]daidzein, a type of [109]isoflavone
   found in [110]soybeans and other plant sources, by bacterial flora in
   the [111]intestines.^[112][2]^[113][3] While endogenous estrogenic
   hormones such as [114]estradiol are [115]steroids, equol is a
   [116]nonsteroidal [117]estrogen. Only about 30–50% of people have
   intestinal bacteria that make equol.^[118][4]

History

   [[119]edit]

   (S)-Equol was first isolated from horse urine in 1932,^[120][5] and the
   name was suggested by this equine connection.^[121][6] Since then,
   equol has been found in the urine or plasma of many other animal
   species, although these animals have significant differences in their
   ability to metabolize daidzein into equol.^[122][6] In 1980, scientists
   reported the discovery of equol in humans.^[123][7] The ability of
   (S)-equol to play a role in the treatment of [124]estrogen- or
   [125]androgen-mediated diseases or disorders was first proposed in
   1984.^[126][8]

Chemical structure

   [[127]edit]

   Equol is a compound that can exist in two mirror-image forms known as
   [128]enantiomers: (S)-equol and (R)-equol. (S)-equol is produced in
   humans and animals with the ability to metabolize the soy isoflavone
   daidzein, while (R)-equol can be chemically synthesized.^[129][9] The
   molecular and physical structure of (S)-equol is similar to that of the
   hormone [130]estradiol.^[131][10] (S)-Equol preferentially binds
   [132]estrogen receptor beta.^[133][2]^[134][11]

Pharmacology

   [[135]edit]

Estrogen receptor binding

   [[136]edit]

   (S)-equol is a [137]nonsteroidal, selective [138]agonist of [139]ERβ
   (K[i] = 16 nM) with 13-fold selectivity for ERβ over [140]ERα.^[141][3]
   Relative to (S)-equol, (R)-equol is less potent and binds to ERα (K[i]
   = 50 nM) with 3.5-fold selectivity over ERβ.^[142][3] (S)-Equol has
   about 2% of [143]estradiol's binding affinity for human [144]estrogen
   receptor alpha (ERα) and 20% of estradiol's binding affinity for human
   [145]estrogen receptor beta (ERβ). The preferential binding of
   (S)-equol to ERβ vs. ERα and in comparison to that of estradiol
   suggests the molecule may share some of the characteristics of a
   [146]selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM).^[147][12] Equol has
   been found to act as an [148]agonist of the [149]GPER
   (GPR30).^[150][13]

Pharmacokinetics

   [[151]edit]

   (S)-Equol is a very stable molecule that essentially remains unchanged
   when digested, and this lack of further metabolism explains its very
   quick [152]absorption and high [153]bioavailability.^[154][14] When
   (S)-equol is consumed, it is rapidly absorbed and achieves a T[max]
   (rate of [155]peak plasma concentration) in two to three hours. In
   comparison, the T[max] of daidzein is 4 to 10 hours because daidzein
   exists in [156]glycoside (with a glucose side chain) form. The body
   must convert daidzein to its [157]aglycone form (without the glucose
   side chain) via removal of the sugar side chain during digestion before
   it can use daidzein. If consumed directly in aglycone form, daidzein
   has a T[max] of one to three hours.^[158][15] The percent fractional
   elimination of (S)-equol in urine after oral administration is high and
   in some adults can reach close to 100 percent. The percent fractional
   elimination of daidzein is much lower at 30 to 40 percent.^[159][16]

Production in humans

   [[160]edit]

   To produce (S)-equol after soy consumption, humans must have certain
   strains of bacteria living within their intestines.^[161][8] Twenty-one
   different strains of [162]intestinal bacteria cultured from humans have
   been shown to have the ability to transform daidzein into (S)-equol or
   a related intermediate compound.^[163][6] Several studies indicate that
   only 25 to 30 percent of the adult populations of Western countries
   produce (S)-equol after eating soy foods containing
   isoflavones,^[164][10]^[165][17]^[166][18]^[167][19] while 50 to 60
   percent of adults from Japan, Korea, and China are
   equol-producers.^[168][20]^[169][21]^[170][22]^[171][23] Vegetarians
   have also been reported to be more capable of transforming
   [172]daidzein into (S)-equol.^[173][24] [174]Seaweed and dairy
   consumption can enhance the production of equol.^[175][10]^[176][25]
   The ability of a person to produce (S)-equol is determined by testing
   people who have not taken any [177]antibiotics for at least a month.
   For this standardized test, the individual drinks two 240 milliliter
   glasses of soy milk or eats a soy food equivalent for three days. The
   (S)-equol concentration in each test subject's urine is determined on
   day four.^[178][26]

Equol producing bacteria

   [[179]edit]

   While many more bacteria are involved in the related intermediate
   process of equol production, such as conversion of daidzin to daidzein,
   or [180]genistein to 5-Hydroxy-equol, the bacteria that achieve the
   complete conversion of daidzein to (S)-equol,^[181][27]
   include:^[182][28]
     * [183]Adlercreutzia equolifaciens
     * [184]Asaccharobacter celatus AHU1763
     * [185]Bacteroides ovatus
     * [186]Bifidobacterium
     * [187]Bifidobacterium animalis
     * [188]Coriobacteriaceae sp MT1B9
     * [189]Eggerthella sp YY7918
     * [190]Enterococcus faecium
     * [191]Eubacterium sp D1 and D2
     * [192]Finegoldia magna
     * [193]Lactobacillus mucosae
     * [194]Lactobacillus sp Niu-O16
     * [195]Lactococcus garvieae (Lc 20-92)
     * [196]Ruminococcus productus
     * [197]Slackia sp HE8
     * [198]Slackia equolifaciens (Strain DZE)
     * [199]Streptococcus intermedius
     * [200]Veillonella sp

   Conversion by Bifidobacterium has only been reported once by Tsangalis
   et al. in 2002^[201][29] and not reproduced since.[202]Bifidobacteria:
   Genomics and Molecular Aspects Mixed cultures such as
   [203]Lactobacillus sp. Niu-O16 and [204]Eggerthella sp. Julong 732 can
   also produce (S)-equol.[205]Bifidobacteria: Genomics and Molecular
   Aspects Some equol producing bacteria, as implied by their
   nomenclature, are [206]Adlercreutzia equolifaciens, [207]Slackia
   equolifaciens and [208]Slackia isoflavoniconvertens.

Health effects

   [[209]edit]

Skin health

   [[210]edit]

   The topical effects of equol as an [211]anti-aging substance have been
   shown in different studies. The effects result from both molecular and
   structural changes to the skin. Equol can, for instance, lead to an
   increase in [212]telomere length. As an antioxidant, equol can decrease
   the aging process by reducing damage caused by [213]reactive oxygen
   species (ROS). It may also act as a protective anti-photoaging
   substance by inhibiting acute UVA- induced lipid
   [214]peroxidation.^[215][30] In addition, equol may have a positive
   impact on [216]epigenetic regulation.^[217][31] Equol's phytoestrogenic
   properties may also affect skin health.^[218][32] Reduction of dark
   circles and eye wrinkles after treatment with equol has been
   reported.^[219][33] Equol may also protect skin from damage by
   pollution due to its [220]anti-oxidative and [221]anti-inflammatory
   properties.^[222][34]

   Each of the enantiomers and the [223]racemic mixture of both
   enantiomers have different characteristics, bioavailabilities and
   molecular effects.^[224][35] According to one study, (RS)-equol
   provided the greatest overall improvement in skin health, especially
   when applied topically.^[225][35]

Hormone-related health effects

   [[226]edit]

   Beyond topical effects, medicinal equol has been shown to relieve
   [227]menopausal symptoms such as hot flashes and muscle and joint
   pain.^[228][36]^[229][37] (RS)-equol was also reported to reduce
   symptoms associated with menopausal [230]vaginal atrophy, such as
   vaginal itching, vaginal dryness and pain with intercourse and cause
   positive shifts in the vaginal bacterial population, cell composition,
   and pH.^[231][38]

   According to animal studies,^[232][39] it has anti-androgenic effects
   and may lead to inhibited [233]5-alpha reductase as well as lowered
   [234]dihydrotestosterone (DHT) levels.
