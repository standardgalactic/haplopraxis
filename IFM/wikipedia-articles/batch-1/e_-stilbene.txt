   Chemical compound

   (E)-Stilbene, commonly known as trans-stilbene, is an [122]organic
   compound represented by the [123]condensed structural formula
   C[6]H[5]CH=CHC[6]H[5]. Classified as a [124]diarylethene, it features a
   central [125]ethylene [126]moiety with one [127]phenyl group
   [128]substituent on each end of the carbon–carbon [129]double bond. It
   has an [130](E) stereochemistry, meaning that the phenyl groups are
   located on opposite sides of the double bond, the opposite of its
   [131]geometric isomer, [132]cis-stilbene. Trans-stilbene occurs as a
   white crystalline solid at room temperature and is highly soluble in
   organic solvents. It can be converted to cis-stilbene
   [133]photochemically, and further reacted to produce [134]phenanthrene.

   Stilbene was discovered in 1843 by the French chemist [135]Auguste
   Laurent.^[136][2] The name "stilbene" is derived from the [137]Greek
   word στίλβω (stilbo), which means "I shine", on account of the lustrous
   appearance of the compound.^[138][3]

Isomers

   [[139]edit]

   [140][400px-Cis-trans-stilbene.svg.png] The isomerization of stilbene
   under the influence of radiation.

   Stilbene exists as two possible [141]stereoisomers. One is
   trans-1,2-diphenylethylene, called (E)-stilbene or [142]trans-stilbene.
   The second is cis-1,2-diphenylethylene, called [143](Z)-stilbene or
   [144]cis-stilbene, and is [145]sterically hindered and less stable
   because the steric interactions force the aromatic rings out-of-plane
   and prevent [146]conjugation.^[147][4] Cis-stilbene is a liquid at room
   temperature (melting point: 5–6 °C (41–43 °F)), while trans-stilbene is
   a crystalline solid which does not melt until around 125 °C (257 °F),
   illustrating the two isomers have significantly different physical
   properties.^[148][5]^[149][6]

   Both isomers of stilbene can be produced by decarboxylation of
   [154]α-phenylcinnamic acid, trans-stilbene being produced from the
   (Z)-isomer of the acid.^[155][5]

   [156]Richard F. Heck^[157][7] and Tsutomu Mizoroki^[158][8]
   independently reported the synthesis of trans-stilbene by coupling of
   [159]iodobenzene and [160]styrene using a palladium(II) catalyst, in
   what is now known as the [161]Mizoroki-Heck
   reaction.^[162][9]^[163][10] The Mizoroki approach produced the higher
   yield.

          [164][500px-Mizoroki_1971_iodobenzene_styrene.svg.png]

   Stilbene undergoes reactions typical of alkenes. Trans-stilbene
   undergoes epoxidation with [165]peroxymonophosphoric acid, H[3]PO[5],
   producing a 74% yield of trans-stilbene oxide in
   [166]dioxane.^[167][11] The [168]epoxide product formed is a
   [169]racemic mixture of the two [170]enantiomers of
   1,2-diphenyl[171]oxirane. The [172]achiral [173]meso compound
   (1R,2S)-1,2-diphenyloxirane arises from cis-stilbene, though peroxide
   epoxidations of the cis-isomer produce both cis- and trans-epoxide
   products. For example, using [174]tert-butyl hydroperoxide, oxidation
   of cis-stilbene produces 0.8% cis-stilbene oxide, 13.5% trans-stilbene
   oxide, and 6.1% [175]benzaldehyde.^[176][12]^[177][13] Enantiopure
   stilbene oxide has been prepared by Nobel laureate [178]Karl Barry
   Sharpless.^[179][14]

          [180][500px-Peroxomonophosphoric_acid_reaction01.svg.png]

   Stilbene can be cleanly oxidised to benzaldehyde by
   [181]ozonolysis^[182][15] or [183]Lemieux–Johnson oxidation, and
   stronger oxidants such as acidified [184]potassium permanganate will
   produce [185]benzoic acid. [186]Vicinal [187]diols can be produced via
   the [188]Upjohn dihydroxylation or [189]enantioselectively using
   [190]Sharpless asymmetric dihydroxylation^[191][16]^[192][17] with
   [193]enantiomeric excesses as high as
   100%.^[194][18]^[195][19]^[196][20]

   Bromination of trans-stilbene produces predominantly
   meso-1,2-dibromo-1,2-diphenylethane (sometimes called
   [197]meso-stilbene dibromide), in line with a mechanism involving a
   cyclic [198]bromonium ion intermediate of a typical electrophilic
   [199]bromine addition reaction;^[200][21] cis-stilbene yields a
   [201]racemic mixture of the two [202]enantiomers of
   1,2-dibromo-1,2-diphenylethane in a non-polar solvent such as
   [203]carbon tetrachloride, but the extent of production of the
   [204]meso compound increases with solvent polarity, with a yield of 90%
   in [205]nitromethane.^[206][22] The formation of small quantities of
   the two enantiomers of stilbene dibromide from the trans-isomer
   suggests that the bromonium ion intermediate exists in [207]chemical
   equilibrium with a carbocation intermediate PhCHBr–C^+(H)Ph with a
   vacant [208]p orbital vulnerable to nucleophilic attack from either
   face.^[209][21] The addition of bromide or tribromide salts restores
   much of the stereospecificity even in solvents with a [210]dielectric
   constant above 35.^[211][23]

   Upon UV irradiation it converts to cis-stilbene, a classic example of a
   [212]photochemical reaction involving [213]trans-cis isomerization, and
   can undergo further reaction to form [214]phenanthrene.^[215][24]

          [216][800px-Stilbene_isomerization2.png]

Derivatives and uses

   [[217]edit]

Synthetic

   [[218]edit]

   (E)-Stilbene itself is of little value, but it is a precursor to other
   derivatives used as [219]dyes, [220]optical brighteners,
   [221]phosphors, and [222]scintillators.^[223][25] Stilbene is one of
   the [224]gain mediums used in [225]dye lasers.^[226][26]
   [227][220px-Diaminostilbene_disulfonic_acid.svg.png]
   [228]4,4′-diamino-2,2′-stilbenedisulfonic acid is a popular
   [229]optical brightener used in some laundry
   detergents.[230][220px-Diethylstilbestrol.svg.png]
   [231]Diethylstilbestrol exhibits estrogenic properties, even though it
   is not a steroid.

   [232]Disodium 4,4'-dinitrostilbene-2,2'-disulfonate is prepared by the
   [233]sulfonation of [234]4-nitrotoluene to form
   4-nitrotoluene-2-sulfonic acid, which can then be oxidatively coupled
   using [235]sodium hypochlorite to form the (E)-stilbene
   [236]derivative^[237][27] in a process originally developed by
   [238]Arthur George Green and André Wahl in the late nineteenth
   century.^[239][28]^[240][29] Improvements to the process with higher
   yields have been developed, using air oxidation in liquid
   ammonia.^[241][30] The product is useful as its reaction with
   [242]aniline derivatives results in the formation of [243]azo dyes.
   Commercially important dyes derived from this compound include Direct
   Red 76, Direct Brown 78, and Direct Orange 40.^[244][26]

Natural stilbenes

   [[245]edit]

   The [246]stilbenoids are naturally occurring stilbene derivatives.
   Examples include [247]resveratrol and its cousin, [248]pterostilbene.
   The [249]stilbestrols, which are structurally but not synthetically
   related to (E)-stilbene, exhibit [250]estrogenic activity. Members of
   this group include [251]diethylstilbestrol, [252]fosfestrol, and
   [253]dienestrol. Some such derivative are produced by [254]condensation
   of coenzyme A derivatives of [255]cinnamic acid or
   [256]4-hydroxycinnamic acid and the malonic acid.
