   47171 Lempo, or as a binary (47171) Lempo–Hiisi ([158]provisional
   designation 1999 TC[36]), is a [159]trans-Neptunian object and
   [160]trinary system from the [161]Kuiper belt, located in the outermost
   regions of the [162]Solar System. It was discovered on 1 October 1999,
   by American astronomers [163]Eric Rubenstein and [164]Louis-Gregory
   Strolger during an observing run at [165]Kitt Peak National Observatory
   in [166]Arizona, United States.^[167][2]^[168][11] Rubenstein was
   searching images taken by Strolger as part of their Nearby Galaxies
   Supernova Search project. It is classified as a [169]plutino with a 2:3
   [170]mean-motion resonance with [171]Neptune and is among the brighter
   TNOs. It reached [172]perihelion in July 2015. This minor planet was
   named after [173]Lempo from Finnish mythology.^[174][2]

   The trinary system's other two components, Paha [175]/ˈpɑːhɑː/ and
   Hiisi [176]/ˈhiːsi/, were discovered in 2001 and 2007, respectively,
   and later named after Lempo's two demon cohorts, Paha and
   [177]Hiisi.^[178][12]

History

   [[179]edit]

Discovery

   [[180]edit]
   [181][220px-Baboquivari%26KittPeakNatObs.jpg] Lempo was discovered with
   the 0.9-meter WIYN telescope (left) at Kitt Peak

   The Lempo system was discovered on 1 October 1999 by American
   astronomers [182]Eric Rubenstein and [183]Louis-Gregory Strolger during
   an observing run for their Nearby Galaxies Supernova Search (NGSS)
   project at the [184]Kitt Peak National Observatory in [185]Arizona.
   Initiated in 1998 as part of Strolger's [186]doctoral thesis, the NGSS
   project was a three-year-long [187]CCD-based survey of [188]galaxies
   along the [189]celestial equator to search for nearby,
   low-[190]redshift [191]supernovae. The Kitt Peak Observatory's WIYN
   0.9-meter telescope was used for wide-field imaging of this region,
   which coincided with the [192]ecliptic plane where [193]Kuiper belt
   objects (KBOs) including Lempo were likely to appear.^[194][13]
   Rubenstein identified Lempo as a relatively bright, slow-moving object
   in the [195]constellation [196]Cetus on images taken by Strolger on 1
   October 1999.^[197][11]^[198][a] At an [199]apparent magnitude of 20,
   its exceptional brightness for a suspected KBO warranted follow-up
   observations to confirm the object.^[200][13]^[201][14]

   Lempo was observed by Rubenstein and Strolger for three consecutive
   days after its discovery. The object was also found in images taken by
   Strolger on 30 September 1999, one day prior to its discovery. The
   discovery was then announced by the [202]Minor Planet Center on 21
   December 1999 and the object was given the [203]provisional designation
   1999 TC[36].^[204][11] The provisional designation indicates that Lempo
   was the 903rd [205]minor planet discovered in the first half of October
   1999.^[206][b] By 2002, additional observations have extended Lempo's
   [207]observation arc to over two years, sufficient to determine an
   accurate orbit.^[208][2] Lempo was consequently given the permanent
   [209]minor planet number 47171 by the Minor Planet Center on 21
   September 2002.^[210][16] As of 2021^[211][update], more than 500 total
   observations of Lempo over an observation arc of over 46 years have
   been documented.^[212][2] The earliest known [213]precovery
   observations of Lempo have been found in [214]photographic plates of
   the [215]Siding Spring Observatory's [216]Digitized Sky Survey from
   June 1974 and May and September 1976.^[217][17]

Name

   [[218]edit]

   The largest primary component of the triple system is named after
   [219]Lempo from [220]Finnish mythology.^[221][c] Originally worshiped
   as the god of love and fertility, he was later depicted as a [222]devil
   after [223]Christianity came to Finland. Lempo brought down the hero
   [224]Väinämöinen with the help of his two demon cohorts [225]Hiisi and
   [226]Paha, whose names denominate the smaller inner and outer
   components, respectively.^[227][2] The names were chosen on behalf of
   astronomer Bryan J. Holler.^[228][14] The official naming citation was
   published by the Minor Planet Center on 5 October 2017.^[229][12]

Trinary system

   [[230]edit]
   Size comparison of the Lempo system's components
   Orbit diagram of the Lempo triple system

Lempo

   [[231]edit]

   Lempo is a hierarchical [232]triple (or trinary) system consisting of a
   central primary, which is itself a [233]binary system of two
   similarly-sized components (Lempo and Hiisi), and a small
   [234]satellite on a wide and [235]eccentric [236]circumbinary orbit
   (Paha). The structure of the hierarchy is discerned by denoting the
   apparent Lempo–Hiisi primary with the letter A and the smaller, outer
   companion Paha with the letter B; the individual primary components
   Lempo and Hiisi are distinguished as A1 and A2, respectively.^[237][4]
   The three components ordered from largest to smallest are Lempo, Hiisi,
   and Paha.^[238][18]

   Assuming spherical shapes with a uniform [239]bulk density for all
   components, the system mass estimated based on the motion of Paha is
   (12.75±0.06)×10^18 kg.^[240][4]^[241][6] The orbital motion of the
   Lempo–Hiisi components gives somewhat a higher estimated mass of
   (14.20±0.05)×10^18 kg. This discrepancy is probably related to
   unaccounted gravitational interactions of the components in a complex
   triple system.^[242][4]

   Lempo is one of the only three known trans-Neptunian multiple systems
   with more than two components; the other two are the [243]dwarf planets
   [244]Pluto and [245]Haumea.^[246][4] The binary [247]Kuiper belt object
   [248]385446 Manwë is suspected to have once been a hierarchical triple
   system similar to Lempo, but the orbit of its inner binary evolved by
   tides and became a [249]contact binary.^[250][19]

Hiisi

   [[251]edit]
   [252][220px-Lempo_Hiisi.png] Hubble images of the Lempo–Hiisi system,
   with the two components marked "1" and
   "2"[253][220px-TNO_close_binaries_comparison.svg.png] Comparison of
   mean separation distances and diameters of trans-Neptunian close
   binaries, including Lempo–Hiisi

   Hiisi, officially designated (47171) Lempo II Hiisi,^[254][20] is the
   inner, second-largest component of the Lempo triple system, discovered
   last among the three. Together with the primary component Lempo, it
   forms the central binary Lempo–Hiisi which the outer component Paha
   revolves around. The existence of a third, inner component (or second
   companion) in the Lempo system was first hypothesized in 2006 by John
   Stansberry and collaborators, who noted that the primary seemed to have
   an unusually low density.^[255][21] Further evidence to the existence
   of an inner component was posited by Seth Jacobson and [256]Jean-Luc
   Margot in October 2007, who noticed a distinct elongation of the
   primary in Hubble images.^[257][22] The binarity of the Lempo primary
   was eventually confirmed in a more extensive analysis of Hubble images
   by Susan Benecchi, Keith Noll, Will Grundy and Hal Levison in
   2009.^[258][4]^[259][2]

   Due to complex discovery circumstances involving different independent
   groups of researchers,^[260][23] Hiisi did not have a formal
   provisional designation signifying the year of its first observation or
   discovery.^[261][20] Instead, it was unofficially designated "component
   A2" in scientific literature for being the smaller component of the
   central Lempo–Hiisi binary.^[262][4] It eventually received its
   permanent [263]satellite designation and name while the larger, first
   component A1 maintained the name Lempo on 5 October 2017.^[264][12]

   The separation between the two components is only about half the
   [265]diffraction limit of Hubble, making it impossible to fully resolve
   the system. Instead, it appears elongated in Hubble images, revealing
   its binary nature.^[266][4] This central pair has a semi-major axis of
   around 867 km and a period of about 1.9 days.^[267][4] Assuming equal
   [268]albedos of about 0.079, Lempo and Hiisi are approximately 272+17
   −19 km and 251+16
   −17 km in diameter, respectively.^[269][5] Assuming a uniform density
   for all components, the mass of Hiisi itself 5.273×10^18 kg.^[270][6]

Paha

   [[271]edit]
   [272][220px-47171-Lempo-Hubble.gif] The Lempo system imaged by Hubble
   from 2003–2006

   Paha, officially designated (47171) Lempo I Paha,^[273][2] is the
   smaller, outer component of the Lempo triple system. It was discovered
   on 8 December 2001 by astronomers [274]Chadwick Trujillo and
   [275]Michael Brown using the [276]Hubble Space Telescope's [277]Space
   Telescope Imaging Spectrograph to survey for binary trans-Neptunian
   objects.^[278][24] The discovery was reported in an [279]IAU Circular
   notice published by the [280]International Astronomical Union 10
   January 2002.^[281][25] The confirmation of Paha in archival 4 October
   2001 observations from the [282]Lick Observatory's [283]Shane telescope
   [284]adaptive optics system was reported in a follow-up IAU Circular
   published on 24 January 2002.^[285][26]^[286][27]

   Paha previously had the temporary [287]provisional designation S/2001
   (1999 TC[36]) 1 before it was changed to S/2001 (47171) 1 after Lempo
   was numbered.^[288][28] Being the smaller, outer component on a
   circumbinary orbit around the central Lempo–Hiisi binary, it was
   sometimes designated "component B" in scientific literature.^[289][4]
   It received its permanent [290]satellite designation and name alongside
   Lempo and Hiisi on 5 October 2017.^[291][12]

   In unfiltered visual wavelengths, Paha appears 2.2 magnitudes dimmer
   than the primary on average, corresponding to an individual
   [292]apparent magnitude of 22.6.^[293][29] The satellite has an
   estimated diameter of 132+8
   −9 km^[294][5] and a [295]semi-major axis of 7411±12 km, orbiting its
   primary in 50.302±0.001 d.^[296][4] It is estimated to only have a mass
   of about 7.67×10^17 kg.^[297][6]

System dynamics

   [[298]edit]

   The orbital dynamics of the Lempo system are highly complex and could
   not be modelled with solely [299]Keplerian dynamics.^[300][4]^[301][6]
   Many crucial parameters such as initial spin states and shapes of the
   individual components are unknown and thus could not adequately model
   the dynamics of the Lempo system as a [302]three-body problem without
   leading to significantly [303]chaotic behavior. In a 2018 dynamical
   study, Alexandre Correia found that simulated models using
   realistically assumed spin states and shapes failed to explain the
   presently eccentric mutual orbit of the inner Lempo–Hiisi binary, even
   with the inclusion of eccentricity-damping [304]tidal forces. Correia
   concluded that the present orbits, spin states and shapes of all
   components of the Lempo system needed to be remeasured to a greater
   precision before a more sophisticated model could be
   developed.^[305][6]

Origin

   [[306]edit]

   There exist two main [307]hypotheses on how this triple system formed.
   The first one is a giant collision and subsequent reaccretion in the
   [308]disc. The second one is gravitational capture of a third object by
   a preexisting binary. The similar sizes of Lempo and Hiisi favor the
   latter hypothesis.^[309][4]

Physical characteristics

   [[310]edit]

   The combined observations by the infrared [311]Spitzer Space
   Telescope,^[312][21] [313]Herschel Space Telescope^[314][5] and the
   [315]Hubble Space Telescope (HST) make it possible to estimate the
   sizes of the system's components and consequently provide the range of
   possible values for the objects' bulk [316]density.^[317][4] The
   single-body diameter (effective system size) of Lempo is currently
   estimated at 393.1+25.2
   −26.8 km.^[318][5]

   The very low estimated density of 0.3–0.8 g/cm^3 obtained in 2006 (when
   the system was thought to be a binary) would require an unusually high
   [319]porosity of 50–75%, assuming an equal mixture of rock and
   ice.^[320][21] The direct measurement of visible fluxes of all three
   components of the system in 2009 by the HST has resulted in an improved
   average density of 0.532+0.317
   −0.211 g/cm^3 confirming the earlier conclusion that the object is
   probably a [321]rubble pile.^[322][4] The density was revised up to
   0.64+0.15
   −0.11 g/cm^3 in 2012 when new information from the Herschel became
   available. For a bulk density in the range 1–2 g/cm^3 the porosity is
   in the range 36–68%, again confirming that the object is a rubble
   pile.^[323][5]

   Lempo has a very red [324]spectral slope in [325]visible
   light^[326][30] and a flat spectrum in [327]near infrared. There is
   also a weak absorption feature near the wavelength of 2 [328]μm,
   probably caused by water [329]ice. The best model reproducing the near
   infrared spectrum includes [330]tholins, crystalline water ice, and
   [331]serpentine as surface materials. These results are for the
   integrated spectrum of all three components of the system.^[332][31]

Exploration

   [[333]edit]

   The Lempo system has been considered for future exploration due to its
   unusual configuration.^[334][32] Lempo was suggested as a target for
   [335]New Horizons 2, a proposed twin of its namesake that would fly by
   Jupiter, Uranus, and up to four KBOs.^[336][33]

Notes

   [[342]edit]
    1. [343]^ The [344]celestial coordinates of Lempo at the time of
       discovery were 23^h 57^m 49.00^s −09° 31′ 52.1″.^[345][11] See
       [346]Cetus for constellation coordinates.
    2. [347]^ In the convention for minor planet provisional designations,
       the first letter represents the half-month of the year of discovery
       while the second letter and numbers indicate the order of discovery
       within that half-month. In the case for 1999 TC[36], the first
       letter 'T' corresponds to the first half-month of October 1999
       while the succeeding letter 'C' indicates that it is the 3rd object
       discovered on the 37th cycle of discoveries (with 36 cycles
       completed). Each completed cycle consists of 25 letters
       representing discoveries, therefore 3 + (36 completed cycles ×
       25 letters) = 903.^[348][15]
    3. [349]^ The primary's namesake is also used to refer to the entire
       triple system.^[350][6]
