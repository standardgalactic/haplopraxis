   Gaussia ([180]minor planet designation: 1001 Gaussia), provisional
   designation 1923 OA, is a dark background [181]asteroid from the outer
   regions of the [182]asteroid belt, approximately 73 kilometers in
   diameter. It was discovered on 8 August 1923, by Soviet astronomer
   [183]Sergey Belyavsky at the [184]Simeiz Observatory on the Crimean
   peninsula.^[185][15] The asteroid was named after German mathematician
   [186]Carl Friedrich Gauss.^[187][3] Gauss computed the orbit of Ceres,
   and 1001 Gaussia was named along with [188]1000 Piazzia, and [189]1002
   Olbersia in part for their work on [190]Ceres, with names for
   [191]Giuseppe Piazzi, who found Ceres, and [192]Heinrich Wilhelm
   Matthias Olbers, who recovered it later that year.^[193][16]

Orbit and classification

   [[194]edit]

   Gaussia is a background asteroid that does not belong to any known
   [195]asteroid family. It orbits the Sun in the [196]outer main-belt at
   a distance of 2.8–3.6 [197]AU once every 5 years and 9 months (2,100
   days). Its orbit has an [198]eccentricity of 0.13 and an
   [199]inclination of 9[200]° with respect to the [201]ecliptic.^[202][1]

   The asteroid was first identified as A907 XC at Taunton Observatory
   ([203]803) in December 1907. The body's [204]observation arc begins at
   [205]UNSO in January 1908, more than 15 years prior to its official
   discovery observation at Simeiz.^[206][15]

Physical characteristics

   [[207]edit]

   In the [208]Tholen classification, Gaussia has an ambiguous
   [209]spectral type. Its type is closest to the primitive [210]P-type
   asteroids, followed by the common carbonaceous [211]C-type
   asteroids.^[212][1]^[213][4]

Rotation period

   [[214]edit]

   In November 2005, a rotational [215]lightcurve of Gaussia was obtained
   from photometric observations. Lightcurve analysis gave a well-defined
   [216]rotation period of 20.99 hours with a brightness amplitude of 0.11
   [217]magnitude ([218]U=3).^[219][a]

   Lower-rated lightcurves with a divergent period of 4.08 and 9.17 hours
   were previously obtained in 2005 and 2009, respectively
   ([220]U=1/2-).^[221][12]^[222][13]

Diameter and albedo

   [[223]edit]

   According to the surveys carried out by the Infrared Astronomical
   Satellite [224]IRAS, the Japanese [225]Akari satellite and the
   [226]NEOWISE mission of NASA's [227]Wide-field Infrared Survey
   Explorer, Gaussia measures between 67.80 and 80.07 kilometers in
   diameter and its surface has an [228]albedo between 0.036 and
   0.05.^[229][5]^[230][6]^[231][7]^[232][8]^[233][9]^[234][10]^[235][11]

   The Collaborative Asteroid Lightcurve Link derives an albedo of 0.0417
   and a diameter of 74.71 kilometers based on an [236]absolute magnitude
   of 9.7.^[237][4]

Naming

   [[238]edit]

   This [239]minor planet was named by Swedish astronomer Bror Ansgar
   Asplind after [240]Carl Friedrich Gauss (1777–1855), German
   mathematician and director of the Göttingen Observatory ([241]528), who
   also rediscovered [242]Ceres using a new orbital computing method by
   [243]Franz Xaver von Zach.^[244][3]

   The official naming citation was mentioned in [245]The Names of the
   Minor Planets by [246]Paul Herget in 1955 ([247]H 96). He is also
   honored by the lunar crater [248]Gauss.^[249][3]

   1001 Gaussia was named as part of trio honoring the events surrounding
   the discovery of Ceres in 1801.^[250][17] Carl Friedrich Gauss who
   computed the orbit of Ceres was for 1001 Gaussia, [251]1000 Piazzia for
   [252]Giuseppe Piazzi and [253]1002 Olbersia for [254]Heinrich Wilhelm
   Matthias Olbers.^[255][17] Olbers recovered Ceres after it has passed
   behind the Sun and returned.^[256][17] In the next few years only three
   more astronomical bodies were found between Mars and Jupiter,
   [257]Pallas, [258]Juno, and [259]4 Vesta, however it would be 37 years
   before another asteroid was found, [260]5 Astraea in 1845.^[261][17]
