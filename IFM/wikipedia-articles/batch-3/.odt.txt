   The Open Document Format for Office Applications (ODF), also known as
   OpenDocument, standardized as ISO 26300, is an [166]open file format
   for [167]word processing documents, [168]spreadsheets,
   [169]presentations and graphics and using [170]ZIP-compressed^[171][6]
   [172]XML files. It was developed with the aim of providing an open,
   XML-based file format specification for office applications.^[173][7]

   The standard is developed and maintained by a technical committee in
   the [174]Organization for the Advancement of Structured Information
   Standards (OASIS) consortium.^[175][8] It was based on the [176]Sun
   Microsystems specification for [177]OpenOffice.org XML, the default
   format for [178]OpenOffice.org and [179]LibreOffice. It was originally
   developed for [180]StarOffice "to provide an open standard for office
   documents."^[181][9]

   In addition to being an OASIS standard, it is published as an
   [182]ISO/[183]IEC international standard ISO/IEC 26300 – Open Document
   Format for Office Applications
   (OpenDocument).^[184][2]^[185][3]^[186][4]^[187][5]^[188][10]^[189][11]
   From March 2024, the current version is 1.4.^[190][12]

Specifications

   [[191]edit]

   Main article: [192]OpenDocument technical specification

   The most common [193]filename extensions used for OpenDocument
   documents are:^[194][13]^[195][14]
     * .odt and .fodt for [196]word processing (text) documents
     * .ods and .fods for [197]spreadsheets
     * .odp and .fodp for [198]presentations
     * .odg and .fodg for [199]graphics
     * .odf for [200]formula, mathematical equations

   The original OpenDocument format consists of an [201]XML document that
   has <document> as its [202]root element. OpenDocument files can also
   take the format of a [203]ZIP compressed archive containing a number of
   files and directories; these can contain binary content and benefit
   from ZIP's [204]lossless compression to reduce file size. OpenDocument
   benefits from [205]separation of concerns by separating the content,
   styles, metadata, and application settings into four separate XML
   files.

   There is a comprehensive set of example documents in OpenDocument
   format available.^[206][15] The whole test suite is available under the
   [207]Creative Commons Attribution 2.5 license.

History

   [[208]edit]

   Main article: [209]OpenDocument standardization

Conception

   [[210]edit]

   The OpenDocument standard was developed by a Technical Committee (TC)
   under the [211]Organization for the Advancement of Structured
   Information Standards (OASIS) industry consortium. The ODF-TC has
   members from a diverse set of companies and individuals. Active TC
   members have voting rights. Members associated with Sun and IBM have
   sometimes had a large voting influence.^[212][16] The standardization
   process involved the developers of many office suites or related
   document systems.

   The first official ODF-TC meeting to discuss the standard was 16
   December 2002. OASIS approved OpenDocument as an OASIS standard on 1
   May 2005. OASIS submitted the ODF specification to [213]ISO/IEC Joint
   Technical Committee 1 (JTC 1) on 16 November 2005, under Publicly
   Available Specification (PAS) rules. ISO/IEC standardization for an
   open document standard including text, spreadsheet and presentation was
   proposed for the first time in [214]DKUUG 28 August 2001.^[215][17]

   After a six-month review period, on 3 May 2006, OpenDocument
   unanimously passed its six-month DIS (Draft International Standard)
   ballot in [216]JTC 1 ([217]ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 34), with broad
   participation,^[218][18] after which the OpenDocument specification was
   "approved for release as an ISO and IEC International Standard" under
   the name ISO/IEC 26300:2006.^[219][19]

   After responding to all written ballot comments, and a 30-day default
   ballot, the OpenDocument [220]international standard went to
   publication in ISO, officially published 30 November 2006.

   In 2006,^[221][20] Garry Edwards, a member of OASIS TC since
   2002,^[222][21] along with Sam Hiser, and Paul "Marbux" E.
   Merrell^[223][22] founded the OpenDocument Foundation. The aim of this
   project was to be open-source representative of the format in
   OASIS.^[224][20]^[225][22] The immediate aim of this project was to
   develop software that would convert legacy [226]Microsoft Office
   documents to ODF. By October 2007 the project was a failure: Conversion
   of Microsoft Office documents could not be achieved.^[227][20] By this
   time, The foundation was convinced that ODF was not moving in a
   direction that they supported.^[228][23] As a result, it announced the
   decision to abandon its namesake format in favor of [229]W3C's
   [230]Compound Document Format (CDF), which was in early stages of its
   development.^[231][23]^[232][20] The foundation, however, never acted
   on this decision and was soon dissolved. The CDF was never designed for
   this purpose either.^[233][24]

Further standardization

   [[234]edit]

   Main article: [235]OpenDocument standardization

   Further standardization work with OpenDocument includes:
     * The [236]OASIS Committee Specification [237]OpenDocument 1.0
       (second edition) corresponds to the published ISO/IEC 26300:2006
       standard. The content of ISO/IEC 26300 and OASIS OpenDocument v1.0
       2nd ed. is identical.^[238][5] It includes the editorial changes
       made to address JTC1 ballot comments. It is available in ODF, HTML
       and PDF formats.
     * [239]OpenDocument 1.1 includes additional features to address
       accessibility concerns.^[240][25] It was approved as an OASIS
       Standard on 2007-02-01 following a call for vote issued on
       2007-01-16.^[241][26] The public announcement was made on
       2007-02-13.^[242][27] This version was not initially submitted to
       ISO/IEC, because it is considered to be a minor update to ODF 1.0
       only, and OASIS were working already on ODF 1.2 at the time ODF 1.1
       was approved.^[243][28] However it was later submitted to ISO/IEC
       and published in March 2012 as "ISO/IEC 26300:2006/Amd 1:2012 –
       Open Document Format for Office Applications (OpenDocument)
       v1.1".^[244][10]^[245][11]
     * [246]OpenDocument 1.2 includes additional accessibility features,
       [247]RDF-based metadata,^[248][29] a spreadsheet formula
       specification based on [249]OpenFormula,^[250][29] support for
       [251]digital signatures and some features suggested by the public.
       It consists of three parts: Part 1: OpenDocument Schema, Part 2:
       Recalculated Formula (OpenFormula) Format and Part 3: Packages.
       Version 1.2 of the specification was approved as an OASIS Standard
       on 29 September 2011.^[252][30] It was submitted to the relevant
       ISO committee under the Publicly Available Specification (PAS)
       procedure in March 2014.^[253][31] In October 2014, it was
       unanimously approved as a Draft International Standard. Some
       comments were raised in the process that needed to be addressed
       before OpenDocument 1.2 could proceed to become an International
       Standard.^[254][32] OpenDocument 1.2 was published as ISO/IEC
       standard on 17 June 2015.^[255][2]^[256][3]^[257][4]
     * [258]OpenDocument 1.3 includes additional features for digital
       signatures, encryption, change-tracking and inter-operability.
       Version 1.3 of the OpenDocument specification was approved as an
       OASIS Standard April 2021. The specification was completed as the
       result of the COSM crowdfunding project seeded by [259]The Document
       Foundation.^[260][33]

Application support

   [[261]edit]

Software

   [[262]edit]

   Main article: [263]OpenDocument software

   The OpenDocument format is used in [264]free software and in
   [265]proprietary software. This includes [266]office suites (both
   stand-alone and web-based) and individual applications such as
   word-processors, spreadsheets, presentation, and data management
   applications. Prominent text editors, word processors and office suites
   supporting OpenDocument fully or partially include:

     * [267]AbiWord^[268][34]^[269][35]
     * [270]Adobe Buzzword^[271][36]
     * [272]Apache OpenOffice
     * [273]Bean (software)
     * [274]Calibre [275]ebook viewer, converter, editor, and manager
     * [276]Calligra Suite^[277][37]
     * [278]Collabora Office and [279]Collabora Online^[280][38]
     * [281]Corel [282]WordPerfect Office X6^[283][39]
     * [284]Dropbox
     * [285]Evince
     * [286]Gnumeric^[287][40]
     * [288]Google Docs
     * [289]IBM Lotus Symphony^[290][29]^[291][41]^[292][42]
     * [293]Inkscape exports .odg
     * [294]KOffice^[295][43]
     * [296]LibreOffice
     * [297]Microsoft Office 2003 and [298]Office XP (with the Open Source
       OpenXML/ODF Translator Add-in for Office)^[299][44]
     * [300]Microsoft Office 2007 (with Service Pack 2 or 3) supports ODF
       1.1^[301][45] (Windows only)
     * [302]Microsoft Office 2010 supports ODF 1.1 (Windows only)
     * [303]Microsoft Office 2013 supports ODF 1.2 (Windows only)
     * [304]Microsoft Office 2016 and [305]2019 support ODF 1.2 (Windows:
       read/write; OS X: read-only after online conversion^[306][46])
     * [307]Microsoft Office 2021 supports ODF 1.3^[308][47] (Windows and
       MacOS)
     * [309]Microsoft OneDrive / [310]Office Web Apps^[311][48]
     * [312]NeoOffice
     * [313]Okular
     * [314]ONLYOFFICE
     * [315]OpenOffice.org
     * [316]Quarto and R Markdown can export to .odt^[317][49]
     * [318]Scribus imports .odt and .odg
     * [319]SoftMaker Office
     * [320]Sun Microsystems [321]StarOffice
     * [322]TextEdit
     * [323]WordPad (Windows 7 and later, Windows Server 2008 R2 and
       later)^[324][50] supports ODF 1.1^[325][51]
     * [326]Zoho Office Suite^[327][29]

   Various organizations have announced development of conversion software
   (including plugins and filters) to support OpenDocument on
   [328]Microsoft's products.^[329][52]^[330][53] As of July
   2007^[331][update], there are nine packages of conversion software.
   Microsoft first released support for the OpenDocument Format in Office
   2007 SP2.^[332][54] However, the implementation faced [333]substantial
   criticism and the [334]ODF Alliance and others claimed that the third
   party plugins provided better support.^[335][55] Microsoft Office 2010
   can open and save OpenDocument Format documents natively, although not
   all features are supported.^[336][56] In July 2024, Microsoft announced
   support for ODF 1.4 (prior to it being released) in Microsoft 365 apps,
   starting with version 2404 on Windows and 16.84 on macOS.^[337][57]

   Starting with [338]Mac OS X 10.5, the [339]TextEdit application and
   [340]Quick Look preview feature support the OpenDocument Text format.

Licensing

   [[343]edit]

Public access to the standard

   [[344]edit]

   Versions of the OpenDocument Format approved by [345]OASIS are
   available for free download and use.^[346][58] The [347]ITTF has added
   ISO/IEC 26300 to its "list of freely available standards"; anyone may
   download and use this standard free-of-charge under the terms of a
   click-through license.^[348][59]

Additional royalty-free licensing

   [[349]edit]

   Obligated members^[350][60] of the OASIS ODF TC have agreed to make
   deliverables available to implementors under the OASIS Royalty Free
   with Limited Terms [351]policy.

   Key contributor [352]Sun Microsystems made an irrevocable intellectual
   property covenant, providing all implementers with the guarantee that
   Sun will not seek to enforce any of its enforceable U.S. or foreign
   patents against any implementation of the OpenDocument specification in
   which development Sun participates to the point of incurring an
   obligation.^[353][61]

   A second contributor to ODF development, [354]IBM – which, for
   instance, has contributed Lotus spreadsheet documentation^[355][62] –
   has made their patent rights available through their Interoperability
   Specifications Pledge in which "IBM irrevocably covenants to you that
   it will not assert any Necessary Claims against you for your making,
   using, importing, selling, or offering for sale Covered
   Implementations."^[356][63]

   The [357]Software Freedom Law Center has [358]examined whether there
   are any legal barriers to the use of the OpenDocument Format (ODF) in
   free and open source software arising from the standardization process.
   In their opinion ODF is free of legal encumbrances that would prevent
   its use in free and open source software, as distributed under licenses
   authored by Apache and the FSF.^[[359]citation needed]

Response

   [[360]edit]

Support for OpenDocument

   [[361]edit]

   Several governments, companies, organizations and software products
   support the OpenDocument format. For example:
     * The OpenDoc Society runs frequent [362]ODF
       Plugfests^[[363]definition needed] in association with industry
       groups and Public Sector organisations. The 10th Plugfest^[364][64]
       was hosted by the UK [365]Government Digital Service in conjunction
       with industry associations including the [366]OpenForum Europe and
       [367]OpenUK (formerly Open Source Consortium).
          + An output of the 10th Plugfest was an ODF toolkit^[368][65]
            which includes "Open Document Format principles for Government
            Technology" that has the purpose of simply explaining the case
            for ODF directed at the "average civil servant" and includes
            an extract from the UK Government policy relating to Open
            Document Format.
          + The toolkit also includes a single page graphical
            image^[369][66] designed to articulate the consequences of not
            choosing Open Document Format. The illustration has now been
            translated into more than 10 languages.
     * Information technology companies like [370]Apple Inc., [371]Adobe
       Systems, [372]Google, [373]IBM, [374]Intel, [375]Microsoft,
       [376]Nokia, [377]Novell, [378]Red Hat, [379]Oracle as well as other
       companies who may or may not be working inside the OASIS
       OpenDocument Adoption Technical Committee.
     * Over 600 companies and organizations promote OpenDocument format
       through The [380]OpenDocument Format Alliance.^[381][67]
     * [382]NATO with its 26 members uses ODF as a mandatory standard for
       all members.^[383][68]
     * The TAC (Telematics between Administrations Committee), composed of
       e-government policy-makers from the 25 [384]European Union Member
       States, endorsed a set of recommendations for promoting the use of
       open document formats in the public sector.^[385][69]
     * The free office suites [386]Apache OpenOffice, [387]Calligra,
       [388]KOffice, [389]NeoOffice and [390]LibreOffice all use
       OpenDocument as their default file format.
     * Several organisations, such as the [391]OpenDocument Fellowship and
       [392]OpenDoc Society [393]Archived 19 November 2021 at the
       [394]Wayback Machine were founded to support and promote
       OpenDocument.
     * The UK government has adopted ODF as the standard for all documents
       in the UK civil service^[395][70]
     * The Russian government has recommended adopting ODF as the standard
       in the public sector as by GOST R ISO/MEK 26300-2010
     * The [396]Wikimedia Foundation supports ODF export from
       [397]MediaWiki, which powers [398]Wikipedia and a number of other
       Internet [399]wiki-based sites.^[400][71]
     * The default text processing applications in Windows 10
       ([401]WordPad) and Mac OS 10.9 ([402]TextEdit) support OpenDocument
       Text.

   On 4 November 2005, IBM and Sun Microsystems convened the "OpenDocument
   (ODF) Summit" in [403]Armonk, New York, to discuss how to boost
   OpenDocument adoption. The ODF Summit brought together representatives
   from several industry groups and technology companies, including
   Oracle, Google, Adobe, Novell, Red Hat, Computer Associates, Corel,
   Nokia, Intel, and Linux e-mail company Scalix (LaMonica, 10 November
   2005). The providers committed resources to technically improve
   OpenDocument through existing standards bodies and to promote its usage
   in the marketplace, possibly through a stand-alone
   foundation.^[404][72] Scholars have suggested that the "OpenDocument
   standard is the wedge that can hold open the door for competition,
   particularly with regard to the specific concerns of the public
   sector."^[405][73] Indeed, adoption by the public sector has risen
   considerably since the promulgation of the OpenDocument format
   initiated the 2005/2006 time period.^[406][73]
     * Different applications using ODF as a standard document format have
       different methods of providing macro/scripting capabilities. There
       is no [407]macro language specified in ODF. Users and developers
       differ on whether inclusion of a standard scripting language would
       be desirable.^[408][74]
     * The ODF specification for tracked changes is limited and does not
       fully specify all cases, resulting in implementation-specific
       behaviors.^[409][75] In addition, OpenDocument does not support
       change tracking in elements like tables or [410]MathML.^[411][76]
     * It is not permitted to use generic ODF formatting style elements
       (like font information) for the MathML elements.^[412][76]

Adoption

   [[413]edit]

   Main article: [414]OpenDocument adoption

   One objective of open formats like OpenDocument is to guarantee
   long-term access to data without legal or technical barriers, and some
   governments have come to view open formats as a public policy issue.
   Several governments around the world have introduced policies of
   partial or complete adoption.^[415][73] What this means varies from
   case to case; in some cases, it means that the ODF standard has a
   national standard identifier; in some cases, it means that the ODF
   standard is permitted to be used where national regulation says that
   non-proprietary formats must be used, and in still other cases, it
   means that some government body has actually decided that ODF will be
   used in some specific context. The following is an incomplete list:

Notes

   [[483]edit]

    1. [484]^ The standard that was proclaimed to be national is in fact
       the following: «ISO/IEC 26300:2006 "Information technology - Open
       Document Format for Office Applications (OpenDocument) v1.0"».
       Designation as the national standard only means that it's
       encouraged to be used voluntarily and is not a legally bounding.
       [485]Eurasian Economic Union is the territory where the standard is
       assumed to be applied.
