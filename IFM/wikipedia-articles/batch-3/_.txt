   /
   Slash or solidus

                     ⁄              ∕                ／
               Fraction slash Division slash Fullwidth solidus

   The slash is a slanting line [149]punctuation mark /. It is also known
   as a stroke, a solidus, a forward slash and [150]several other
   historical or technical names. Once used to mark [151]periods and
   [152]commas, the slash is now used to represent [153]division and
   [154]fractions, [155]exclusive 'or' and [156]inclusive 'or', and as a
   [157]date separator.

   A slash in the reverse direction \ is known as a [158]backslash.

History

   [[159]edit]

   Slashes may be found in early writing as a variant form of [160]dashes,
   [161]vertical strokes, etc. The present use of a slash distinguished
   from such other marks derives from the [162]medieval European
   [163]virgule ([164]Latin: virgula, lit. "twig"), which was used as a
   [165]period, [166]scratch comma, and [167]caesura mark.^[168][1] (The
   first sense was eventually lost to the [169]low dot and the other two
   developed separately into the [170]comma , and [171]caesura mark ||)
   Its use as a comma became especially widespread in [172]France, where
   it was also used to mark the continuation of a word onto the next line
   of a page, a sense later taken on by the [173]hyphen -.^[174][2] The
   [175]Fraktur script used throughout [176]Central Europe in the
   [177]early modern period used a single slash as a scratch comma and a
   double slash // as a dash. The double slash developed into the
   [178]double oblique hyphen ⸗ and [179]double hyphen ⹀ or ゠ before being
   usually simplified into [180]various single dashes.

   In the 18th century, the mark was generally known in English as the
   "[181]oblique".^[182][3] but particularly the less vertical
   [183]fraction slash.^[184][4] The variant "oblique stroke" was
   increasingly shortened to "[185]stroke", which became the common
   British name for the character, although printers and publishing
   professionals often instead referred to it as an "oblique". In the 19th
   and early 20th century, it was also widely known as the "[186]shilling
   mark" or "[187]solidus", from its use as a notation or abbreviation for
   the [188]shilling.^[189][5]^[190][6] The name "slash" is a recent
   development, not appearing in [191]Webster's Dictionary until the Third
   Edition (1961)^[192][7]^[193][a] but has gained wide currency through
   its use in [194]computing, a context where it is sometimes used in
   British English in preference to "stroke". Clarifying terms such as
   "forward slash" have been coined owing to widespread use of
   [195]Microsoft's [196]DOS and [197]Windows [198]operating systems,
   which use the [199]backslash extensively.^[200][9]^[201][10]

Usage

   [[202]edit]

Disjunction and conjunction

   [[203]edit]

Connecting alternatives

   [[204]edit]
   See also: [205]Gender neutrality in languages with grammatical gender

   The slash is commonly used in many languages as a shorter substitute
   for the [206]conjunction "or", typically with the sense of
   [207]exclusive or (e.g., Y/N permits yes or no but not both).^[208][11]
   Its use in this sense is somewhat informal,^[209][12] although it is
   used in [210]philology to note variants (e.g., virgula/uirgula) and
   [211]etymologies (e.g., [212]F. virgule/[213]LL. virgula/[214]L.
   virga/[215]PIE. *wirgā).^[216][2]

   Such slashes may be used to avoid taking a position in [217]naming
   disputes. One example is the [218]Assyrian naming dispute, which
   prompted the [219]US and [220]Swedish censuses to use the respective
   official designations "[221]Assyrian/Chaldean/Syriac" and
   "[222]Assyrier/Syrianer" for the ethnic group.

   In particular, since the late 20th century, the slash is used to permit
   more [223]gender-neutral language in place of the traditional
   [224]masculine or [225]plural gender neutrals. In the case of
   [226]English, this is usually restricted to [227]degendered pronouns
   such as "he/she" or "s/he". Most other [228]Indo-European languages
   include more far-reaching use of [229]grammatical gender. In these, the
   separate gendered [230]desinences (grammatical suffices) of the words
   may be given divided by slashes or set off with [231]parentheses. For
   example, in [232]Spanish, hijo is a son and a hija is a daughter; some
   proponents of gender-neutral language advocate the use of hijo/a or
   hijo(a) when writing for a general audience or addressing a listener of
   unknown gender.^[233][13]^[234][14]^[235][15] Less commonly, [236]at
   sign ⟨@⟩ is used instead: hij@. Similarly, in [237]German and some
   Scandinavian and Baltic languages, Sekretär refers to any secretary and
   Sekretärin to an explicitly female secretary; some advocates of gender
   neutrality support forms such as Sekretär/-in for general use. This
   does not always work smoothly, however: problems arise in the case of
   words like Arzt ('doctor') where the explicitly female form Ärztin is
   [238]umlauted and words like Chinese ('Chinese person') where the
   explicitly female form Chinesin loses the terminal -e.

Connecting non-contrasting items

   [[239]edit]

   The slash is also used as a shorter substitute for the conjunction
   "and" or [240]inclusive or (i.e., A or B or both),^[241][12] typically
   in situations where it fills the role of a hyphen or [242]en dash. For
   example, the "Hemingway/Faulkner generation" might be used to discuss
   the era of the [243]Lost Generation inclusive of the people around and
   affected by both [244]Hemingway and [245]Faulkner. This use is
   sometimes proscribed, as by [246]New Hart's Rules, the style guide for
   the [247]Oxford University Press.^[248][11]

Presenting routes

   [[249]edit]

   The slash, as a form of inclusive or, is also used to punctuate the
   stages of a route (e.g.,
   [250]Shanghai/[251]Nanjing/[252]Wuhan/[253]Chongqing as stops on a tour
   of the [254]Yangtze).^[255][2]

Introducing topic shifts

   [[256]edit]

   The word slash is also developing as a way to introduce topic shifts or
   follow-up statements. Slash can introduce a follow-up statement, such
   as, "I really love that hot dog place on Liberty Street. Slash can we
   go there tomorrow?" It can also indicate a shift to an unrelated topic,
   as in "JUST SAW ALEX! Slash I just chubbed on oatmeal raisin cookies at
   north quad and i miss you." The new usage of "slash" appears most
   frequently in spoken conversation, though it can also appear in
   writing.^[257][16]

In speech

   [[258]edit]

   Sometimes the word slash is used in speech as a [259]conjunction to
   represent the written role of the character (as if a written slash were
   being read aloud from text), e.g. "bee slash mosquito protection" for a
   beekeeper's net hood,^[260][17] and "There's a little bit of nectar
   slash honey over here, but really it's not a lot." (said by a beekeeper
   examining in a beehive),^[261][18] and "[262]Gastornis slash
   [263]Diatryma" for two supposed genera of prehistoric birds which are
   now thought to be one genus.^[264][19]

Mathematics

   [[265]edit]

Fractions

   [[266]edit]
   See also: [267]Unicode subscripts and superscripts § Fraction slash

   The fraction slash ⟨ ⁄⟩ is used between two numbers to indicate a
   [268]fraction or [269]ratio. Such formatting developed as a way to
   write the horizontal [270]fraction bar on a single line of text. It is
   first attested in [271]England and [272]Mexico in the 18th
   century.^[273][20] This notation is known as an online,
   solidus,^[274][21] or shilling fraction.^[275][21] Nowadays fractions,
   unlike inline division, are often given using smaller numbers,
   [276]superscript, and [277]subscript (e.g., ^23⁄[43]). This notation is
   responsible for the current form of the [278]percent ⟨%⟩, [279]permille
   ⟨‰⟩, and [280]permyriad ⟨‱⟩ signs, developed from the horizontal form
   ⁠0/0⁠ which represented an early modern corruption of an Italian
   abbreviation of per cento.^[281][22]

   Many fonts draw the fraction slash (and the division slash) less
   vertical than the slash. The separate encoding is also intended to
   permit automatic formatting of the preceding and succeeding digits by
   glyph substitution with numerator and denominator glyphs (e.g., display
   of "1, fraction slash, 2" as "½"),^[282][23] though this is not yet
   supported in many environments or fonts. Because of this lack of
   support, some authors still use [283]Unicode subscripts and
   superscripts to compose fractions, and many fonts design these
   characters for this purpose. In addition, all of the multiples less
   than 1 of ^1⁄[n] for 2 ≤ n ≤ 6 and n = 8 (e.g. ^2⁄[3] and ^5⁄[8]), as
   well as ^1⁄[7], ^1⁄[9], and ^1⁄[10], are in the Unicode [284]Number
   Forms or [285]Latin-1 Supplement block as [286]precomposed
   characters.^[287][24]

   This notation can also be used when the concept of fractions is
   extended from numbers to arbitrary rings by the method of
   [288]localization of a ring.

Division

   [[289]edit]

   The division slash ⟨ ∕⟩, equivalent to the [290]division sign ⟨ ÷⟩, may
   be used between two numbers to indicate [291]division. For example,
   23 ÷ 43 can also be written as 23 ∕ 43. This use developed from the
   [292]fraction slash in the late 18th or early 19th century.^[293][20]
   The formatting was advocated by [294]De Morgan in the mid-19th
   century.^[295][25]^[[296]full citation needed]

Computing

   [[317]edit]

   The slash, sometimes distinguished as "forward slash", is used in
   [318]computing in a number of ways, primarily as a separator among
   levels in a given hierarchy, for example in the path of a filesystem.

File paths

   [[319]edit]

   The slash is used as the [320]path component separator in many
   [321]computer operating systems (e.g., Unix's
   pictures/image.png). In [322]Unix and [323]Unix-like systems, such as
   [324]macOS and [325]Linux, the slash is also used for the [326]volume
   [327]root directory (e.g., the initial slash in
   /usr/john/pictures). Confusion of the slash with the backslash ⟨\⟩
   largely arises from the use of the latter as the path component
   separator in the widely used MS-DOS and [328]Microsoft Windows
   systems.^[329][9]^[330][10]

Networking

   [[331]edit]

   The slash is used in a similar fashion in internet [332]URLs (e.g.,

   http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slash_(punctuation)).^[333][11] Often this
   portion of such URLs corresponds with files on a Unix [334]server with
   the same name, and this is where this convention for [335]internet URLs
   comes from.

   The slash in an [336]IP address (e.g.,
   192.0.2.0/29) indicates the prefix size in [337]CIDR notation. The
   number of addresses of a [338]subnet may be calculated as 2^address
   size − prefix size, in which the address size is 128 for [339]IPv6 and
   32 for [340]IPv4. For example, in IPv4, the prefix size/29 gives:
   2^32–29 = 2^3 = 8 addresses.

Programming

   [[341]edit]

   The slash is used as a [342]division operator in most [343]programming
   languages while [344]APL uses it for reduction ([345]fold) and
   compression ([346]filter). The double slash is used by [347]Rexx as a
   [348]modulo operator, and [349]Python (starting in version 2.2) uses a
   double slash for division which rounds (using [350]floor) to an
   integer. In [351]Raku the double slash is used as a "defined-or"
   alternative to ||. A dot and slash ⟨./⟩ is used in [352]MATLAB and
   [353]GNU Octave to indicate an element-by-element division of matrices.

   [354]Comments that begin with
   /* (a slash and an asterisk) and end with */ were introduced in
   [355]PL/I and subsequently adopted by [356]SAS, [357]C, Rexx, [358]C++,
   [359]Java, [360]JavaScript, [361]PHP, [362]CSS, and [363]C#. A double
   slash // is also used by [364]C99, C++, C#, PHP, Java, [365]Swift, and
   JavaScript to start a single line comment.

   In [366]SGML and derived languages such as [367]HTML and [368]XML, a
   slash is used in closing tags. For example, in HTML,
   <b> begins a section of bold text and </b> closes it. In XHTML, slashes
   are also necessary for "self-closing" elements such as the [369]newline
   command <br /> where HTML has simply <br>.

   In a style originating in the [370]Digital Equipment Corporation line
   of operating systems ([371]OS/8, [372]RT-11, [373]TOPS-10, et cetera),
   [374]Windows, [375]DOS, some [376]CP/M programs, [377]OpenVMS, and
   [378]OS/2 all use the slash to indicate [379]command-line options. For
   example, the command
   dir/w is understood as using the command [380]dir ("directory") with
   the "wide" option. No space is required between the command and the
   switch; this was the reason for the choice to use backslashes as the
   path separator since one would otherwise be unable to run a program in
   a different directory.

   Slashes are used as the standard delimiters for [381]regular
   expressions, although other characters can be used instead.

   [382]IBM JCL uses a double slash to start each line in a batch job
   stream except for /* and /&.

Programs

   [[383]edit]

   [384]IRC and many in-game chat clients use the slash to mark commands,
   such as joining and leaving a chat room or sending private messages.
   For example, in IRC,
   /join #services is a command to join the [385]channel "services" and
   /me is a command to format the following message as though it were an
   action instead of a spoken message. In [386]Minecraft's chat function,
   the slash is used for executing console and plugin commands. In
   [387]Second Life's chat function, the slash is used to select the
   "communications channel", allowing users to direct commands to virtual
   objects "listening" on different channels. For example, if a virtual
   house's lights were set to use channel 42, the command "/42 on" would
   turn them on. In [388]Discord, slash commands are used to send special
   messages and execute commands, like sending a [389]shrug emoji
   (¯\_(ツ)_/¯) or a table flip emoji ((╯°□°)╯︵ ┻━┻), or changing one's
   nickname using "/nick". Slash commands can also be used to use Discord
   bots.

   The [390]Gedcom standard for exchanging computerized genealogical data
   uses slashes to delimit surnames; an example would be Bill /Smith/ Jr.
   Slashes around surnames are also used in [391]Personal Ancestral File.

Electronics

   [[392]edit]

   A leading slash is one of several common conventions for indicating an
   [393]active-low digital signal, which performs the named function when
   at a low voltage level.^[394][26] For example, [395]dynamic
   random-access memory has active-low Chip Select, Row Address Strobe and
   Column Address Strobe signals, commonly written /CS, /RAS, and
   /CAS.^[396][27] This extends to signals which select between two
   options, such as "R/W", which indicates that the function is "read"
   when high and "write" when low. (Sometimes written as R/W for greater
   clarity.^[397][28]

Currency

   [[398]edit]

   Main article: [399]Shilling

   [400][220px-038_35_Kisoro%2C_2000_constructed_under_swTws_Project_%2879
   28190604%29.jpg] Sign in [401]Kisoro with prices in [402]Ugandan
   shillings; note the use of the '/=' notation.

   The slash (as the "shilling mark" or "solidus")^[403][29] was an
   abbreviation for the [404]shilling, a former [405]coin of the United
   Kingdom and [406]its former colonies. Before the [407]decimalisation of
   currency in Britain, its currency abbreviations (collectively [408]£sd)
   represented their [409]Latin names, derived from a [410]medieval French
   modification of the late [411]Roman libra, [412]solidus, and
   [413]denarius.^[414][30] Thus, one [415]penny less than two [416]pounds
   was written £1 19s 11d or £1 19ſ 11d. During the period when
   [417]English orthography included the [418]long s, ſ or ſ,
   (abbreviating shilling) the ſ came to be written as a single
   slash.^[419][31]^[420][32] The d. might be omitted, and "2ſ6" ("two
   shillings and sixpence") became simplified as 2/6.^[421][29] Amounts in
   full pounds, shillings and pence could be written in many different
   ways, for example: £1 9s 6d, £1.9.6, £1-9-6, and even £1/9/6d (with a
   slash used also to separate pounds and shillings).^[422][33] The same
   style was also used under the [423]British Raj and early independent
   India for the predecimalization [424]rupee/[425]anna/[426]pie
   system.^[427][34]

   In five East African countries ([428]Kenya, [429]Tanzania, [430]Uganda,
   [431]Somalia, and the de facto country of [432]Somaliland), where the
   national currencies are denominated in shillings, the [433]decimal
   separator is a slash mark (e.g., 2/50). Where the minor unit is zero,
   an [434]equals sign is used (e.g., 5/=).

Dates

   [[435]edit]

   Slashes are a common [436]calendar date separator^[437][11] used
   [438]across many countries and by some standards such as the
   [439]Common Log Format used by web servers. Depending on context, it
   may be in the form Day/Month/Year, Month/Day/Year, or Year/Month/Day.
   If only two elements are present, they typically denote a day and month
   in some order. For example, [440]9/11 is a common American way of
   writing the date 11 September; Britons write this as 11/9. Owing to the
   ambiguity across cultures, the practice of using only two elements to
   denote a date is sometimes proscribed.^[441][35]

   Because of the world's many varying [442]conventional date and time
   formats, [443]ISO 8601 advocates the use of a Year-Month-Day system
   separated by hyphens (e.g., [444]Victory in Europe Day occurred on
   1945-05-08). In the ISO 8601 system, slashes represent date ranges:
   "1939/1945" represents what is more commonly written in [445]Anglophone
   countries as "1939–1945". The autumn term of a northern-hemisphere
   school year might be marked "2010-09-01/12-22".

   In English, a range marked by a slash often has a separate meaning from
   one marked by a dash or hyphen.^[446][11] "24/25 December" would mark
   the time shared by both days (i.e., the night from [447]Christmas Eve
   to [448]Christmas morning) rather than the time made up by both days
   together, which would be written "24–25 December". Similarly, a
   historical reference to "1066/67" might imply an event occurred during
   the winter of late 1066 and early 1067,^[449][36] whereas a reference
   to 1066–67 would cover the entirety of both years. The usage was
   particularly common in British English during [450]World War II, where
   such slash dates were used for [451]night-bombing [452]air raids. It is
   also used by some police forces in the United States.

Numbering

   [[453]edit]

   The slash is used in numbering to note totals. For example, "page
   17/35" indicates that the relevant passage is on the 17th page of a
   35-page document. Similarly, the marking "#333/500" on a product
   indicates it is the 333rd out of 500 identical products or out of a
   batch of 500 such products. For scores on schoolwork, in games, and so
   on, "85/100" indicates 85 points were attained out of a possible 100.

   Slashes are also sometimes used to mark ranges in numbers that already
   include hyphens or dashes. One example is the [454]ISO treatment of
   dating. Another is the [455]US Air Force's treatment of aircraft serial
   numbers, which are normally written to note the fiscal year and
   aircraft number. For example, "85-1000" notes the thousandth aircraft
   ordered in fiscal year 1985. To indicate the next fifty subsequent
   aircraft, a slash is used in place of a hyphen or dash: "85-1001/1050".

Linguistic transcription

   [[456]edit]

   Main article: [457]International Phonetic Alphabet § Brackets and
   transcription delimiters

   A pair of slashes (as "[458]slants") are used in the [459]transcription
   of [460]speech to enclose [461]pronunciations (i.e., [462]phonetic
   transcriptions). For example, the [463]IPA transcription of the English
   pronunciation of "solidus" is written /ˈsɒlɪdəs/.^[464][6] Properly,
   slashes mark [465]broad or phonemic transcriptions, whereas narrow,
   [466]allophonic transcriptions are enclosed by [467]square brackets.
   For example, the word little may be broadly rendered as /ˈlɪtəl/ but a
   careful transcription of the [468]velarization of the second L would be
   written [ˈlɪɾɫ̩].

   In [469]sociolinguistics, a double or triple slash may also be used in
   the transcription of a [470]traditional sociolinguistic interview or in
   other type of linguistic elicitation to represent simultaneous speech,
   interruptions, and certain types of [471]speech disfluencies.

   Single and double slashes are often used as typographic substitutes for
   the [472]click letters [473]ǀ, [474]ǁ.

   A [475]diaphonemic transcription may be marked in several ways, e.g.
   with a pair of slash marks (⫽◌⫽).

Poetry

   [[476]edit]

   The slash is used in various [477]scansion notations for representing
   the metrical pattern of a line of verse, typically to indicate a
   stressed syllable.^[[478]citation needed]

Line breaks

   [[479]edit]

   The slash (as a "virgule") offset by spaces to either side is used to
   mark [480]line breaks when transcribing text from a multi-line format
   into a single-line one.^[481][11]^[482][37] It is particularly common
   in quoting [483]poetry, [484]song lyrics, and [485]dramatic scripts,
   formats where omitting the line breaks risks losing meaningful context.
   For example, here is a part of [486]Hamlet's [487]soliloquy:

   [488]To be, or not to be, that is the question:
   Whether 'tis Nobler in the mind to suffer
   The Slings and Arrows of outrageous Fortune,
   Or to take Arms against a Sea of troubles,
   And by opposing end them...

   — [489]Hamlet, Act II, Scene ii^[490][38]

   ^[[491]full citation needed]

   If someone wanted to quote the above soliloquy in a prose paragraph, it
   is standard to mark the line breaks as follows: "To be, or not to be,
   that is the question: / Whether 'tis nobler in the mind to suffer / The
   slings and arrows of outrageous [492]Fortune, / Or to take arms against
   a sea of troubles, / And by opposing end them..." Less often, virgules
   are used in marking [493]paragraph breaks when quoting a [494]prose
   passage. Some style guides, such as [495]New Hart's, prefer to use a
   pipe | in place of the slash to mark these line and paragraph
   breaks.^[496][11]

   The virgule may be thinner than a standard slash when typeset. In
   computing contexts, it may be necessary to use a [497]non-breaking
   space before the virgule to prevent it from being [498]widowed on the
   next line.

Abbreviation

   [[499]edit]

   The slash has become standard in several abbreviations. Generally, it
   is used to mark two-letter [500]initialisms such as A/C (short for "air
   conditioner"), w/o ("without"), b/w ("black and white" or, less often,
   "between"), w/e ("whatever" or, less often, "weekend" or "week
   ending"), i/o ("[501]input/output"), r/w ("read/write"), and n/a ("not
   applicable"). Other initialisms employing the slash include w/ ("with")
   and w/r/t ("with regard to"). Such slashed abbreviations are somewhat
   more common in British English and were more common around the
   [502]Second World War (as with "S/E" to mean "single-engined"). The
   abbreviation 24/7 (denoting 24 hours a day, 7 days a week) describes a
   business that is always open or unceasing activity.^[503][11]

   The slash in [504]derived units such as m/s (meters per second) is not
   an abbreviation slash, but a straight division. It is however in that
   position read as 'per' rather than e.g. 'over', which can be seen as
   analogous to units whose symbols are pure abbreviations such as mph
   (miles per hour), although in abbreviations 'per' is 'p' or dropped
   entirely (psi, pounds per square inch) rather than a slash.

   In the [505]US government, the names of offices within various
   departments are abbreviated using slashes, starting with the larger
   office and following with its subdivisions. For example, the
   [506]Federal Aviation Administration's [507]Office of Commercial Space
   Transportation is formally abbreviated FAA/AST.

Proofreading

   [[508]edit]

   The slash or vertical bar (as a "[509]separatrix") is used in
   [510]proofreading to mark the end of [511]margin notes^[512][b] or to
   separate margin notes from one another. The slash is also sometimes
   used in various proofreading [513]initialisms, such as l/c and u/c for
   changes to [514]lower and [515]upper case, respectively.

Typist

   [[516]edit]

   In formal business correspondence, when a letter is typed by someone
   other than the person responsible for its contents, it is standard to
   add a suffix with the initials of the author (in upper-case), and
   typist (in lower-case) after the signature block, separated by a slash.
   For example, a letter typed by D.E. at the direction of A.B.C. would
   include the line "ABC/de".^[517][40]

Fiction

   [[518]edit]

   The slash is used in [519]fan fiction to mark the [520]romantic pairing
   a piece will focus upon (e.g., a K/S denoted a [521]Star Trek story
   would focus on a sexual relationship between [522]Kirk and [523]Spock),
   a usage which developed in the 1970s from the earlier friendship
   pairings marked by [524]ampersands (e.g., K&S). The genre as a whole is
   now known as [525]slash fiction. Because it is more generally
   associated with [526]homosexual male relationships, lesbian slash
   fiction is sometimes distinguished as [527]femslash. In situations
   where other pairings occur, the genres may be distinguished as m/m,
   f/f, and so on.

Libraries

   [[528]edit]

   The slash is used under the [529]Anglo-American Cataloguing Rules to
   separate the title of a work from its statement of responsibility
   (i.e., the listing of its author, director, etc.). Like a [530]line
   break, this slash is surrounded by a single space on either side. For
   example:
     * Gone with the Wind / by Margaret Mitchell.
     * Star Trek II. The Wrath of Khan [videorecording] / Paramount
       Pictures.

   The format is used in both [531]card catalogs and online records.

Addresses

   [[532]edit]

   The slash is sometimes used as an abbreviation for building numbers.
   For example, in some contexts,^[[533]where?] 8/A Evergreen Gardens
   specifies Apartment 8 in Building A of the residential complex
   Evergreen Gardens. In the United States, however, such an address
   refers to the first division of Apartment 8 and is simply a variant of
   Apartment 8A or 8-A. Similarly in the United Kingdom, an address such
   as 12/2 Anywhere Road means flat (or apartment) 2 in the building
   numbered 12 on Anywhere Road.

   The slash is also used in the United States in the postal abbreviation
   for "care of." For example, Judy Smith c/o Bob Smith could be used when
   Bob Smith is receiving mail on Judy's behalf. Typically, this would be
   used in a situation where someone is either out of town, in an
   institution or hotel, or temporarily staying at another's address.

   In Spanish address writings, "c/" is used as the abbreviation of
   "calle" (or "carrer" in Catalan) meaning "street".

Music

   [[534]edit]

   Slashes are used in [535]musical notation as an alternative to writing
   out specific [536]notes where it is easier to read than traditional
   notation or where the player can [537]improvise. They are commonly used
   to indicate [538]chords either in place of or in combination with
   traditional notation and for [539]drummers as an indication to continue
   with the previously indicated style.

Sports

   [[540]edit]

   A slash is used to mark a [541]spare (knocking down all ten pins in two
   throws) when scoring [542]ten-pin and [543]duckpin bowling.^[544][41]

Text messaging

   [[545]edit]

   In online messaging, a slash might be used to imitate the formatting of
   a chat command (e.g., writing "/fliptable" as though there were such a
   command) or the closing tags of languages such as HTML (e.g., writing
   "/endrant" to end a diatribe or "/s" to mark the preceding text as
   [546]sarcastic). A pair of slashes is sometimes used as a way to mark
   [547]italic text, where no special formatting is available (e.g.,
   /italics/).^[[548]citation needed]

Before an e-signature

   [[549]edit]

   In legal writing, especially in a pleading, attorneys often sign their
   name with an "s" that is either enclosed by two slashes or followed by
   a single slash and preceding the attorney's name.^[550][42] An example
   would be the following:

     /s/ Bob Smith

     Attorney for Plaintiff

As a letter

   [[551]edit]

   The [552]Iraqw language of Tanzania uses the slash as a letter,
   representing the [553]voiced pharyngeal fricative, as in [554]/ameeni,
   "woman".^[555][43]

Spacing

   [[556]edit]

   There are usually no spaces either before or after a slash. According
   to [557]New Hart's Rules: The Oxford Style Guide, a slash is usually
   written without spacing on either side when it connects single words,
   letters or symbols.^[558][11] Exceptions are in [559]representing the
   start of a new line when quoting verse, or a new paragraph when quoting
   prose. [560]The Chicago Manual of Style also allows spaces when either
   of the separated items is a compound that itself includes a space: "Our
   New Zealand / Western Australia trip".^[561][44] (Compare [562]use of
   an en dash used to separate such compounds.) The Canadian Style: A
   Guide to Writing and Editing prescribes: "No space before or after an
   oblique when used between individual words, letters or symbols; one
   space before and after the oblique when used between longer groups
   which contain internal spacing", giving the examples "n/a" and
   "Language and Society / Langue et société".^[563][45]

   According to The Chicago Manual of Style, when typesetting a URL or
   computer path, line breaks should occur before a slash but not in the
   text between two slashes.^[564][46]

     * U+29F8 ⧸ BIG SOLIDUS
     * U+FF0F ／ FULLWIDTH SOLIDUS ([580]fullwidth version of solidus)
     * U+1F67C 🙼 VERY HEAVY SOLIDUS

   In XML and HTML, the slash can also be represented with the
   [581]character entity &sol;  or the [582]numeric character reference
   &#47;  or &#x2F; .^[583][48]

Alternative names

   [[584]edit]

   Name Used for
   diagonal An uncommon name for the slash in all its uses,^[585][3]
   division slash This is the Unicode Consortium's formal name for the
   variant of the slash used to mark [586]division.^[587][49] (U+2215
   ∕ DIVISION SLASH)
   forward slash A [588]retronym used to distinguish slash from a
   backslash following the popularization of MS-DOS and other Microsoft
   operating systems, which use the backslash for paths in its file
   system.^[589][9]^[590][10] Less often forward stroke (UK), foreslash,
   front slash, and frontslash. It is not unknown even to see such
   [591]back-formations as reverse backslash.^[592][50]
   fraction slash
   This is the Unicode Consortium's formal name for the low slash used to
   mark fractions.^[593][49] (U+2044 ⁄ FRACTION SLASH)
   Also sometimes known as the [594]fraction bar, although this more
   commonly refers to the horizontal bar style, as in

   ⁠1/2⁠. When used as a fraction bar, this form of the mark is less
   vertical than an ASCII slash, generally close to 45° and [595]kerned on
   both sides;^[596][51] this use is distinguished by Unicode as the
   fraction slash.^[597][49] (This use is sometimes mistakenly described
   as the sole meaning of "solidus", with its use as a shilling mark and
   slash distinguished under the name "virgule".^[598][51]^[599][52])
   oblique
   A formerly common name for the slash in all its uses.^[600][3] Also
   oblique stroke,^[601][53]^[602][54] oblique dash, etc.
   scratch comma A modern name for the virgule's historic use as a form of
   comma.^[603][55]
   separatrix Originally, the [604]vertical line separating integers from
   decimals before the advent of the [605]decimal point; later used for
   the vertical bar or slash used in [606]proofreader's marginalia to
   denote the intended replacement for a letter or word [607]struckthrough
   in proofed text^[608][56] or to separate margin notes.^[609][57]
   Sometimes misapplied to virgules.
   shilling mark
   A development of the [610]long S ſ used as an abbreviation for the
   (obsolete) [611]British shilling ([612]Latin: [613]solidus),^[614][5]
   and also for some modern-day currencies (Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda and
   Somalia), where it sometimes takes the place of a decimal point. The
   'slash' is known as a "shilling stroke".^[615][21]
   slant
   From its shape, an infrequent name except (as slants) in its use to
   mark pronunciations off from other text^[616][58] and as the original
   [617]ASCII name of the character.^[618][47] Also slant line(s) or
   bar(s).^[619][9]
   slash mark An alternative name used to distinguish the punctuation mark
   from the word's other senses.^[620][59]
   slat An uncommon name for the slash used by the [621]esoteric
   programming language [622]INTERCAL.^[623][54] Also slak.^[624][54]
   solidus Another name for the mark (derived from the Latin form of
   'shilling'), also applied to other slashes separating numbers or
   letters,^[625][6] used in typography,^[626][51] and adopted by the
   [627]ISO and [628]Unicode^[629][49]^[630][60] as their formal name for
   the ASCII slash ("slant"). (U+002F
   / SOLIDUS)

   The solidus's use as a division sign is distinguished as the division
   slash.^[631][49]
   stroke A contraction of the phrase [632]oblique stroke, used in
   [633]telegraphy.^[634][53] It is particularly employed in reading the
   mark out loud: "he stroke she" is a common British reading of "he/she".
   "Slash" has, however, become common in Britain in computing contexts,
   while some North American [635]amateur radio enthusiasts employ the
   British "stroke". Less frequently, "stroke" is also used to refer to
   hyphens.^[636][9]
   virgule
   A development of virgula ("twig"),^[637][1] the original medieval Latin
   name of the character when it was used as a scratch comma and caesura
   mark.^[638][1] Now primarily used as the name of the slash when it is
   used to mark line breaks in quotations.^[[639]citation needed]
   Sometimes mistakenly distinguished as a formal name for the slash, as
   against the solidus's supposed use as a fraction
   slash.^[640][51]^[641][52] Formerly sometimes [642]anglicized in
   British sources as the virgil.^[643][2]

   The slash may also be read out as and, or, and/or, to, or cum in some
   compounds separated by a slash; over or out of in fractions, division,
   and [644]numbering; and per or a(n) in derived units (as km/h) and
   prices (as $~/kg), where the division slash stands for
   "each".^[645][9]^[646][61]
